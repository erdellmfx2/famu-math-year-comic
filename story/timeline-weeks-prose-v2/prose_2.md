# Week Two: The Shape of an Invitation

By Friday afternoon, Nia Reynolds had learned that the campus radio booth was much smaller than it sounded.

On air, Julian Cross's voice seemed to occupy its own weather system: warm, smooth, and confident enough to convince listeners that he had never once walked into the wrong classroom. In person, the booth on the second floor of Hart Student Union was a narrow room with two microphones, three chairs, a control board covered in glowing buttons, and a handwritten sign that said **DO NOT SPIN IN THE GOOD CHAIR**.

Julian was spinning in the good chair.

"You came," he said when Nia appeared behind the glass.

"You invited me."

"People say yes to things all the time. Showing up is the impressive part."

She liked that answer. She also suspected he knew she would.

The radio showcase gathered new students around the Media House and union booths for short demonstrations. Julian's segment was called *First Week Frequencies*, a mix of music, interviews, and confessions collected from students who were excited, lost, homesick, overdressed, underprepared, or some combination of all five.

Nia stood at the back while Julian leaned toward the microphone.

"Today's public-service announcement," he said, "is for every first-year who looked at a campus map, calculated the shortest distance between two buildings, and still arrived late because McCall-Hart added a staircase as a character-building exercise."

Nia laughed.

Then Julian continued. "In mathematical terms, the domain is the campus, the range is wherever you accidentally end up, and the function is confusion."

Nia stopped laughing.

When the music came up, she tapped on the glass.

Julian opened the door. "Good confusion joke?"

"That is not how domain and range work."

He put a hand over his heart. "I have wounded mathematics."

"Publicly."

"Can it be saved?"

Nia stepped into the booth. "Move over."

## Friday, August 8: Fact-Checking the Flirt

During the next break, Nia rewrote Julian's joke on the back of a station schedule.

"The domain could be all the starting locations students choose," she explained. "The function maps each starting point to the place they reach after following your directions. The range is the set of places they actually end up."

Julian stared at the paper. "That is both more correct and less funny."

"Accuracy has a price."

"What if the function maps every student to the wrong building?"

"Then your directions are consistent, which is somehow worse."

He grinned. "There it is. That's the joke."

When the microphone went live again, Julian introduced her as "Nia Reynolds, first-year mathematical sciences major and defender of functions everywhere."

She corrected the explanation in twenty seconds, added the line about consistently bad directions, and earned a burst of laughter from the students outside the booth.

Julian watched her with open admiration.

Afterward, he walked her to the terrace. "You make ideas sound like stories."

"Ideas are stories. They have relationships, rules, and consequences."

"I might need you to fact-check the whole show."

"That sounds suspiciously like unpaid labor."

"Then I will pay in coffee."

"Copper Cup coffee?"

"I respect professionals."

Nia tried not to smile too widely and failed.

## Saturday, August 9: The Test Before the Team

While Nia replayed the radio clip more times than she planned to admit, Malik sat in Porter Center for Enterprise learning that signing an interest sheet did not make him a member of Heron Analytics.

Dr. Isaac Bennett stood beside a screen displaying a football field overlaid with numbers. He was a statistics professor, actuarial adviser, and the kind of speaker who could turn a room quiet without raising his voice.

"Sports analytics is not decorating opinions with decimals," he told the group. "A model has to help someone make a decision under uncertainty. If your answer only works after the game is over, you have built a very expensive replay."

Malik sat straighter.

The competitive team studied questions such as fourth-down choices, fatigue, roster use, and attendance. Experienced students would form the core squad, but first-years could earn developmental positions through a screening test and a short explanation exercise.

Then Simone Hart took the front of the room.

She was a second-year data science and finance major with a copper clip holding back her braids and a look that suggested she had already found three flaws in everyone's assumptions.

"The screening is not a trivia contest," she said. "We need people who can calculate, interpret, and revise. If you love your first answer more than you love the evidence, this team will be uncomfortable for you."

Malik wrote that down, then immediately wondered whether writing it down was evidence that it applied to him.

After the session, he studied the sample problem taped near the exit. Simone stopped beside him.

"You have been looking at that table for four minutes," she said.

"Three and a half."

"That correction tells me more than you intended."

"The average gain is hiding the distribution."

"Good. What else?"

"Small sample. Different opponents. Unknown field position."

Simone nodded once. "Screening is the Wednesday after move-in. Bring that suspicion with you. Leave room for other people to be right."

Malik watched her go. He was not sure whether he had been encouraged or warned.

## Sunday, August 10: Who Counts

The Bridge class returned to domain and range on Sunday with a scenario about a tutoring center that assigned one appointment time to each student request.

Malik defined the domain as submitted requests and the range as available appointment slots. His answer was fast, clean, and incomplete.

Nia circled the phrase *submitted requests*.

"What about students who need tutoring but cannot use the request form?"

"They are not in the domain."

"That is my point."

"It is the domain described by the problem."

"And if we report that the center served ninety percent of its domain, it sounds excellent. But maybe the form excludes students with unreliable internet, screen-reader problems, or schedules outside the listed times."

Malik rubbed his forehead. "You keep adding people the problem does not mention."

"Real life keeps doing that too."

The sentence landed harder than she meant it to.

Malik closed his notebook. "A model has boundaries. That does not make the model cruel."

"I didn't call it cruel."

"You looked at me like I was removing people from campus."

Nia crossed her arms. "And you looked at me like context was an annoying hobby."

For the rest of the exercise, their table became painfully quiet.

Dr. Brooks eventually stopped beside them. She looked at Malik's formal definition and Nia's notes in the margin.

"Both of you are protecting something useful," she said. "Malik is protecting a precise claim. Nia is protecting the difference between the observed population and the population we care about. Can your answer do both?"

Neither spoke immediately.

Then Malik reopened his notebook. "We can define the given domain and add a limitation about unobserved demand."

Nia uncrossed her arms. "And recommend more ways to request help."

"As a next step, not part of the current function."

"Fine."

They revised the answer.

Outside class, Nia bumped his shoulder. "For the record, I do not think you are cruel."

"For the record, I do not think context is a hobby."

"You think it is an aggressive side quest."

"Sometimes."

That was close enough to peace.

## Monday, August 11: Saying Yes Too Fast

Julian found Nia outside Jubilee Library on Monday with a saxophone case on his back and two cold drinks in his hands.

"One is coffee," he said. "One is a science experiment pretending to be tea. Choose wisely."

She chose the tea.

"Marching Herons rehearse tonight," he said. "You should come."

"Tonight?"

"That is generally when tonight happens."

Nia checked neither her planner nor the Bridge assignment waiting in her bag.

"Yes," she said.

Julian's smile made the decision feel excellent for almost six minutes.

Then her phone reminded her about a residence-life meeting. Another notification announced a Math Circle interest session. Malik had also sent the time for their accountability check-in.

She stared at the screen.

"Problem?" Julian asked.

"No. Just a very popular evening."

"You can say no."

"I know."

But Nia had spent years believing that good opportunities were doors that might lock if she did not sprint through them. By dinner she had moved the residence meeting, skipped Math Circle, and asked Malik to push their check-in later.

His reply came quickly: **Okay. Send a time you can actually keep.**

The message was reasonable. Somehow, it irritated her.

She typed three different responses before sending: **9:30. I will keep it.**

## Tuesday, August 12: Absolutely Not About Nia

Malik met his future roommate, Darius "DJ" Cole, during a residence-hall orientation on Tuesday.

DJ wore a McCall-Hart football training shirt, carried no pen, and borrowed Malik's before the first slide ended.

"You look like a man who travels with backup ink," DJ whispered.

Malik handed him a second pen.

"I knew it. We are going to thrive."

They discovered they had been assigned the same room. DJ immediately proposed putting a television on the largest wall. Malik immediately explained that the largest wall offered the best placement for two desks.

"We have not moved in and you are already taking square footage from joy," DJ said.

"A desk supports multiple activities."

"Not Madden."

After orientation, they ate at the union. Nia passed the table, waved, and hurried toward the Media House.

DJ watched Malik watch her.

"Who is that?"

"My accountability partner."

"That phrase has never sounded less academic."

"We met in Bridge. We work well together."

"You like her."

"I am interested in the Heron Analytics Challenge."

DJ blinked. "I asked about a person."

"And I answered with the thing I am interested in."

"That is the verbal version of leaving through a bathroom window."

Malik took back the spare pen. "I need this."

"Truth tax?"

"You have your own pen now."

## Wednesday, August 13: Under the Sound

Marching-band rehearsal did not resemble the loose jam session Nia had imagined.

It was repetition under pressure. Musicians reset a section again and again while student leaders corrected timing, spacing, posture, and breath. The August air stayed thick even after sunset. Julian's shirt darkened with sweat, but each time the altos returned to the phrase, his sound came out clear.

Nia sat high in the empty side of Henry Boyd Stadium, watching discipline transform into spectacle one measured piece at a time.

When rehearsal ended, Julian climbed the steps and dropped beside her.

"Still think I just wander around being charming?" he asked between breaths.

"I did not say *just*."

He laughed. "Fair."

They shared fries from a paper tray while the stadium lights clicked off section by section.

Julian told her that media arts had given a name to something he had always done: notice what changed when a story was framed one way instead of another. Campus radio let him ask questions. Documentary work let him keep the answers.

"And band?" Nia asked.

He looked toward the field. "Band is where nobody cares how naturally talented you think you are. Either you hit the mark with everybody else or the whole shape bends."

That answer made her see him differently. Beneath the easy smile was someone who practiced until the public moment looked effortless.

"Why math?" he asked.

Nia told him about her aunt's tutoring program and the reports that determined whether it received support. She told him she wanted to make evidence understandable without stripping away the people inside it.

"So you want numbers to testify," Julian said.

"Exactly."

He tapped his fry against hers like a toast. "I knew you were good radio."

When he walked her back toward the residence hall, their shoulders touched twice. Neither apologized.

## Thursday, August 14: Range and Limits

Nia met Malik at North Star Learning Commons the next morning, nine minutes late and still smiling about the night before.

"It felt like a date," she said before sitting down.

Malik's pencil stopped.

He could have asked whether Julian had called it a date. He could have pointed out that walking someone home and sharing fries did not constitute formal evidence. He could have done several things that would have made Nia's happiness smaller.

Instead he said, "Did you have a good time?"

"A really good time. He is different when he is working. Focused. Not fake-focused for two minutes, either."

"Then it sounds like you learned something useful."

She watched him. "You okay?"

"Yes."

It was not exactly a lie. He was healthy. Seated. Fully conscious.

Nia opened her notebook. "We can talk about something else."

"We should talk about your schedule. You moved three commitments for one invitation."

"I handled it."

"You skipped the interest session you said mattered to you."

Her smile faded. "I know what I skipped."

Malik heard the edge in her voice and stopped. This was not the moment to turn disappointment into authority.

"You're right," he said. "It is your schedule."

They completed their check-in, but the conversation never fully recovered.

That evening, Malik stayed at North Star after the tables emptied. The analytics screening test was less than a week away. He worked through probability questions, interpreting every mistake as evidence that somebody else wanted the position more.

Across campus, Nia sat on her bed and added Julian's rehearsal to her planner in copper ink. Then she moved her missed Math Circle session to a page titled **Find Another Way In**.

Two invitations had taken shape that week. One opened toward romance; the other toward competition. Both looked exciting from the outside. Neither had yet revealed what it would cost.
