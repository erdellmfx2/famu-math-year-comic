# Week Thirty-Four: Radius of Influence

Nia replaced one expensive calculation with a power-series approximation and watched the model run faster.

The improvement was dramatic. A route update that had taken several seconds now appeared almost instantly.

"We fixed it," she said.

Keisha did not celebrate. "Where does the approximation converge?"

Nia looked at the expression again.

They had expanded the function around a typical congestion value. Near that center, the first few terms approximated it well. Farther away, the error grew. At extreme congestion levels, the shortcut could produce a route cost less reliable than the slow calculation it replaced.

"We accelerated the model inside one neighborhood of values," Nia said.

"Then label the neighborhood."

On the board, Nia wrote the center, radius of convergence, and error bound. Speed was useful only where the approximation deserved trust.

## Friday, March 20: Valid Here, Not Everywhere

Spring break ended with students returning to Dr. Delgado's classroom in various stages of denial.

He introduced power series as functions represented by infinite polynomial expressions. Around a chosen center, a complicated function could become easier to calculate term by term. But the representation did not necessarily work for every input.

"A formula can be excellent inside its interval and disastrous outside it," he said. "State the domain of your confidence."

Nia tested her approximation across possible congestion values. The first few terms matched the full function closely near ordinary conditions. As traffic approached the extreme scenarios, the difference widened.

She added a safeguard: outside the validated interval, the software would revert to the slower calculation rather than continue producing quick uncertainty disguised as precision.

Malik reviewed the logic before the Delta trip.

"You could also display the expected approximation error," he said.

"Keisha already demanded it."

"Naturally."

They spoke easily about the model, though both knew the last weeks had made ease complicated. Nia did not ask about Simone. Malik did not mention the spring-break image.

Professional limits were still limits.

That evening, the five students packed for Delta National Laboratory: Nia and Keisha for the symposium project, Julian for communication design, Simone for data-governance sessions, and Malik for risk analysis.

They were traveling together because their skills intersected.

Nothing about the trip promised that their feelings would converge as neatly.

## Saturday, March 21: Behind the Glass

Delta National Laboratory's supercomputing floor lived behind thick glass and a security door that opened only after orientation, identification checks, and a lecture about devices nobody was allowed to connect.

Rows of dark cabinets stretched beneath white lights. Cooling systems filled the room with a low mechanical roar. A scientist explained that the computers could run thousands of storm scenarios, but larger capacity did not remove uncertainty. It allowed teams to examine more versions of what might happen.

"Computation multiplies assumptions," she said. "It does not purify them."

Keisha wrote that down immediately.

During an uncertainty-communication talk, researchers compared three maps built from the same forecast. One used a single sharp flood boundary. One displayed several possible boundaries without explanation. The third connected probability ranges to specific planning decisions.

Most viewers preferred the first because it looked decisive.

Emergency planners found the third more useful.

"The clearest image is not always the most truthful," Julian whispered.

"Please frame that above your editing station," Nia said.

The speaker demonstrated how color, animation speed, and label placement could make low-confidence outcomes look inevitable. Julian asked about testing visuals with residents rather than technical teams. Simone asked who approved communication standards when agencies disagreed.

Nia watched each person enter the discussion from a different direction.

The project belonged in this kind of room, not because it was finished, but because its weaknesses required more than one discipline to see.

## Sunday, March 22: Pricing the Rare Event

Malik attended a session on extreme-event risk.

An actuarial researcher described the difficulty of estimating events that were both rare and enormously costly. Historical records were limited. Climate conditions changed. Property data favored documented financial loss over losses that insurance never recorded.

Malik raised his hand.

"How do agencies price a rare risk without presenting the estimate as more certain than it is?"

The researcher distinguished between expected loss, tail risk, scenario ranges, and decisions made under uncertainty. A premium or reserve was not proof that analysts knew the future. It was a policy choice informed by models, available evidence, and tolerance for failure.

"The ethical problem begins," she said, "when the number's precision is allowed to hide the judgment inside it."

Malik thought of his family's insurance claim after the Gulf storm. Every damaged item had required documentation. Emotional loss, unpaid recovery time, and rooms his parents could not safely use had entered the calculation unevenly, if at all.

After the session, Nia asked what he had learned.

He explained the difference between a rare-event estimate and the decision built from it. She connected it to route instability near uncertain roads.

Simone joined them and expanded the question to financial institutions deciding which communities received resilience investment.

For several minutes, the conversation was balanced among three people.

Then a scientist approached with a question that narrowed the room.

## Monday, March 23: The Old Ease

The scientist had reviewed Nia's poster abstract.

"If your warning updates and route calculations change at the same time," she asked, "how do you distinguish instability caused by communication from instability caused by road capacity?"

Nia began with the scenario matrix. Malik entered at the exact point where risk decomposition mattered.

"Hold one uncertainty source fixed," he said, "then vary the other across its plausible range. After separate sensitivity runs, test interactions."

"But holding communication fixed is not realistic during an actual event," Nia added.

"It is not a forecast. It isolates influence. The combined scenario comes after."

"And the display must show which run the audience is seeing."

"Otherwise interaction looks like one unexplained risk score."

They passed the answer back and forth without interrupting. Nia drew the scenario grid. Malik named the assumptions. Each anticipated the other's missing step.

The scientist nodded. "That design could work. Document the artificial separation so no one mistakes it for reality."

Only after she left did Nia realize Simone had been watching.

The exchange had felt like Summer Bridge: two students discovering that the other person's mind moved differently but arrived at the same problem. The ease was real. So was the history attached to it.

Malik returned to Simone and asked about her afternoon session.

He was present.

The fact that he could be present afterward did not erase where part of him had just gone.

## Tuesday, March 24: The Part Not Available

Simone did not blame Nia.

She waited until she and Malik were walking from the guest residence to breakfast.

"Yesterday, when you answered that scientist with Nia, you looked relieved," she said.

"Relieved?"

"Like you had returned to a conversation that never required translation."

Malik searched for a less dangerous description. "We know how each other thinks about these problems."

"I know. I watched it."

Simone stopped near a courtyard bench. "Has part of you remained unavailable all spring?"

He could have answered with actions. He had chosen Simone. He kept dates, supported London, attended her panel, and told the truth when it was difficult. All of that mattered.

It did not fully answer her.

"I thought choosing you meant the old feelings would become irrelevant," he said.

"That is not a no."

"No."

Simone absorbed the word without turning toward Nia's building or asking what Nia had done. "Then London is not the only uncertainty between us."

"No."

"I need you to stop acting as if geography created a problem that was already here."

Malik nodded.

The walk to breakfast continued. Neither reached for the other's hand.

## Wednesday, March 25: Strong Splits and One-Run Games

Keisha opened outdoor track season with her strongest relay split of the year.

She received the baton in third, closed the gap on the backstretch, and handed off nearly even. Her team finished second, but the coaches replayed her split on a tablet and showed how her curve mechanics had improved.

"No medal for one leg," Keisha told Nia afterward.

"Still your work."

"Yes. Both things."

Imani's softball team played two road games decided by one run. She did not produce a dramatic highlight. She moved a runner with a sacrifice bunt, made a difficult backhand stop, and called a defensive shift that prevented a tying run.

McCall-Hart swept the series through accumulated decisions that would look small in a summary.

The friends followed both events through updates because the Delta trip and travel schedules prevented everyone from attending. No one confused distance with lack of support.

Malik saw the relay video while sitting beside Simone on the return bus. They spoke politely about sports, the lab sessions, and work due Friday.

Politeness had become a boundary around the conversation they could no longer postpone.

## Thursday, March 26: Honest Is Not Harmless

Simone asked Malik one final question after they returned to campus.

"What does your connection with Nia mean?"

They sat in an empty Porter Center classroom. The projector fan hummed above them.

Malik could name what it had meant: friendship, competition, attraction, waiting, resentment, shared academic ambition. He could not name what it meant now without reaching beyond what Nia had said or what he had chosen.

"I do not know," he answered.

It was honest.

Simone's face tightened. "And you have been dating me while not knowing."

"I know what you mean to me."

"Do you? Or do you know that I am the person you chose when you decided waiting for Nia was unhealthy?"

"That is not all this is."

"I believe you. That is why this hurts."

Simone did not accuse him of using her. Their relationship had contained real affection, attention, humor, and growth. It had also contained a room he kept comparing with another room he had never entered.

"I need time," she said.

Malik did not ask how much. He had turned enough feelings into schedules.

She left the classroom alone.

His honesty had ended one kind of hiding. It had also given Simone information she could use to make a choice he might not like.

Truth did not promise mercy. It promised that the person affected could finally decide with the real evidence.
