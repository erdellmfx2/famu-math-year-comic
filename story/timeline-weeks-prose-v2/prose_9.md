# Week Nine: What Water Takes

The house smelled wrong.

Malik noticed it before he saw the waterline. The air held mud, wet wood, spoiled food, cleaning chemicals, and the sour beginning of mold. It did not smell like the lemon oil his mother used on Saturdays or the pepper and onions his father cooked into nearly every breakfast. It did not smell like home.

A brown line crossed the walls of the lower rooms, marking how high the water had risen. Cabinet doors hung open. The living-room rug had been dragged into the yard, where it lay folded over itself like something exhausted. Marcel's tools rested on towels across Aunt Renee's garage, separated into piles labeled **DRY**, **CLEAN**, and **MAYBE**.

Malik stood in the doorway with his backpack still on.

His mother came from the hall wearing rubber boots and work gloves.

"Baby."

She hugged him carefully, though she was not the one injured.

Behind her, Marcel sat in a dining chair with his left leg braced and elevated on a plastic storage bin. A bandage covered the cut along his shin. He lifted one hand.

"You came all this way to inspect my bad decisions?"

Malik dropped his backpack. "I can start with the knee."

His father smiled, but pain tightened the corners of his mouth.

Nothing in Malik's forecasts had prepared him for that expression.

## Friday, September 26: The Inventory

Insurance required an inventory.

Malik built one at the dining table while relatives and neighbors moved through the house in boots and masks. He listed the couch, two bookcases, kitchen cabinets, small appliances, Marcel's damaged equipment, and boxes of documents stored too close to the floor.

Each line needed a description, estimated age, estimated value, and evidence if available.

The work should have suited him.

Instead, every item resisted becoming a number.

The couch was where Micah slept during movie nights. One bookcase had belonged to Malik's grandmother. A ruined plastic bin held old school records, childhood drawings, honor-roll certificates, and the program from Malik's high school graduation.

Celeste lifted damp papers one by one and placed them between towels.

"Take pictures before we throw anything away," Malik said.

"I know."

"The policy may require--"

"Malik." His mother's voice was tired, not angry. "I know."

He stopped.

Across the room, Marcel tried to stand without help. His injured leg buckled, and he caught the chair back.

Malik moved toward him.

"I have it," Marcel said.

"You almost fell."

"Almost is not fell."

"That is not how risk works."

His father gave a humorless laugh. "Do not bring college words into my kitchen."

The kitchen floor squished beneath his crutch.

Malik looked down.

Marcel did too. Some of the fight left his face.

"Help me to the porch," he said.

It was the first task Malik completed all day that felt entirely useful.

## Saturday, September 27: The Rate of Rising Water

Dr. Brooks had sent a make-up packet with flexible dates and a note: **Complete only what you can document time for. Family recovery comes first. Communication keeps options open.**

One assignment covered related rates.

Water poured into a cylindrical tank. Its depth increased at a known rate. Students had to determine how quickly the volume changed.

Malik stared at the problem from Aunt Renee's kitchen table.

He wanted to close the laptop.

Outside, waterlogged furniture lined the curbs. Inside, a textbook had invented polite water that entered a perfect container at a steady rate and damaged nothing.

Micah climbed into the chair beside him. The twelve-year-old had been quieter than usual since the storm. He wore somebody else's slides because his sneakers were still inside a ruined closet.

"Is that flood math?" he asked.

"Tank math."

"Same water."

"Different problem."

Micah looked toward the room where Celeste was speaking with the insurance company. "How fast did the water come in our house?"

Malik began to say they did not have enough information. Then he remembered his mother's texts.

At 5:36, water was at the steps. At 6:02, it reached the utility room. At 6:19, the power failed after water moved farther inside. They had approximate depths from marks on the walls and videos from a neighbor.

Malik drew the house footprint on paper.

"If we estimate how much floor area the water covered," he explained, "and how the depth changed over time, we can estimate how quickly the amount of water inside was growing."

"Can we find when it got to my game?"

The console had been on a low shelf.

Malik swallowed. "Probably within a range. Not an exact minute."

Together they marked times and estimated depths. The numbers suggested the worst increase came after water crossed the threshold, when a small rise outside spread across a large indoor area.

"So it got deep fast," Micah said.

"Yes."

"Could we have moved more stuff?"

Malik heard the real question.

"Maybe a few things," he said. "But nobody should have stayed inside trying to beat the water. Things can be replaced. You cannot."

Micah traced the line representing the wall. "Even the game?"

"Even the game."

The textbook problem still felt cruel. But the mathematics gave Micah a way to ask what had happened, and Malik a way to answer without pretending the loss had been preventable.

## Sunday, September 28: Divided Work

Simone called Sunday afternoon.

Malik stepped onto Aunt Renee's porch, where an extension cord ran from a neighbor's generator and three pairs of cleaned work gloves dried on the rail.

"How is your father?" she asked.

"The knee is badly sprained. The cut needed stitches. He has an orthopedic appointment."

"And the house?"

Malik looked across the street at debris piled along the curb. "Not livable yet."

Simone let the answer stand.

"I divided your analytics section," she said. "Andre has formation tagging. Priya has game-state splits. I am handling the uncertainty notes."

Malik's grip tightened on the phone. "I can work tonight."

"No."

"The deadline did not change because of me."

"Correct. That is why the team adjusted."

"I should at least review the final version."

"When you return, if there is time."

He paced to the end of the porch. "It was my section."

"It was the team's section assigned to you."

The distinction irritated him because it was true.

Simone's voice softened. "Nobody is punishing you. Nobody thinks you disappeared because you are unreliable. We are doing what a team is supposed to do when one person's life catches fire. Or floods. You know what I mean."

Malik looked through the screen door. His father struggled to adjust the brace without asking for help.

"Thank you," he said.

After the call, he opened the shared folder anyway. Andre had already improved the tagging notes. Priya had found a useful game-state split Malik had missed.

Relief and jealousy arrived together.

The work could continue without him.

That was the point of a team. It was also, at that moment, the part he found hardest to accept.

## Monday, September 29: Official

At McCall-Hart, Founders' Cup week opened with a short ceremony near Founders' Bell. Alumni representatives spoke about the university's history, student leaders announced service projects, and the Marching Herons formed an indigo-and-copper line along Cypress Walk.

Nia stood with Keisha near the limestone arch. She had checked her phone twice for an update from Malik and stopped herself from checking a third time.

Julian found her after the ceremony.

"Walk with me?"

They moved away from the crowd, though not far enough to escape the bell's shadow.

Julian had been thoughtful all week: sharing verified resources on air, helping publicize the supply drive, and asking about Malik without behaving as if concern made him part of a competition.

"I have a question," he said.

"Math or media?"

"Relationship administration."

"Very serious department."

Julian smiled, then became nervous enough that Nia stopped joking.

"We have been seeing each other," he said. "I would like to call you my girlfriend. I would like to be your boyfriend. Not because a label makes everything different, but because I do not want to act casual about something that is not casual to me."

Nia looked at him beneath the old bell, surrounded by students beginning traditions they did not yet fully understand.

"Yes," she said. "I want that too."

Julian exhaled. "Good. I had a whole second speech if you asked for time."

"Was it better?"

"Much worse."

She laughed and kissed him.

Later, Nia sent Malik a photo of the supply shipment leaving campus, not a relationship announcement. It did not feel like the moment to add another emotion to his week.

She told herself she would explain when he came back.

## Tuesday, September 30: Three Catches

The Founders' Cup game gave DJ his first real opportunity on offense.

An upperclassman receiver left in the second quarter with a shoulder injury. DJ heard his name and ran into the huddle before anybody could reconsider.

His first target came on third-and-seven. The pass arrived high and behind him. DJ twisted, caught it against his shoulder, and took a hit before securing the ball.

First down.

The second catch required him to drag both feet near the sideline. The third came across the middle with a safety closing fast.

Three difficult catches. Thirty-eight yards. No touchdown, no viral miracle, but enough reliable work to keep him on the field during the fourth-quarter drive that sealed McCall-Hart's win.

Nia, Keisha, and Imani celebrated in the stands. Julian played with the band, turning toward Nia's section after every scoring drive. Malik watched the game on Aunt Renee's television with Micah asleep on the couch and Marcel's injured leg elevated beside him.

When DJ made the sideline catch, Marcel pointed at the screen.

"That is your roommate?"

"Yes."

"Boy has hands."

Malik texted DJ: **Three catches. One missed blocking assignment in Q3. Good work.**

DJ replied after the game: **I LOVE YOU TOO, DATA MAN.**

Malik smiled at the phone.

For the first time since arriving home, campus felt connected to him rather than impossibly far away.

## Wednesday, October 1: Emergency Aid

Malik returned to Bellwether Wednesday morning carrying photographs, medical paperwork, repair estimates, and a new understanding of how quickly a family could become a file.

His emergency financial-aid appointment took place at Porter Center. The counselor reviewed temporary assistance, documentation requirements, meal support, and a possible adjustment while his father's income was interrupted.

"Some funds are limited," she said. "I do not want to promise an amount before review."

Malik appreciated the honest uncertainty and hated needing it.

He submitted copies of the urgent-care bill and storm-damage report. He asked about campus employment.

"There may be evening hours at North Star Learning Commons," the counselor said. "Desk support, equipment checkout, and closing tasks. It will compete with study time."

"I can manage it."

The answer came automatically.

That afternoon, Malik completed the job application. He listed his Bridge experience, technical skills, and availability after classes. He did not list analytics meetings until the form forced him to.

At the bottom, a question asked why he wanted the position.

He typed: **To support student access to academic resources while developing professional experience.**

Then he deleted it.

He wrote: **My family experienced storm damage and a temporary loss of income. I need evening work that can fit around my courses.**

The second answer felt exposed.

He submitted it anyway.

## Thursday, October 2: What He Does Not Hand Her

Malik saw Nia and Julian outside Hart Student Union.

They were holding hands. Not accidentally, not tentatively. Julian said something that made Nia lean into his shoulder, and the happiness on her face was familiar enough to hurt.

Nia noticed Malik first.

Her smile changed. "You're back."

She released Julian's hand and stepped forward, then seemed unsure whether to hug him.

Malik solved the uncertainty by hugging her.

"How is your family?" she asked.

"Safe. My father has more appointments. The house needs a lot of work."

"I am sorry."

"The supply shipment arrived. My mother saw the McCall-Hart labels. She told me to thank everybody."

Julian offered his hand. Malik shook it.

"The station is still sharing recovery resources," Julian said. "If your family has a verified fundraiser or community drive, send it to me. No promises, but I can take it through review."

"Thank you."

The three of them stood in a small triangle of things not yet said.

Nia drew a breath. "Julian and I made it official Monday. I wanted to tell you myself, but you were dealing with--"

"Congratulations," Malik said.

This time, the word cost more. It was also true.

"Are you okay?" she asked.

No. His father's work had stopped. The insurance claim had barely begun. He had make-up calculus assignments, a sixty-four, a job application, and analytics work now improved by other people. He had returned to find the person he wanted holding hands with someone who had shown up responsibly during a crisis.

Malik could have placed all of that in Nia's arms and waited to see what she did with it.

"I am glad you told me," he said instead. "I need to meet Dr. Brooks before class. We can talk later."

Nia studied him. "Do not disappear."

"I will not."

He walked toward Eliza Moss Hall.

The promise was harder than it sounded. Malik's instinct was to hide until the grade improved, the job was secured, the house was repaired, and wanting Nia no longer made him feel disloyal to a friendship he valued.

But water had already shown him what waiting could take.

This time, he entered Dr. Brooks's office with the bad news still visible.
