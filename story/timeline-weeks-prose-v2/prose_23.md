# Week Twenty-Three: The First Return

DJ had placed the Heritage Bowl trophy beside an unfinished economics assignment.

Malik discovered both when he returned to the residence hall Friday afternoon. The trophy occupied the center of their shared shelf beneath a battery-powered spotlight DJ had apparently purchased for this purpose. Beside it, a laptop displayed half a paragraph about labor markets.

"One of these is evidence of completed work," Malik said.

DJ emerged from beneath his bed holding a missing shoe. "One is inspiration for future work."

"Your paper is due Monday."

"The trophy believes in me."

The room had been reorganized. DJ's expanded football schedule claimed a new section of wall. Malik's desk remained clear except for mail, his spring books, and a note from Ms. Alvarez confirming North Star shifts.

His old room in Gulfport remained unfinished. This one had become more recognizably home.

Malik unpacked his calculus materials and placed the analytics postmortem in a drawer rather than on top of the new semester.

Then his phone lit up with Simone's message.

**Coffee Sunday? Personal, in case the analyst remains confused.**

Malik replied: **Interpretation confirmed. Yes.**

## Friday, January 2: Reorganized

DJ showed Malik the scholarship update in his student account.

"Official," he said. "My mother checked twice and then made me check while she watched."

"Reasonable audit procedure."

"She also told me the money does not make me too important to complete economics."

"Strong methodology."

They worked side by side for an hour. Malik reviewed algebra and antiderivatives for Calculus II. DJ finished the missing paragraph, then argued that football had given him practical experience with labor specialization.

"You catch passes," Malik said.

"Specialized labor."

"You also block."

"Cross-functional expertise."

The familiar nonsense settled Malik into campus life more effectively than the unpacked boxes.

Before bed, he looked at Simone's coffee on his calendar. He felt hopeful, nervous, and free of the old obligation to compare every possibility with Nia.

That freedom was not complete.

It was real enough to begin.

## Saturday, January 3: The Skeptical Mentor

Research matching took place in Eliza Moss Hall.

Nia and Keisha pitched their evacuation-model idea to Dr. Price and a room of faculty, graduate mentors, and student teams. Nia explained the public question. Keisha explained the computational limits. Their application named missing road capacity as a central problem rather than an inconvenience to hide.

Dr. Price assigned them Alexis Grant, a second-year applied mathematics master's student.

Alexis read the one-page proposal without smiling.

"What decision will this model support?" she asked.

"Route recommendation under flood risk," Nia said.

"For whom?"

"Households and emergency planners."

"Those are different users with different authority. Which one?"

Nia glanced at Keisha.

"Emergency planners first," Keisha said. "Household communication would require another interface and trust assumptions we do not have."

Alexis nodded slightly. "What is your smallest defensible question?"

Nia described how missing road capacity might change which route appeared feasible.

"Better. Do not tell me you are saving Bellwether. Tell me what comparison you can complete by the campus forum."

The skepticism irritated Nia.

It also prevented the giant dream from swallowing the project before it began.

Alexis scheduled their first data audit for Wednesday.

## Sunday, January 4: Personal Coffee

Residence halls officially reopened Sunday, filling campus with rolling suitcases and reunion noise.

Simone met Malik at Copper Cup.

She wore a rust-colored coat over a cream sweater and had selected a table away from the foot traffic.

"No laptops," she said when he arrived.

"I did not bring one."

"Good. I was prepared to confiscate it."

Their coffee began with holiday stories. Simone described her younger brother attempting to build a stock portfolio using only companies whose commercials he liked. Malik described the non-Euclidean pie and showed photographic evidence.

"That crust needs structural analysis," Simone said.

"Nia recommended whipped cream stabilization."

The name entered naturally. Simone did not react. Malik did not turn the conversation toward Nia.

After an hour, Simone rested her elbows on the table.

"I asked you here because I like talking to you," she said. "Not because I want to rebuild analytics."

"I like talking to you too."

"Are you available to find out what that means?"

Malik thought of the deleted reminder, the friendship he still valued, and the distinction between being uncertain and being unavailable.

"Yes," he said.

This yes belonged to the person in front of him.

## Monday, January 5: Too Bright

Malik told Nia about the coffee during their first accountability check-in.

"Simone and I met yesterday," he said. "It was personal. We are going out again."

He did not ask whether Nia approved. He told her because their friendship had room for significant news.

Nia's smile arrived too quickly.

"That is great! Simone is amazing. Smart, direct, accomplished, very good at telling you when you are being ridiculous. Honestly, ideal."

Malik stared at her.

"What?"

"That was a lot of approval."

"I am happy for you."

"Thank you."

The exchange should have ended there. Instead, Nia asked where they went, how long they talked, and whether Simone had called it a date.

"Coffee," Malik said. "Personal coffee. We are having dinner Thursday."

"Dinner. Nice. Great."

Her brightness confused him. It confused her more.

That night, Nia told herself she was protective. Malik had been through a hard fall. Simone was a second-year with large ambitions. Nia only wanted clarity for her friend.

The explanation had too many words.

## Tuesday, January 6: What Can an Integral Measure?

Dr. Victor Delgado opened Calculus II with one question.

"What can an integral measure besides area?"

Hands rose.

"Volume," one student said.

"That is accumulated area. Try again."

"Distance."

"From what? Under which conditions?"

"Work."

"Define it."

Five memorized answers failed under follow-up questions.

Dr. Delgado was precise without being cold. He did not reject the topics; he rejected labels offered without relationships.

Nia described total change accumulated from a rate. Malik explained that integrating velocity gave displacement, while integrating speed gave distance traveled. Amara added units and conditions.

"Better," Dr. Delgado said. "Calculus II is not a catalog of integration tricks. Every method must answer why this structure represents this quantity."

He assigned an accumulated-area review using water inflow and outflow. Nia saw immediate connections to flood modeling. Malik saw repair costs and risk over time.

The rate graph crossed the horizontal axis twice. Positive regions represented water entering a reservoir; negative regions represented water leaving. A student added the absolute values and reported total movement.

Dr. Delgado shook his head. "The question asks for net change. Direction matters."

Nia split the integral at the sign changes, evaluated each signed region, and combined them. Malik checked that rate units multiplied by time produced volume. Amara explained why total water moved would require absolute accumulation instead.

"Same graph," Dr. Delgado said. "Different quantity. Your integral does not know what you meant to ask."

Nia wrote the sentence beside her research notes. A route model could accumulate travel time, vehicle count, or exposure, but the team had to decide which quantity matched the decision. Computation would not rescue a vague objective.

The course began by demanding meaning before technique.

Neither student could accuse that demand of being unfamiliar.

## Wednesday, January 7: The Model Cannot Run

Spring classes began Wednesday, along with add-drop and the research team's first data audit.

Keisha loaded the road network. Nia prepared flood-zone overlays. Alexis watched without helping them avoid the problem.

Road-capacity values were missing for nearly thirty percent of the network.

Not evenly missing. Smaller neighborhood roads had the least documentation, while major roads had the most. Filling every gap with an average would make poorly documented streets resemble well-measured roads.

"The proposed model cannot run as written," Keisha said.

Nia looked at Alexis. "Could we estimate from road width?"

"Do you have width?"

"For some roads."

"Then you have another missing-data pattern."

The team tested a simplified route set. Even there, capacity assumptions changed the recommended path.

Nia felt the proposal collapsing.

Keisha did not. "We asked how missing capacity changes recommendations. The missing data is not blocking the question. It *is* the question."

Alexis finally smiled. "Now decide what experiment can demonstrate that honestly."

They stayed late, replacing a promise of one best route with a comparison of how route choices shifted under plausible capacity scenarios.

## Thursday, January 8: Coffee Becomes Dinner

Malik met Simone after his North Star shift.

They began at Copper Cup again, but the cafe closed before they ran out of conversation. Simone suggested dinner at a small place off Cypress Walk.

Malik checked his schedule. He had left the evening open on purpose.

"Yes," he said.

Across campus, Nia and Keisha worked with Alexis to define the smallest research question their data could survive. Nia's phone showed no new message from Malik. That was normal. He was not required to report a date in progress.

At dinner, Simone asked Malik what he hoped spring would contain.

"Better calculus. Stable work hours. Progress on the house."

"Those are outcomes. What do you want to experience?"

Malik had no prepared answer.

"I do not know yet," he said.

"Good. Something we cannot turn into a deliverable."

They shared dessert and talked until the server began resetting nearby tables.

At Eliza Moss Hall, Nia erased the phrase **optimal evacuation route** and replaced it with **route sensitivity under missing capacity data**.

Two commitments took shape that night: one relationship and one research question, each becoming more honest as it became narrower.
