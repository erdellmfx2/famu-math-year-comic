# Week Eight: No Model for the Storm

By Friday morning, Malik had six weather tabs open.

One showed the storm's projected path. One displayed rainfall totals. Another tracked river gauges near the Mississippi coast. Two offered different wind estimates. The final tab was a local news livestream from Gulfport, muted beneath Dr. Brooks's calculus lecture.

His mother had texted at 7:11.

**Water is already gathering at the end of the street. We moved the photo boxes up. Daddy says do not worry. You know how he is.**

Malik knew exactly how his father was.

Marcel Baptiste believed most problems improved when met with work boots, a good wrench, and fewer people standing around discussing them. If a storm pushed water toward the house, he would lift furniture until his back hurt. If the lights failed, he would check every breaker before admitting the outage extended beyond their walls.

On the board, Dr. Brooks differentiated a product of two functions.

"We cannot treat both factors as fixed," she said. "Each is changing, so each contributes to the rate of change."

Malik refreshed the forecast.

The projected rainfall increased by two inches.

## Friday, September 19: Refresh

Nia noticed the third time Malik looked down during class.

He never looked at his phone during class. He barely approved when other people did.

"Home?" she whispered.

He nodded.

Dr. Brooks moved from the product rule to examples, but Malik's notes became fragments. He copied the derivative of the first factor, forgot the second, and refreshed the river gauge again.

After class, Nia followed him into the hall.

"What do they need?"

"Nothing yet."

"Do they have supplies?"

"Yes."

"Have they moved the car?"

"My mother did. My father says the truck is high enough."

"Is it?"

"That depends on the water."

Malik was already walking toward North Star, where he could use three screens at once. Nia kept pace.

"I can help call--"

"Please do not."

The words came out harder than he meant.

Nia stopped.

Malik turned back. "I am sorry. Everybody keeps asking what they can do. I do not have a job to assign."

"You do not have to assign me one."

"Then I need quiet."

Nia swallowed the ten responses that arrived immediately.

"Okay," she said. "Quiet."

She left him at North Star with the forecasts, the grade he had still not discussed, and a fear too large for either of them to organize.

## Saturday, September 20: A Range Is Not Comfort

Dr. Bennett's Saturday colloquium had been scheduled weeks earlier. Its title, **Decisions Under Uncertainty**, now appeared designed specifically to torment Malik.

The lecture hall filled with students, faculty, and several members of Heron Analytics. On screen, Dr. Bennett displayed a storm cone.

"People often read a projected path as a promise," he said. "It is not. A forecast communicates a range of plausible outcomes based on current evidence. The center line is not destiny, and the edge is not safety."

Malik wrote down the definitions. Confidence interval. Scenario range. Conditional probability. Expected loss.

Each term gave him a more precise way to describe why he could not breathe normally.

None made breathing easier.

During questions, a student asked why forecasts changed so often.

"Because the atmosphere changes and observations improve," Dr. Bennett said. "Revision is not proof that the earlier forecast was dishonest. It is what responsible modeling looks like when new evidence arrives."

Malik thought of his family budget. His missing-data chart. His calculus score. Every model this semester had asked him to revise.

He was tired of revision. He wanted one answer to hold.

After the talk, Simone found him in the aisle.

"I can reassign your opponent-data section," she said.

"No."

"That was an offer, not a demotion."

"I can finish it."

"I did not ask whether you could."

The echo of Nia's first question at the Bridge table caught him off guard.

Simone lowered her voice. "Tell me by Sunday night. If I do not hear from you, I am dividing it. That is a team decision, not a judgment of your character."

Malik nodded, though control was the only thing he did not want divided.

## Sunday, September 21: Help Without Taking Over

McCall-Hart opened a storm-relief supply table in Hart Student Union on Sunday.

Nia arrived early, created category signs, rearranged the donation bins, drafted a volunteer schedule, and began designing a tracking sheet before remembering that nobody had asked her to run the operation.

The actual coordinator, a public-health senior named Imani Cole, arrived carrying two flattened boxes.

"You must be DJ's sister," Nia said.

"And you must be the person who turned my folding table into a logistics center."

Nia looked at the labels. "Too much?"

Imani considered them. "Useful. But ask before you redesign somebody's whole system."

"That advice is following me lately."

Together they sorted batteries, cleaning supplies, shelf-stable food, work gloves, and hygiene items for affected families. Imani explained that useful relief work depended on verified requests, not on collecting whatever made donors feel generous.

Malik stopped by in the afternoon.

Nia started to tell him about the supply categories, transportation list, and emergency grant information. Then she saw his face.

"Quiet?" she asked.

"Please."

She nodded toward an empty chair behind the table.

Malik sat. Nia worked beside him without requesting updates. She handed him water once. When another student recognized his Gulfport hometown and began asking questions, Nia redirected the student toward the volunteer signup.

After twenty minutes, Malik stood.

"Thank you," he said.

"I did nothing."

"Exactly."

Nia watched him leave and understood, perhaps for the first time, that care did not always need to become visible labor.

## Monday, September 22: Verified

Julian canceled his usual music segment Monday evening.

Instead, campus radio carried storm updates, university support information, and interviews with staff coordinating student assistance. Julian repeated only details confirmed through official sources and corrected one rumor about mandatory evacuations before it could spread across residence-hall group chats.

Nia sat behind the booth glass, watching him work.

His public confidence had changed shape. He did not joke to fill every pause. He checked names, links, and times before speaking. When a caller began describing frightening footage from an unknown account, Julian interrupted gently.

"I hear why that worried you," he said. "We cannot verify that video, so we are not going to tell listeners it shows the current conditions. Let me give you the confirmed update we have."

During a break, Nia entered the booth.

"You are good at this."

Julian removed one headphone. "I am trying to be careful."

"That is what I mean."

He looked through the glass toward the students waiting with resource sheets. "Media can make fear move faster than weather. If we add to it, we need to know why."

Nia thought of Malik refreshing forecasts, trying to separate probability from panic.

"Can you share the supply-drive hours again?" she asked.

"Already in the next block."

He took her hand for three seconds beneath the desk, then returned to the microphone.

Nia had liked Julian when he made a room brighter. That evening, she admired him for refusing to make a crisis about his own voice.

## Tuesday, September 23: The Call

The storm crossed the coast before dawn.

Malik slept in intervals of twelve and seventeen minutes, waking each time his phone vibrated. At 5:36, Celeste reported water at the front steps. At 6:02, it entered the utility room. At 6:19, the power failed.

Then messages stopped.

Malik called his mother. No connection.

He called his father. Voicemail.

He called the neighbor across the street. Nothing.

By eight, water had flooded the lower rooms of the Baptiste home.

At 10:14, Celeste finally reached him from a relative's phone.

"We are safe," she said before he could speak. "We got to Aunt Renee's. Micah is with us."

Micah, Malik's twelve-year-old cousin, spent most afternoons and many weekends at the Baptiste house. His school backpack and the game system he had saved for all summer were still there.

"Where is Daddy?" Malik asked.

Silence.

"Mom."

"He went back when the water started dropping. He wanted to move the shop equipment before more rain. Something slipped. He cut his leg bad and twisted his knee. Mr. Landry took him to urgent care."

Malik stood so quickly his desk chair struck the wall.

DJ sat up in bed. "What happened?"

Celeste continued talking: stitches, imaging, no fracture visible, more evaluation needed. Malik wrote every detail on the back of an analytics printout because it was the closest paper within reach.

"Why did he go back?" he demanded.

"Because that equipment is how he works."

"He cannot work if he cannot walk."

His mother went quiet.

Malik pressed his hand over his eyes. "I am sorry. I am sorry. Tell him I am coming home."

"We do not even know which roads are open."

"I will find one."

The same instinct that had built budgets and charts now seized on bus routes, rental cars, airport schedules, and road closures. If he could arrange transportation, perhaps the rest of the disaster would become a list.

It did not.

## Wednesday, September 24: The Quiz He Misses

Malik was still trying to get home when his calculus quiz began.

The earliest bus had been canceled. The next route required two transfers and ended far from Gulfport. Flights cost more than his family had available. A rideshare estimate looked almost insulting.

At 9:10, Nia texted: **Are you coming to class?**

He typed **Yes**, deleted it, and called Dr. Brooks.

She answered from her office.

"Malik?"

For several seconds, he could not force the situation into an orderly sentence.

"The storm flooded our house," he said. "My father was injured during cleanup. I am trying to get to Gulfport. I missed the quiz."

"Are your family members safe now?"

"Yes. My father has stitches and a knee injury. They are staying with my aunt."

"All right. The quiz is not today's emergency."

"I can take it online."

"No. You can document the absence when you have access to the records. We will arrange make-up work after you know your travel plan."

"I already have a sixty-four on the test."

The confession escaped before he could stop it.

Dr. Brooks's voice remained steady. "Which is why I invited you to office hours. We will address that grade. It is not improved by pretending your family crisis is a normal study day."

Malik sat on the edge of his bed.

"I should have come," he said.

"Yes," she replied, without pretending otherwise. "And you called today. That matters."

After they hung up, Malik emailed documentation requests, notified Dr. Bennett, and accepted that the team would redistribute his work.

The list did not make him calm.

It gave him somewhere to begin.

## Thursday, September 25: The Station

Nia found a bus route leaving Montgomery late Thursday and connecting toward the coast. DJ borrowed his sister's car to get Malik to the station.

The three of them drove through Bellwether beneath a sky scrubbed clear after days of threat. The normal weather felt rude.

Malik carried one backpack. Nia had packed snacks. DJ had packed twice as many snacks and called them "infrastructure."

At the station, announcements cracked through tired speakers. Families clustered around charging outlets. A television above the ticket counter showed aerial footage of flooded neighborhoods.

Malik kept his eyes off the screen.

"Simone said the team has your work," DJ told him. "Dr. Brooks knows. I have the room."

"Do not touch the folders on my desk."

"I will guard them with my life."

Nia held out the bag of food. "You do not have to text updates. But if you want to, I will answer."

Malik took the bag. "Thank you."

His bus had not yet arrived. The waiting made every thought louder.

"My mom says they are safe," he said. "My father says his knee is fine, which means it is not."

DJ nodded.

"I keep looking at flood estimates," Malik continued. "But I do not know how high the water got inside. I do not know what insurance will cover. I do not know whether his equipment works."

He looked at Nia then, letting her see the fear without turning it into a question she had to solve.

"I do not know what they will come back to."

Nia's eyes filled, but she did not promise everything would be all right.

"You will not face it by yourself," she said.

The bus pulled beneath the station lights.

Malik hugged DJ first, quick and hard. When Nia stepped forward, he hesitated only a moment before holding her too.

Then he boarded with no model for the house waiting at the other end.
