# Week Thirty-Six: Different Parameters

Dr. Delgado drew two parametric curves.

Both used time as the parameter. At every moment, each curve had its own horizontal and vertical position. For several values, the points appeared close. Then one curve turned while the other continued forward.

"Sharing a parameter does not mean sharing a path," he said.

Nia calculated velocity components. Malik found where one curve slowed. Amara identified the moment their directions diverged.

Outside class, Friday was the course-withdrawal deadline. Students made decisions about classes they had entered months earlier with different information. Staying was not automatically brave. Leaving was not automatically failure. The question was which path remained honest under current conditions.

Malik copied both curves into his notes.

Two people could share time, affection, and important experiences without moving toward the same destination.

## Friday, April 3: Same Time, Different Motion

Dr. Delgado asked the class to distinguish speed from direction.

Two objects could move at the same speed and travel apart. One object could change direction without stopping. Arc length could measure the distance along a path without explaining why the path had turned.

"Do not let one shared quantity persuade you that two motions are identical," he said.

After class, Malik met Simone at the Porter Center.

They had spent the week discussing London in careful pieces. Underneath every practical question was the larger one neither could postpone: was distance creating their incompatibility, or revealing it?

Simone carried the copper notebook. Many pages now contained answers, crossed-out thoughts, and new questions.

"I want to talk tomorrow," she said. "Not between meetings. Not during interview practice."

Malik understood that she had chosen the time deliberately.

"Okay."

They did not fill the silence with one more pleasant evening.

Across campus, Nia reviewed the final symposium materials with Keisha and Julian. She knew Malik and Simone were strained. She did not ask whether the strain had created an opening.

Respect, her mother had said, was something active.

Nia practiced it by remaining on her own path.

## Saturday, April 4: London Is Not the Whole Reason

Simone met Malik beneath the covered terrace behind Porter Center.

"London matters," she began. "I want the internship. I do not want to begin it by promising that every free hour belongs to maintaining a relationship across an ocean."

Malik listened.

"But London is not the whole reason," she continued. "I have felt you comparing us with an unfinished possibility. Sometimes when Nia enters a room, you become more aware of what our relationship proves. When Julian is near her, you become more afraid of what you lost."

"I did not choose you to prove something."

"I believe that. I also believe you entered this relationship partly because moving forward felt healthier than waiting."

Both statements could be true.

Simone opened the notebook to one of Malik's original questions: *What does support feel like when you are disappointed?*

"Support is not asking me to remain inside a comparison until you resolve it," she said. "And care is not pretending London alone is taking me away."

Malik's instinct was to promise he would stop comparing. The promise would demand that Simone trust a future correction she had already spent spring waiting to see.

He did not make it.

"You deserved my full presence," he said.

"I deserved honesty about what full meant."

They sat with the difference.

## Sunday, April 5: The Apology Without a Request

Malik returned Sunday with no argument for why Simone should stay.

"I am sorry," he said. "I thought moving forward meant never looking back. I treated choosing you as proof that everything unresolved had disappeared. Then whenever it came back, I hid it because admitting it felt disloyal."

Simone listened.

"That made the feeling more powerful," he continued. "And it made you carry consequences for something you did not cause."

He apologized for the distant interview practice, the private comparisons, and the expectation that consultation would prove commitment. He did not describe every good thing he had done as a counterweight. Care was not a credit balance.

"Our relationship was real," Simone said.

"Yes."

"I do not want your apology to rewrite it as a mistake."

"I do not either."

They remembered the food-distribution station, the road trip, her panel, the copper notebook, and the many ordinary lunches that had never needed to become lessons.

Malik did not ask her to give him more time. He did not promise that Nia would become irrelevant. He apologized without converting accountability into a request for another chance.

For Simone, that made the apology kinder.

It did not change her decision.

## Monday, April 6: No Promise After London

They ended the relationship where it had grown: the Porter Center.

Students moved through the lobby carrying laptops and coffee. Somewhere upstairs, a finance club meeting began. The ordinary building refused to become a private stage.

"I am leaving for London," Simone said. "But even if I were staying in Bellwether, I would need to step out of this comparison."

"I understand."

"Do not tell me we can try again after summer."

Malik swallowed. "I will not."

They made no promise to resume when she returned. They did not call the relationship a failure because it ended. Simone had learned that she could name ambition without apology. Malik had learned that choosing someone required more than stopping pursuit of somebody else.

Simone returned the archival-photo sleeve she had borrowed for a presentation. Malik told her to keep the copper notebook.

"The questions are yours now," he said.

She held it against her bag.

They hugged once. Neither hurried away afterward. Sadness occupied the lobby with them until Simone finally turned toward the stairs and Malik walked outside.

No one was humiliated. No one had won.

Two paths that had shared a season separated with both histories intact.

## Tuesday, April 7: Hope Arrives Too Fast

Malik told Nia himself.

He asked her to meet on Cypress Walk in the afternoon. His face told her the news before the sentence did.

"Simone and I ended our relationship yesterday."

Hope rose inside Nia with terrible speed.

It was immediate, bright, and followed by guilt so sharp that she almost apologized aloud.

Malik looked exhausted. "She chose London. But that was not the only reason."

Nia did not ask whether she was another reason. "I am sorry."

"Thank you."

They stood beneath new spring leaves. Nia wanted to comfort him. She also knew that part of her had already turned his pain into possibility. The knowledge made every instinct suspect.

"Do you want company?" she asked.

"Not right now. I wanted you to hear it from me rather than through the group."

"Okay."

She let him leave.

Back in North Star, she told Keisha about the hope.

"A feeling can arrive before your values," Keisha said. "What matters is whether you let it drive."

Nia opened the symposium script instead of Malik's messages.

Hope had arrived.

It did not receive permission to act.

## Wednesday, April 8: No Shortcut Through Grief

Nia found Malik after calculus.

Dr. Delgado had assigned an arc-length problem. Nia used the mathematics as an excuse to begin talking, then heard herself drifting toward the conversation she wanted.

"We never really discussed fall," she said. "Not directly."

Malik closed his notebook.

"No," he said. "We did not."

The quiet invited her to continue.

"Maybe after the symposium, we could--"

"Nia."

He did not sound angry. That made the boundary clearer.

"I am not turning your breakup or mine into a shortcut," he said. "Julian mattered to you. Simone mattered to me. We both need to understand what happened without using each other to prove we are ready."

Embarrassment heated Nia's face. "I was not asking you to date me tomorrow."

"I know. But you were asking us to begin moving in that direction. I cannot."

She wanted to argue that her relationship had ended months ago, that she had respected his, that waiting now felt unfair.

Respect was not a ticket that matured into a claim.

"You are right," she said.

"I care about you. That is why I need the boundary."

The sentence hurt and steadied her at once.

They left separately.

## Thursday, April 9: Submit Without the Answer

The research team completed its final symposium submission at 6:42 Thursday evening.

Keisha verified the software package and checksum. Julian confirmed every media permission and caption. Nia uploaded the presentation, methods summary, limitations statement, and fellowship-interview materials.

Dr. Price required each person to approve the final version.

Keisha clicked first. Julian clicked second. Nia clicked last.

The portal displayed **SUBMISSION COMPLETE**.

They celebrated with takeout in the lab. Simone sent a brief message congratulating all three. Nia replied with thanks and did not mention Malik. Julian discussed the documentary cut. Keisha made everyone close their laptops for twenty minutes.

Later, Malik arrived to return a risk-analysis article Nia had lent him. They stepped into the hallway.

"The submission is done," she said.

"Congratulations."

"I meant what I said yesterday. You were right."

Malik nodded. "Through the presentation, we need distance. No private late-night preparation. No using the research as a reason to have the conversation early."

"Agreed."

The boundary protected both of them. It also removed the person Nia most wanted to use as evidence that she would be okay no matter what the judges decided.

She returned to her team.

The symposium would ask her to stand behind the work without certainty, romance, or reconciliation as emotional support beams.

For the first time all year, Nia would have to enter the most important room wanting something deeply and carrying no private promise about what waited after the answer.
