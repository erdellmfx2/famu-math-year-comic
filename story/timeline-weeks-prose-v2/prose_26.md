# Week Twenty-Six: Choosing a Method

Dr. Delgado wrote three integrals across the board.

At first glance, they looked like siblings: each contained a product, each included an exponential or trigonometric expression, and each appeared designed to ruin somebody's morning.

"Which formula are we using?" a student asked.

"That," Dr. Delgado said, "is the question that causes the trouble."

One integral yielded to substitution. One required integration by parts. The third could be manipulated either way, but one choice produced a longer problem than the one they started with.

Nia raised her hand. "Can we have a decision tree?"

"You may build one after you understand what the branches mean."

A groan moved across the room.

Dr. Delgado did not sympathize. "Methods are not recipes you match by appearance. Ask what changes when you differentiate one part. Ask what simplifies. Ask what new difficulty your choice creates."

Malik stared at the third integral. "So choosing a valid method is not enough."

"Correct. You are responsible for where the method takes you."

Nia wrote the sentence beside the symposium deadline in her notebook.

## Friday, January 23: The Recipe Revolt

By the end of class, the board looked like a family argument conducted in symbols.

Amara had chosen the wrong factor for (u) in integration by parts and produced an integral more complicated than the original. Nia used substitution where no matching differential existed. Malik reached a correct answer, but only after two pages of algebra that Dr. Delgado reduced to six lines.

"I object to the phrase *only after*," Malik said.

"Your objection has been recorded and denied."

Dr. Delgado made them annotate each attempt: what they noticed, why they selected a method, and the first sign that the choice was helping or failing.

Nia hated the exercise until she saw the pattern. Her worst decisions were not random. She noticed one familiar feature, committed too early, and then defended the method because changing course felt like admitting defeat.

After class, she met Keisha in the lab for the research data audit.

"Today I learned we are allowed to abandon a method," Nia said.

"I have been telling you that since August."

"Dr. Delgado used notation."

"I will add notation to future complaints."

They divided the evacuation records. Nia checked route assumptions and units. Keisha tested identifiers, missing values, and duplicate rows. Their application had been submitted, but Dr. Price required a full audit before they presented a single result.

For two hours, everything looked encouraging.

Then Keisha said, "Do not celebrate that graph."

## Saturday, January 24: The Beautiful Duplicate

Nia's first graph was excellent news.

Under most simulated capacity changes, the recommended routes remained stable. The result suggested their network could tolerate uncertain data better than expected. The bars were clean. The colors were accessible. The title almost wrote itself.

Keisha turned her laptop around.

"These records have different row numbers but the same location, time stamp, and route code."

"Could two reports be identical?"

"Four hundred and twelve times?"

Nia's excitement began backing slowly toward the exit.

The duplicated records were not evenly distributed. One well-documented route appeared hundreds of times more often than several uncertain routes. Their graph did not prove broad stability. It proved that repeated copies of one stable case could dominate an average.

"Maybe the duplicates represent confirmed observations," Nia said.

Keisha gave her a look.

"I heard it while I was saying it."

They traced the error to merged files from two municipal exports. One dataset had already included the other's records. Nothing malicious had happened. The mistake was ordinary, quiet, and powerful enough to manufacture good news.

Nia stared at the graph. "The application deadline passed."

"Our question is still valid."

"Our preliminary result is not."

"Then we do not use it."

Nia saved the audit notes, removed the graph from the slide deck, and renamed the image **DO_NOT_RESURRECT.png**.

"Very technical," Keisha said.

"I want future me to feel warned."

The corrected data produced a messier picture: several route recommendations changed dramatically when capacity estimates moved. It was less comforting and far more relevant.

## Sunday, January 25: Delete in Daylight

Dr. Price convened the team Sunday afternoon. Alexis joined by video from the graduate lab, her face appearing above a stack of route maps.

Nia projected the beautiful graph first.

"This result is invalid," she said.

She explained the duplicate records, the biased average, and the corrected output. Then she deleted the graph from the active presentation while everyone watched.

There was no applause. Good research habits rarely received movie music.

Alexis asked why the duplicates had survived their first cleaning step.

Keisha explained that the files used distinct row identifiers, so exact-row detection failed. She proposed a composite check using location, time, and route attributes, followed by manual review of ambiguous matches.

"And what changes in the claim?" Dr. Price asked.

Nia took a breath. "We cannot say recommendations are generally stable. We can identify which parts of this sample network are sensitive to plausible capacity changes. The instability may be the finding."

"May be," Dr. Price repeated. "Keep that word until the evidence earns stronger language."

After the meeting, Nia expected to feel humiliated. Instead, she felt disappointed and strangely lighter. The false graph no longer required protection.

Keisha bumped her shoulder. "You deleted it before anyone had to wrestle the mouse away."

"Growth."

"Do not become unbearable about it."

They began the corrected run. Progress did not look like recovering the result Nia wanted. It looked like trusting the question enough to survive an inconvenient answer.

## Monday, January 26: Her Room, Her Work

The Porter Center career panel was called **Data in High-Stakes Financial Decisions**, a title that guaranteed half the audience came for insight and the other half for free sandwiches.

Simone sat onstage beside an alumna from a regional bank and a professor who studied consumer credit. She spoke about model outputs that appeared objective while encoding historical decisions about who received loans, which neighborhoods attracted investment, and whose missing data was treated as risk.

Malik sat in the third row.

He had helped Simone practice Sunday night, but none of her answers belonged to him. When questions opened, he waited while several students asked about salaries and software certifications.

Then he raised his hand.

"When a model influences a financial decision," he asked, "what should an analyst document so someone outside the technical team can challenge its assumptions?"

Simone's expression brightened, not because he had rescued her, but because the question gave her room.

She discussed model cards, data lineage, uncertainty, monitoring, and the need to record who had authority to override an automated recommendation. The alumna expanded on regulatory review. The conversation became the strongest part of the panel.

Afterward, Malik waited while other students approached Simone. He did not hover behind her chair or tell anyone they were dating. When she finally joined him, she carried three internship contacts and one untouched sandwich.

"Good question," she said.

"Good answer."

"You knew I had one."

"That was the point."

She kissed his cheek in the crowded hallway, then handed him the sandwich because she knew he had skipped lunch.

Supporting her had not required becoming visible in her success. It required paying attention to what she wanted the room to understand.

## Tuesday, January 27: A Seat Without a Sequel

Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse was almost full when Nia arrived for the men's basketball game.

Keisha was at track study hall. Imani was helping with softball recruits. The only open seat Nia could see was beside Julian, who had a camera bag under one knee and a paper tray of fries balanced on the other.

"This seat comes with media obligations," he said.

"I refuse to be interviewed during free throws."

"Reasonable boundary."

She sat.

The conversation began with the game and moved to the Marching Herons' spring travel schedule. Julian was helping produce short documentaries about student musicians balancing rehearsal, classes, and road trips. One trumpet player had recorded a statistics lecture from a hotel stairwell because it was the only quiet place in the building.

"You would like her," Julian said. "She labels every practice recording by date and emotional damage."

Nia laughed. "That is excellent metadata."

They did not discuss why their relationship ended. They did not test whether old tenderness remained. They talked about work they cared about, shared fries, and complained when an official missed an obvious traveling call.

Across the fieldhouse, Malik saw them.

His first reaction arrived before his better judgment: a tightness in his chest, a story about Nia and Julian finding their way back to each other, an old desire to know whether he had lost something.

Then Simone texted him a photograph of four internship application tabs and the caption: **ROMANCE IS TEMPORARILY UNDER REVIEW.**

Malik smiled, but the jealousy did not disappear.

He decided not to feed it with imagination.

## Wednesday, January 28: Ask the Actual Person

Malik found Nia outside Dr. Delgado's classroom.

"Can I ask you something directly?"

Nia closed her notebook. The formality in his voice made her brace herself. "Yes."

"Are you and Julian considering getting back together?"

The question was so clean that she had no place to hide inside it.

"No. We happened to sit together. We talked about his band project."

Malik nodded once.

Nia studied him. "Why are you asking?"

"Because I saw you, felt jealous, and did not want to invent an explanation."

Her heart made one disloyal leap at the word *jealous*.

Malik continued before she could build a future out of it. "The feeling is mine to understand. I am not asking you to change anything."

That sentence returned the room to its proper dimensions.

"Julian and I had closure," Nia said. "Not the fake kind where people say they are fine and then communicate through playlists. We mattered to each other. We are still over."

"Thank you."

"Are you going to tell Simone you asked?"

"Yes."

Nia believed him.

Dr. Delgado opened the classroom door and announced that anyone conducting emotional conferences in the hallway should conduct them elsewhere or begin evaluating an integral.

"We choose the integral," Nia said quickly.

"Speak for yourself," Malik replied, but he followed her inside.

## Thursday, January 29: Wanting the Wrong Proof

Nia told Keisha about the conversation while they checked the corrected dataset.

"He was jealous," Nia said.

"That is a feeling, not a proposal."

"I know."

"Your voice has created a parade for something you know."

Nia lowered her voice. "He cared."

"He asked a direct question, accepted your answer, and plans to be honest with his girlfriend. Those are good actions. Do not convert them into secret permission."

The correction stung because it was accurate.

Nia had told Malik the conversation with Julian was closure. It was true. She had also wanted Malik's jealousy to prove that his relationship with Simone had not changed what existed between them.

That was not a demand she had the right to make.

Across campus, Malik told Simone exactly what had happened.

She listened without pretending the admission was comfortable.

"Do you want to be with Nia?" she asked.

"I chose to be with you," he said.

"That is an action. I asked about the feeling."

Malik looked down. "Some of it is unresolved. I do not want to act on it."

Simone appreciated the truth and disliked needing it. Both reactions were allowed.

"Then do not make me manage what you refuse to examine," she said.

"I will not."

Neither called the conversation a victory. They stayed, asked harder questions, and made plans for Friday.

In the lab, Nia reviewed the corrected route results. She highlighted uncertainty instead of hiding it. A method was not good because it reached the answer she wanted. A relationship was not meaningful because it supplied the proof she wanted either.

Both required a more honest choice.
