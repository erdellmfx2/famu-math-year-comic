# Week Twenty-Five: Work Worth Doing

By nine Friday morning, the gym at Bellwether Community Center had become a machine made of folding tables, canned beans, clipboards, and people shouting helpful instructions in opposite directions.

Nia stood near the entrance holding a map that said the line should move clockwise.

The line had other plans.

Families entered through the east doors, checked in beside the bleachers, crossed to the produce tables, doubled back for dry goods, and then collided with volunteers bringing boxes from the loading dock. On paper, the path was a clean loop. In reality, one grandmother had stopped beside the oranges to reorganize three bags, two toddlers were conducting an unauthorized sprint relay, and a pallet of bottled water had narrowed the safest aisle to one person at a time.

Keisha stepped around a rolling cart. "Your map is being defeated by juice boxes."

"My map is a preliminary model."

"Your preliminary model is still losing."

Nia looked down at the evacuation network she and Keisha had brought for comparison. Roads, capacities, and shortest paths covered the page. It assumed movement happened along available edges. It did not account for people waiting, carrying supplies, helping relatives, or reaching a narrow point at the same time.

She circled the crowded aisle.

"Congestion," she said.

"Here? Absolutely."

"In our model. We treat a route like it stays equally usable no matter how many people choose it."

Keisha stopped teasing. "That is not a small problem."

"No," Nia said, watching the line bend around the water. "It is finally a useful one."

## Friday, January 16: The Map Meets the Room

The service project was part of McCall-Hart's Martin Luther King Jr. weekend, which meant students arrived in matching cream volunteer shirts and spent the first fifteen minutes taking pictures before anyone trusted them near inventory.

Malik and Simone were assigned to packing. Malik counted items with quiet precision. Simone redesigned the station after observing that everyone reached across everyone else for rice.

"You moved my beans," Malik said.

"I improved your beans."

"They were alphabetized by category."

"They are canned goods, not scholarship applications."

Within ten minutes, their table filled boxes faster than either neighboring station. Malik staged empty cartons. Simone called quantities. When supplies ran low, she announced it before the line stopped. They did not compete for control because each noticed what the other was already doing well.

Across the gym, Nia and Keisha timed how long families spent at each station. They asked permission before recording anything and used station numbers rather than names. A route that looked shortest was often slowest because it crossed the loading path. An older volunteer explained that the center normally placed water near the exit, but a delivery delay had forced them to improvise.

"So today's bottleneck is not permanent," Nia said.

"None of them are," the volunteer replied. "That is why a pretty plan can embarrass you."

Nia wrote the sentence in her notebook.

At cleanup, she showed Keisha a new question: how sensitive were recommended evacuation routes to missing road-capacity information and changing congestion?

Keisha read it twice. "Now we have something testable."

"Not just admirable?"

"Admirable does not compile."

Nia laughed. The project felt less like a promise to save everybody and more like work they could honestly do.

## Saturday, January 17: Clear Terms

The second service shift ended with flattened boxes stacked shoulder-high and Malik discovering rice inside one shoe.

Simone sat beside him on the community-center steps while volunteers waited for the campus shuttle. The winter sun was bright without being warm.

"We make a good station," Malik said.

"An irresistible declaration."

"I had a more complete thought."

"I was hoping."

He smiled, then became serious. "I like what we are doing. I do not want to keep calling it two dinners and coffee as if naming it would damage it."

Simone rested her forearms on her knees. "What name are you proposing?"

Malik had rehearsed several sentences. All of them sounded like contract language now.

"I want to date you exclusively," he said. "If you want that too."

Simone did not rescue him from the silence. She considered the offer with the same respect she gave important data.

"I do," she said. "Exclusively, but not invisibly. I am applying for internships, including one overseas. I am not shrinking that plan to make a relationship feel safer."

"I would not ask you to."

"You might try to solve the distance before it exists."

Malik conceded the point with one lifted hand.

"And you," Simone continued, "do not get to present calm when you are actually upset and then expect me to discover the difference."

"That seems specific."

"I pay attention."

"Then yes. Clear terms."

"You make dating sound like a well-designed study."

"Replication is important."

She laughed and leaned against his shoulder. When the shuttle arrived, they boarded as boyfriend and girlfriend, though neither said the titles aloud until DJ asked why Malik looked as if his credit score had improved.

## Sunday, January 18: The Long Way Home

Nia heard the news over brunch.

Malik said it simply. Simone reached for the hot sauce. Keisha studied her waffle with the concentration of a person refusing to become an emergency-response team.

"That is good," Nia said. "Really. Congratulations."

She meant it.

That was the inconvenient part.

Simone deserved someone who listened to her ambition without treating it as a threat. Malik deserved a relationship he had chosen rather than a waiting room built around Nia's uncertainty. Wanting those things for them did not prevent something inside Nia from folding in on itself.

After brunch, Malik and Simone walked toward the Porter Center. Nia said she needed to stop by the library.

She did not.

Instead, she took the longest reasonable route to North Star Hall. She crossed the arts quad, circled Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse, and followed the campus edge where bare trees scratched at the blue sky. Her phone offered shortcuts she ignored.

The walk was not punishment. It was privacy.

She did not want to perform being unaffected for friends who knew her too well. She also did not want pain to turn into evidence that Malik had done something wrong.

Near the residence hall, she found Julian loading saxophone cases into a van.

"You know North Star is the other direction," he said.

"I am pursuing a scenic theory of navigation."

"Your theory looks cold."

He offered no questions. She offered no explanation. They lifted the final case together, exchanged a small goodbye, and continued with separate days.

In her room, Nia opened the research map. A longer route was not always a mistake. Sometimes the shortest available path cost more than distance could measure.

## Monday, January 19: What the Work Requires

The university was closed for Martin Luther King Jr. Day, but Dr. Delgado had posted one optional problem to prepare for Tuesday.

A cart carrying relief supplies moved along a route while the force required to push it changed with distance. Students had to calculate the work.

Nia nearly skipped it. Then she pictured the community center: bottled water narrowing an aisle, full bags becoming heavier, volunteers slowing at turns.

The integral did not simply multiply one force by one distance. It accumulated changing effort across many tiny intervals.

She covered her desk with notes.

If evacuation difficulty changed along a road, then route length alone was not enough. A mile with reliable capacity, level access, and low congestion was different from a mile that narrowed near a flooded underpass. They could define a cost that changed by segment, then test how recommendations shifted when uncertain capacities changed.

Keisha returned from an early conditioning session and found Nia talking to a diagram.

"Should I give you privacy?"

"The diagram and I are developing a weighted route cost."

"That sounds serious."

Nia explained distance, expected load, access constraints, and uncertainty. Keisha listened, then opened her laptop.

"We can run scenarios instead of pretending we know one exact capacity," she said. "But we have to state which weights are assumptions."

"Agreed."

"And we are not calling it the Reynolds model."

"I was thinking Morgan-Reynolds."

"Alphabetical order has briefly saved you."

They worked until dinner, building a method that admitted what it did not know. The mathematics did not make the project noble. It made the work accountable.

## Tuesday, January 20: A Question, Not a Halo

Dr. Alana Price read their symposium draft in complete silence.

Nia had written about flooding, unequal access, vulnerable neighborhoods, and the urgent need for better evacuation planning. The introduction glowed with purpose. The method occupied four uncertain lines near the bottom.

Dr. Price placed the pages on her desk.

"You care," she said.

"Yes."

"That is not a research method."

Nia felt the sentence hit every paragraph she had polished.

Dr. Price tapped the title. "Symposium reviewers are not judging whether flooding is sad or whether service is good. They are judging whether you asked a precise question, used a defensible method, and understood your limits. Noble intentions can still produce unreliable claims."

Keisha turned the draft sideways and began crossing out entire sentences.

Nia stared at her. "Those took hours."

"They are wearing a halo where the method should be."

Dr. Price almost smiled.

Together, they narrowed the application. They would not claim to identify the best evacuation plan for Bellwether. They would test how missing or uncertain road-capacity data changed route recommendations in a defined sample network. They would compare scenarios, report instability, and name assumptions.

"Better," Dr. Price said. "Now who made which decisions?"

Nia began, "I developed--"

Keisha's chair made one quiet sound against the floor.

Nia stopped.

The application named Nia first. It described Keisha as technical support.

The project had survived its first inflated claim. Its next problem was sitting beside her.

## Wednesday, January 21: Put Her Name on the Decision

Keisha did not argue in Dr. Price's office. That would have made the correction too easy.

Back at North Star, she changed for track practice while Nia stood beside the desk holding the application.

"I wrote that badly," Nia said.

"You wrote what you believed."

"No. I know you are a coauthor."

"Then describe my authorship."

Keisha tied one shoe. "Who chose the data structure? Who built the scenario runner? Who flagged that one missing capacity should not become zero by accident? Who decided we need uncertainty ranges instead of one dramatic result?"

"You did."

"That is not support. Those are research decisions."

Nia wanted to explain that she had been rushing, that application language was awkward, that she had not meant to diminish anyone. Each explanation placed her intention at the center again.

"You are right," she said. "I will rewrite it. You decide whether the new version is accurate."

While Keisha practiced, Nia rebuilt the contribution statement. She named their shared question development. She credited Keisha's data-cleaning rules, code architecture, scenario design, and decision to preserve uncertainty. She described her own mathematical formulation, literature synthesis, and interpretation plan. She replaced every vague *we* with an actual responsibility where the distinction mattered.

Keisha returned after dark, damp with sweat and unimpressed by Nia's hopeful expression.

She read the new version.

"Move scenario design above implementation," she said. "The decision came before the code."

Nia moved it.

Keisha read again, then typed her name into the coauthor field herself.

"I am in," she said.

Nia did not hug her without asking. She did, however, perform a seated dance so embarrassing that Keisha threatened to withdraw on aesthetic grounds.

## Thursday, January 22: A Clean Exit

Malik found the symposium draft open on a shared-lab monitor Thursday evening.

Nia was at the whiteboard, trying to write a route-cost expression without making every variable look equally certain. Malik read the title, then the research question.

"This is sharper," he said.

She turned. "Sharper than the version you saw last week?"

"That version wanted to solve evacuation. This one asks what your data can support."

He pointed to the scenario table. "You are measuring how often the recommended route changes?"

"And where it changes. Keisha thinks instability near one bridge could matter more than an average over the whole network."

"She is right."

"I know. I credited her this time."

Malik looked at her, understanding more than the sentence directly said. "Good."

For several minutes, they talked about the model. It was familiar and easy. Nia felt the old rhythm return: question, answer, correction, dry joke. She wanted to ask whether becoming official felt different. She wanted to tell him that she had taken the long route Sunday. She wanted, most dangerously, to see whether the quiet between them could still become something else.

Malik checked the time.

"I am meeting Simone," he said. "Your question is strong. Submit it."

He packed his notebook and left before Nia could turn intellectual closeness into emotional trespassing.

The door clicked shut.

Nia faced the whiteboard again. Respecting a boundary did not require pretending the feeling vanished. It required deciding what the feeling was allowed to do.

She completed the equation, sent the draft to Keisha, and waited for her coauthor's approval before touching the submit button.
