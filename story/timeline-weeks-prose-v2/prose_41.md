# Week Forty-One: Vectors and Boundaries

Malik returned from Gulfport carrying photographs of new framing inside the Baptiste home.

Fresh lumber outlined the back rooms damaged by the storm. Electrical work had passed inspection. Drywall waited in stacks.

The pictures looked like progress.

They did not contain a move-back date.

One contractor still needed to finish roof work. Insurance reimbursement remained incomplete. Marcel's injured leg had improved, but he could not yet handle every repair task himself.

Malik placed the photographs in archival sleeves. He labeled dates without labeling the family recovered.

The house had a direction.

Its remaining distance was unknown.

## Friday, May 8: Framing Is Not a Finished Room

Celeste showed Malik where the repaired wall would divide the back bedroom from the hall.

"By July, maybe," she said.

Marcel responded from the doorway. "Do not give the contractor a date he did not give us."

They had learned to distrust hopeful estimates spoken as promises.

Before returning to Bellwether, Malik photographed the framing, moisture barriers, and uncompleted ceiling. He asked his parents whether he could show the images to the summer research team if the discussion required a real example of documented and undocumented loss.

"Not yet," Celeste said. "Tell them the story first. Let us decide about pictures after we know why they need them."

"Okay."

The permission boundary was clear.

On the drive back, Malik considered how insurance records would price the repaired materials. The records would not capture months of living around closed rooms, his mother's caution about dates, or his father's frustration at being unable to finish work with his own hands.

Risk metrics turned experience into quantities.

The quantities needed to remain answerable to the people whose experience they represented.

## Saturday, May 9: Carry the Label

Nia returned to campus with the rejected symposium poster rolled beneath one arm.

Keisha saw the fellowship-finalist label printed across the bottom.

"We can trim that off," she said.

Nia considered it. The label made the loss visible. It also described the version they had actually built.

"No," she said. "We archive honestly."

They mounted the poster in the summer project room beside a blank board titled **COMMUNITY VALIDATION QUESTIONS**. The old project and the new work occupied the same wall without pretending they were the same stage.

Julian would contribute during scheduled media sessions around his summer production work. He was not enrolled in the full quantitative program. Imani was preparing for tournament travel. DJ began offseason training. Simone sent the group a photo from orientation, standing beneath an airport departure board with the copper notebook visible in one hand.

Everyone's summer had a different direction.

Nia unpacked her route maps. She felt nervous about seeing Malik and refused to disguise the feeling as research intensity.

"I have feelings," she told Keisha.

"Historically verified."

"I am also a co-lead."

"Then lead the project, not the feelings."

The advice lacked poetry and possessed excellent timing.

## Sunday, May 10: Separate Responsibilities

Summer Quantitative Scholars orientation began in Eliza Moss Hall.

Dr. Renee Okafor welcomed a smaller group than the spring program. Summer moved faster. Calculus III met nearly every weekday. Research sessions occupied afternoons. Students who treated the compressed calendar like ordinary semester time would discover the error before the calendar felt sympathy.

She introduced the evacuation team by responsibility.

Nia: mathematical model and community-integration lead.

Keisha: computation and data-governance lead.

Malik: risk and validation analyst.

Dr. Price supervised the public-mathematics and research design. Dr. Okafor supervised multivariable modeling. No one described Malik and Nia as a natural pair.

During role questions, Malik asked which decisions required co-lead approval. Keisha answered before Nia. Nia supported her answer without repeating it louder.

Progress already.

The team received one shared workspace and separate task folders. Dr. Okafor warned them that interdisciplinary work would create disagreement.

"Professional collaboration does not mean avoiding conflict," she said. "It means making the conflict inspectable."

Nia and Malik exchanged a glance.

They had several kinds of conflict waiting.

Only one belonged in the first design review.

## Monday, May 11: Magnitude Is Not Direction

Calculus III began with vectors.

Dr. Okafor drew arrows in three-dimensional space. Each had magnitude and direction. Two vectors could have equal magnitude while pointing opposite ways. A very large vector aimed badly could move a system far from its goal.

"Students often confuse intensity with progress," she said. "Working harder, caring more, or moving faster tells us nothing about whether the direction is useful."

Nia wrote the sentence beside the rejected fellowship label in her notes.

Malik calculated vector components. Keisha represented a route displacement in three dimensions, adding elevation to the road network. A short horizontal path up a steep grade might impose very different access demands.

During practice, Nia and Malik solved one vector sum almost simultaneously.

The old ease appeared.

Neither treated it as a sign.

After class, Nia went to the project room with Keisha. Malik met Dr. Bennett to review validation standards. They had moved in related directions without attaching themselves to one arrow.

## Tuesday, May 12: Put the Boundaries in the Document

Malik and Nia wrote collaboration rules in a shared document.

Keisha attended as co-lead and delighted witness.

No one-on-one work after nine at night unless the task was urgent and logged with the team.

Private relationship conversations would not occur during project meetings.

Technical disagreement could happen in front of the team. Personal history could not be used to win it.

Either person could request a pause without the pause being treated as abandonment.

No private messages about project decisions that excluded Keisha from co-lead authority.

"That one should be first," Keisha said.

They moved it.

Nia added: no interpreting academic ease as romantic progress.

Malik added: no using distance as punishment after disagreement.

The document felt awkward because it named the exact habits they could otherwise pretend were accidental.

They all signed.

"Congratulations," Keisha said. "Your feelings now have governance."

"Please never say that again," Malik replied.

The rules could not guarantee wise behavior. They could make violations visible before the team paid for them.

## Wednesday, May 13: The Last Road Loss

Baseball's final road series ended with McCall-Hart outside postseason qualification.

The seniors knew before the last out that no tournament invitation was coming. They packed the dugout quietly, then gathered on the field for a team photograph in another school's stadium.

Julian filmed short interviews for campus media. He did not ask what lesson the seniors had learned from losing. He asked what work they wanted younger players to inherit.

One senior named the tutoring schedule they had established. Another named offseason lifting standards. A pitcher described the system for checking on injured teammates after travel.

The season record remained below .500.

The inherited work remained real.

Back in Bellwether, the friend group sent messages without demanding that seniors perform gratitude. A home finale still waited, but postseason hope did not.

Nia thought about the fellowship poster mounted in the project room. A failed desired outcome and useful inherited work could occupy the same wall.

Summer was already asking them to practice that truth again.

## Thursday, May 14: Leave When the Assignment Ends

The first vector assignment took Nia and Malik less than an hour together.

They decomposed forces, found magnitudes, and used unit vectors to express direction. Their approaches fit. Malik checked components. Nia visualized geometry. Each caught one small error from the other.

At 8:17, they finished.

The campus outside Eliza Moss Hall was warm and quiet. In the past, they would have extended the night with another problem, a walk, or a conversation neither was ready to name.

"Good work," Nia said, packing her notebook.

"You too."

They left the building together, reached the courtyard, and separated.

Malik went to North Star. Nia called her mother while walking toward summer housing. Neither turned back to see whether the other did.

The efficient assignment proved they still worked well together.

Leaving on time proved they could decide what that fact was allowed to mean.
