# Week Eighteen: The Work Before the Answer

McCall-Hart welcomed students back from Thanksgiving with couple traditions Nia had never noticed before.

People took matching photos beneath the first holiday lights. Two students shared headphones on Cypress Walk. Copper Cup advertised a two-drink study special called **The Perfect Pair**, which felt less like marketing and more like targeted harassment.

Nia returned to the residence hall Friday afternoon, unpacked, and discovered the scarf Julian had returned during their breakup folded between two sweaters.

She sat on the edge of her bed holding it.

Keisha looked up from her laptop. "What do you need?"

The old Nia would have said she was fine. A slightly newer Nia might have created an evening plan involving three friends, a review session, and service work until sadness lost her address.

"Can you stay?" she asked. "Just for tonight. I do not want the first quiet evening back by myself."

Keisha closed the laptop.

"Yes."

The answer contained no advice, which was one reason Nia trusted it.

## Friday, November 28: Ask, Then Receive

They ordered food, watched a ridiculous baking competition, and kept their phones on the other side of the room.

Halfway through the second episode, Nia began crying because one contestant dropped a cake.

"This is not about cake," Keisha said.

"The cake worked hard."

"The cake is structurally unsound sugar."

Nia laughed and cried harder.

She told Keisha about seeing Julian's performance, returning the key, and how respectful closure had somehow made the good memories more available.

"If he had been awful," Nia said, "I could be angry every time I missed him."

"You would still miss who you hoped he was."

"Why are you wise during my suffering?"

"Efficiency."

At ten, Nia thanked her.

"You do not have to thank me for living here," Keisha said.

"I asked you to change your evening."

"And I said yes because I wanted to. See how consent makes the schedule less haunted?"

Nia threw a pillow at her. This time Keisha did not catch it. The pillow struck her laptop bag, and both of them laughed until the room felt inhabitable again.

## Saturday, November 29: Grounds for Appeal

Malik met Dr. Bennett with the semifinal rubric, score report, and a draft appeal.

"I think the transferability question affected the judges too much," Malik said.

"That is not grounds for appeal."

"Another team also lacked external validation."

"Did the panel violate a published scoring rule?"

"Not that I can prove."

"Was there a mathematical error in the feedback?"

"The feedback did not include enough detail to determine that."

"Was your score recorded incorrectly? Did a conflict of interest go undisclosed? Was your team denied presentation time promised in the rules?"

"No."

Dr. Bennett slid the appeal draft back across the desk.

"Then identify the procedural error before you ask me to support this. Disliking how the judges weighted a real weakness is not evidence that the process failed."

Malik looked at the score report. "So we do nothing?"

"You document. You ask for clarification if the process allows it. You improve the work. An appeal is not a device for converting disappointment into another round."

Malik left angry enough to search every rule.

For two hours, he compared the timeline, scoring categories, judge assignments, and written feedback.

He found ambiguity.

He found no violation.

## Sunday, November 30: Withdraw

Malik withdrew the draft appeal Sunday morning.

The email was short: **After reviewing the published process and available records, I do not have evidence of a procedural error. I will not submit an appeal.**

Then he returned to his portfolio.

He added the semifinal result, judge feedback, and technical postmortem. His analysis distinguished performance error from model limitation. He could have framed his unanswered question as a speaking failure. Instead, he wrote that the question exposed an evidence boundary the team had communicated but not fully investigated.

He described what a stronger project would require: multi-team data, out-of-sample validation, operational testing, and a clearer account of which relationships could reasonably transfer.

Simone reviewed the entry.

"This makes us look unfinished," she said.

"We were unfinished."

"Correct answer."

She suggested one edit: add what the team had contributed successfully, not as consolation but as evidence. Malik included uncertainty communication, missing-data transparency, and decision-focused design.

The portfolio no longer read like a victory page or a confession.

It read like work.

## Monday, December 1: Share the Board

Nia led the final Calculus I review circle at North Star.

She divided the board into limits, derivatives, applications, and integrals. Normally she would have prepared every explanation herself. This time, she assigned Amara one-sided limits, Malik linear approximation, another student related rates, and two upperclass tutors integral applications.

"What are you teaching?" Amara asked.

"Connections between units," Nia said. "And snacks. I am coordinating snacks."

The circle began well. Then a student asked about optimization, and Nia lost the thread halfway through explaining endpoint checks. For a terrible second, her mind supplied Julian's joke about her turning every idea into a story.

She stopped.

"Amara, can you finish why the critical point is not automatically the maximum?"

Amara stepped forward and completed the explanation.

Nia drank water, looked at her notes, and returned for the next section.

She did not apologize for needing help. She did not reclaim the board with double energy. The review became stronger because several voices carried it.

Afterward, a student told Nia, "I like how everybody explains it differently."

Nia looked at the erased board. "Me too."

## Tuesday, December 2: Freshman of the Week

DJ was named conference freshman of the week after the River Classic.

The announcement appeared at nine. By nine fifteen, his family group chat had produced forty-three messages and one aunt had misspelled "conference" in three unique ways.

At ten, coaches congratulated him and reminded the team that the regular-season finale still required preparation. At noon, a student reporter requested another interview.

At two, DJ sat in Jubilee Library staring at a statistics paper due by midnight.

The assignment asked him to compare two measures of player performance and discuss how sample size affected the conclusion. He had chosen catch percentage and yards per route run, then discovered that celebrating online did not generate a literature review.

Malik found him surrounded by articles.

"Freshman of the week," Malik said.

"Freshman with a paper due. The title did not grant immunity."

"Tragic."

DJ showed him the comparison. Malik asked questions instead of rewriting it: Did catch percentage account for target difficulty? Could limited snaps inflate yards per route? What did each metric reward?

"So the answer is both numbers have agendas," DJ said.

"The numbers do not. The definitions do."

DJ typed that sentence into his notes, then added quotation marks and Malik's name.

"Do not cite me."

"Too late, Professor Data Man."

He submitted at 11:47 p.m.

Public success had not completed his private assignments. It had made them harder to schedule.

## Wednesday, December 3: The Group Outing

The basketball home opener gave everyone an excuse to leave their review notes for two hours.

Malik and Nia could have gone together. Neither suggested it.

Instead, they joined DJ, Keisha, Imani, Amara, and three analytics teammates at Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse. The group occupied most of a row and disagreed immediately about which concession line moved faster.

McCall-Hart's women opened with aggressive defense and a quick transition game. The men's team followed in the second half of the doubleheader. DJ analyzed spacing as if secretly auditioning for another sport. Keisha informed him that knowing statistics did not qualify him to coach from row twelve.

Nia cheered without checking whether Julian was covering the game. Malik sat beside Simone for part of the second half and listened to her explain why one lineup's apparent efficiency came from a favorable three-minute matchup.

No one used the evening to escape into romance.

When Nia became quiet during the band's halftime feature, Keisha squeezed her hand. When Malik checked a family message, Simone asked whether he needed to step out. Care moved through the group without becoming a spotlight.

McCall-Hart won both games.

For one night, uncomplicated celebration was allowed.

## Thursday, December 4: The Review Map

Malik and Nia finished their Calculus I review map at a wide table in Eliza Moss Hall.

They began with August: function values versus limits, one-sided behavior, and the first graph that had unsettled Malik. From there they linked continuity, derivatives as limits, rules, applications, antiderivatives, Riemann sums, definite integrals, the Fundamental Theorem, and net change.

Beside each topic, they wrote three things:

**What we thought. What changed. What still needs work.**

Malik wrote that he once believed a correct answer could speak for itself. Now he knew reasoning had to reveal assumptions, boundaries, and meaning. He still needed to slow down under time pressure.

Nia wrote that she once believed explaining quickly proved understanding. Now she knew listening could improve both the mathematics and the group. She still needed to check algebra when emotion scattered her attention.

For their final project, they used the map to build a short guide showing how one concept supported the next. The guide did not present their semester as a straight line. It showed loops, revisions, and ideas they had to encounter more than once.

"This is messy," Malik said.

"Accurately messy."

"The arrows cross."

"Learning does that."

He let the arrows remain.

Finals would produce grades soon. Football still had a conference title to chase. Neither result would answer every question the semester had raised.

For now, the work before the answer was complete.
