# Week Ten: The Cost of Looking Fine

Malik's new job began at the exact place where he was supposed to study.

At six Friday evening, he sat behind the welcome desk at North Star Learning Commons wearing a temporary name badge and learning the checkout system for calculators, laptop chargers, whiteboard kits, and small study rooms. Students passed him carrying calculus books, coffee, and the unmistakable panic of deadlines that had been reasonable until they became tonight's problem.

His supervisor, Ms. Alvarez, showed him the closing checklist.

"Last reservation ends at ten thirty. We close at eleven. You wipe the boards, check equipment, reset the rooms, and call campus safety if anybody refuses to leave."

"Does that happen often?"

"Around finals, students try to claim squatter's rights."

Malik smiled.

The shift fit the availability he had submitted. Friday and two weeknights, mostly after classes and analytics. It offered enough hours to send something home without losing every study block.

That was the model.

The model assumed no delayed buses, family calls, make-up assignments, team emergencies, or human need for sleep.

Malik did not write those limitations down.

## Friday, October 3: Closing Time

The first shift was quiet until nine, then every group room filled at once.

Malik found a missing calculator, reset a student's password request through the proper office, and explained three times that North Star loaned chargers but did not possess magical replacements for unfinished essays.

At ten fifty-eight, the final students left.

Malik completed the closing checklist, clocked out, and chose a table near the darkened tutoring boards. His linear approximation assignment was due Saturday. He only needed to finish four problems.

The first asked him to estimate a square root using a nearby value and a tangent-line approximation.

He wrote the function, chose the convenient point, calculated the derivative, and rested his forehead on one hand.

His phone showed two messages from Celeste about an insurance adjuster's request and one from Simone asking for a model update by noon.

Malik answered all three.

Then he returned to the tangent line.

At 12:43, Ms. Alvarez found him asleep over the problem while returning for the keys she had left in her office.

"Malik."

He woke so quickly his pencil rolled to the floor.

"The building is closed," she said.

"I work here."

"You worked here. Past tense. Now you are unconscious furniture."

He packed his bag, embarrassed.

Outside, Bellwether's night air felt cooler than it had all semester. Malik walked back to the residence hall telling himself the first shift was an adjustment.

He did not change the next shift on his calendar.

## Saturday, October 4: The Narrow Story

The analytics model checkpoint focused on late-game fourth-down decisions.

Malik had built a forecast using field position, yards to gain, score difference, time remaining, and recent opponent tendencies. The main visualization produced one clean recommendation for each scenario: attempt, punt, or kick.

It looked decisive.

Simone studied the screen. "Where is the uncertainty?"

"In the appendix."

"Put it here."

Malik opened a second chart. The recommendation changed across a wide interval when the sample was small or when assumptions about possession value shifted.

Andre leaned back. "So the main chart says go for it, but the range says all three options may be defensible."

"Under certain parameter choices," Malik said.

"How certain are those choices?" Simone asked.

"They use league averages."

"That was not my question."

Malik felt heat rise beneath his collar. "The single recommendation is easier for staff to interpret."

"Easy is not the same as useful."

"If we present every interval, the model looks indecisive."

Simone turned from the screen. "The model *is* uncertain. You are asking the colors to hide that."

The room went still.

Malik heard another meaning beneath her words, though she had not put it there. His family was safe. His job was manageable. His calculus recovery was underway. His analytics work remained on schedule. Every statement was technically defensible if nobody asked for the intervals.

"I will not present the main chart like this," Simone said. "Revise it."

Malik closed the file. "Understood."

He did understand.

He also resented her for making the weakness visible again.

## Sunday, October 5: Two Promises, One Evening

Julian's media showcase had been on Nia's calendar for three weeks.

His documentary class would screen one-minute student portraits at Bellwether Media House on Tuesday. Julian had filmed an elderly barber describing the first haircut he gave after returning from military service, all without spoken narration. He had shown Nia an early cut and asked her to attend the final screening.

"Front row?" he asked Sunday over lunch.

"Front row," she promised.

Two hours later, an Association for Women in Mathematics group chat began filling with panic about Monday's applications exam. The student scheduled to lead the review had lost her voice. Nia knew the material better than most of the first-years and had already made visual notes.

**Can anyone cover Tuesday at six?**

The showcase began at six thirty.

Nia typed **I can do 5:00-6:00**, then stared at the message. If the review ran on time, she could cross campus in fifteen minutes. If students had questions, she could still leave. Somebody else could close.

She sent it.

Within minutes, a flyer appeared in the group chat: **CALCULUS APPLICATIONS EXAM RESCUE REVIEW, TUESDAY 5-6:30, LED BY NIA REYNOLDS.**

"Six thirty?" Nia whispered.

She messaged the organizer to correct the time, but the flyer had already been reposted by two student accounts.

Nia considered telling Julian.

Instead, she decided she would make both.

It did not feel like lying. It felt like optimism with a tight schedule.

## Monday, October 6: The Missing Sentence

The applications exam began after Malik's father called at 12:08 a.m.

Marcel could not sleep. The knee brace rubbed against his stitches, the insurance adjuster had questioned part of the equipment claim, and he wanted to know whether Malik remembered where a receipt folder had been stored.

They talked until one twenty.

Malik studied until two fifteen because stopping felt like letting the call take something else.

On the exam, he handled basic derivatives correctly. Then he reached a linearization question asking him to explain why the approximation should be accurate near the chosen point.

He had practiced the justification: differentiability gives a locally linear model; error generally grows as the input moves farther from the center; the estimate should be evaluated within a reasonable neighborhood.

The words would not arrive.

Malik wrote, **Because the tangent line is close to the function**, then stared at the sentence. Close where? Under what conditions? How did he know?

He could see Dr. Brooks's likely comments before she wrote them.

Across the room, Nia worked steadily. Amara paused over one problem, then continued. The clock moved with cruel consistency.

Malik added a diagram and an incomplete error statement before time was called.

Outside, Nia asked, "How was it?"

He nearly said fine.

"I could not retrieve one justification," he said.

It was a small truth, but at least it was not the locked door.

## Tuesday, October 7: The Flyer

Julian discovered Nia's schedule conflict on a poster outside the union.

His own girlfriend's name appeared in large copper letters beneath **EXAM RESCUE REVIEW**. The end time matched the beginning of the media showcase.

He photographed the flyer and sent it to her with one question: **When were you planning to tell me?**

Nia called immediately.

"The time is wrong," she said.

"So you are not leading it?"

"I am, but only until six."

"The showcase starts at six thirty."

"I can make it."

"That is not what I asked."

Nia looked around the North Star lobby, lowering her voice. "I was going to tell you."

"Before or after I saved you a seat?"

The question felt sharper because she had promised the front row.

"Students need help," she said.

"I am not asking you to abandon them. I am asking why I keep learning your choices after everybody else."

"This is not like rehearsal."

"No. At rehearsal you rearranged your life without telling me. Here you rearranged our plans without telling me. Different direction, same surprise."

Nia's instinct was to list the ways she could still make everything work. Instead she looked at the students beginning to gather.

"You are right," she said. "I should have told you Sunday."

Julian was quiet.

"I will be there," she added.

"I hope so."

The review ended at 6:12. Three students still had questions. Nia handed them to another volunteer and ran across campus.

She entered the showcase as Julian's film ended.

He saw her in the doorway.

The empty front-row seat saw her too.

## Wednesday, October 8: No Rescue Plan

Malik brought the first test, the make-up packet, and his unfinished correction form to Nia.

They sat on a bench behind Jubilee Library, away from the students preparing Cypress Week decorations.

"I got a sixty-four," he said.

"I know. I saw the sheet."

"I have not finished the make-up work. I missed office hours before the storm. After I came back, I told Dr. Brooks about the travel and documentation, but not how far behind I already was."

Nia waited.

Malik had expected questions, schedules, and possibly a color-coded emergency plan.

"That is all?" he asked.

"Do you want me to say something?"

"You usually do."

"I am practicing not turning every hard thing into my assignment."

He looked at the test in his hands. "I rushed because of analytics. Then I hid the score because my parents had just celebrated the roster seat."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was avoidable."

"Those can both be true."

Nia did not tell him whom to email. She did not open her planner. She listened while he explained the midnight calls, the job hours, and the model revision Simone had refused to present.

When he finished, she asked, "What truth have you not told Dr. Brooks yet?"

Malik knew.

"That I cannot do all of it at the level I keep promising."

## Thursday, October 9: Reduced

Dr. Brooks listened without interrupting.

Malik told her about leaving the first test early, missing office hours, the make-up work, North Star shifts, analytics deadlines, and family calls that he kept treating as interruptions rather than part of his actual life.

"What are you asking for?" she said.

Malik had prepared requests for more time. Instead he said, "Help deciding what has to change."

Together they built a documented recovery plan: complete corrections, attend weekly office hours, use one North Star shift break only for food and rest, and schedule make-up work in pieces rather than one impossible weekend.

Then Malik met Dr. Bennett and Simone.

"I need a reduced role until my calculus work stabilizes," he said.

The sentence felt like stepping away from the chair he had fought to earn.

Dr. Bennett nodded. "What can you responsibly own?"

They moved Malik from primary model development to data validation and one smaller decision module. He would remain on the roster but lose some presentation responsibility at the checkpoint.

Simone updated the task board.

"You are not being removed," she said.

"It feels like going backward."

"Sometimes going backward is how you stop walking over a cliff."

Malik looked at the reduced assignment beside his name.

It was not the plan he wanted.

For the first time in weeks, it was a plan that might be true.
