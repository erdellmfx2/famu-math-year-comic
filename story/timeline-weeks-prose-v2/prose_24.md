# Week Twenty-Four: Volumes and Openings

Dr. Delgado refused to let anyone integrate.

He drew a region between two curves and announced that it would be rotated around a vertical line. Students reached for formulas.

"No computation," he said. "Choose a method first."

The room became uneasy.

Washers would slice perpendicular to the axis of rotation. Shells would slice parallel. Both could produce the same volume, but one setup required solving each curve for the other variable and splitting the region. The other used one clean integral.

"Shells," Malik said.

"Why?"

"Vertical slices match the current equations. Rotating them around a vertical axis creates shells. We avoid rewriting and splitting."

Dr. Delgado looked at Nia. "Would washers be wrong?"

"No. Just less efficient here. If the axis or equations changed, the choice could change."

"Exactly. Technique is a decision, not a reflex."

Only after the class defended its choice did he allow the first integral.

Nia drew a shell beside the region. Malik labeled radius, height, and thickness. The volume came last.

## Friday, January 9: Choose Before Computing

The class compared washer and shell methods across several solids.

Dr. Delgado required a short justification before every setup. "Because I remember the formula" earned no credit. Students had to name the slice, axis, radius or radii, and reason the method reduced complexity.

Nia liked the visual logic. She could imagine each rectangle turning through space. Malik liked that method choice could be defended by structure rather than preference.

Amara found a case where both methods required two integrals.

"Which is better?" she asked.

"Define better," Dr. Delgado replied.

Fewer algebraic steps? Easier bounds? Lower risk of sign error? More meaningful units? The answer depended on what they were trying to protect.

After class, Malik left to meet Simone for lunch. He did not announce it.

Nia noticed anyway.

She returned to her research notes and drew flood zones as regions. If a region rotated, she could visualize volume. If water depth varied across the actual city, the elegant solid became an approximation with geography hidden inside it.

Her project demanded a method choice too.

## Saturday, January 10: Elegant and Impossible

Nia presented the team with a polished three-dimensional sketch.

The flood zone became a rotated region. Height represented water depth. Concentric layers suggested growing exposure. The image was elegant enough for a poster.

Keisha placed the road network over it.

The recommended paths crossed water, ignored a rail line, and sent traffic toward a bridge with incomplete capacity data.

"Your picture is beautiful," Keisha said.

"I hear the word *but*."

"But the roads did not agree to become radial coordinates."

Alexis asked what the visual was supposed to establish.

"Accumulated exposure," Nia said.

"Then do not let the audience confuse exposure volume with an evacuation network."

They separated the layers. One visual estimated accumulated flood exposure. Another displayed the real road graph. A third showed where uncertain road capacities changed feasible routes.

To test the distinction, Nia used washers to estimate the volume represented by one simplified cross-section. Outer radius described the projected flood boundary; inner radius excluded a raised region. Keisha verified the numerical setup, then pointed to three roads inside the same geometric band.

One road crossed a low underpass. One ended at a locked service gate. The third appeared connected on the map but lacked a verified capacity value.

"The volume estimate can be mathematically sound," Keisha said, "and still tell us nothing about whether a bus can reach a shelter."

Alexis made Nia write that limitation directly beneath the image. The picture could describe exposure. It could not stand in for transportation access.

The result was less elegant and more truthful.

Nia saved the original image in a folder labeled **ATTRACTIVE WRONG IDEAS**.

Keisha approved. "That folder will be full by March."

"From both of us."

"Correct answer."

## Sunday, January 11: After the Error

The Heritage Tip-Off began with the women's basketball game at Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse.

Nia sat with Keisha and Imani. The McCall-Hart women led early, then lost the lead after two turnovers. A first-year guard made the second mistake in front of the student section and immediately looked toward the bench.

"That walk back is the longest distance in sports," Imani said.

As a softball shortstop, she knew what a visible error felt like: the ball rolling beneath a glove, the crowd reacting before the play finished, the next batter arriving while shame still occupied the last pitch.

The guard returned in the fourth quarter. She did not take a miraculous game-winning shot. She defended well, made the simple pass, and helped stabilize the offense during a winning run.

"People think confidence means forgetting the error," Imani said. "Usually it means making the next choice while remembering it."

Nia thought about the attractive wrong image, her first relationship, and the too-bright congratulations she had given Malik.

"How do you know when to trust yourself again?" she asked.

"You do not wait for trust to become a feeling. You prepare, then make the next defensible choice."

McCall-Hart won by seven.

Nia left the fieldhouse with no clear answer about Malik, only a better rule for what not to do next.

## Monday, January 12: Leave on Time

The men's Heritage Tip-Off game ran late.

Overtime began at 7:28. Malik's dinner with Simone was scheduled for eight. The student section begged everyone to stay. DJ argued that leaving overtime violated campus law.

At 7:34, Malik stood.

"The game is tied," DJ said.

"I can see that."

"History is occurring."

"Simone is waiting at eight."

"Tell her basketball requested an extension."

Malik put on his coat. "I told her I would be there."

Nia watched from two seats away as he left before the final score.

In fall, Malik had rushed a calculus test for analytics. Nia had tried to squeeze a review and Julian's showcase into one evening. Now Malik chose the prior commitment and accepted missing the campus moment.

He arrived at the restaurant at 7:56.

Simone glanced at the game updates on his phone. "You left overtime?"

"Yes."

"I am impressed and slightly concerned about your school spirit."

"DJ has enough for both of us."

McCall-Hart won while they ordered dinner.

Malik was disappointed to miss it.

He did not make Simone pay for the choice.

## Tuesday, January 13: Outside Achievement

Spring add-drop ended Tuesday, locking schedules into something more serious.

At dinner, Simone returned to the question Malik had not answered Thursday.

"What do you want outside achievement?"

Malik considered giving a polished response. He had none.

"Stability," he said. "A home where one storm does not undo everything. Work that pays enough for my parents to stop treating every bill like a threat."

"Those are still outcomes."

"I know."

He looked at his hands. "I want to be able to enjoy something while it is happening. I want people to tell me bad news without worrying I will turn it into a plan before they finish. I want to rest without feeling like someone else is becoming more prepared than me."

Simone's expression softened.

"That is unfinished," Malik said.

"Good. I asked what you want, not for a mission statement."

He asked her the same question.

Simone wanted work that placed her in consequential rooms, travel, financial independence, and relationships that did not require her to make ambition smaller to appear loyal.

Neither promise fit perfectly with the other.

They liked the honesty enough to keep learning.

## Wednesday, January 14: The Smile

Malik returned to the residence hall courtyard smiling.

Nia was crossing toward North Star with research maps under one arm. She saw him before he saw her.

The smile was not the public grin from a team announcement. It was loose, private, and attached to whatever message he had just read from Simone.

Nia stopped walking.

All fall, she had assumed Malik's feelings would remain where she left them: patient, quiet, available if she ever looked directly. She had never asked him to wait. She had also benefited from believing he would.

Malik noticed her. "Hey."

"Hey. Good date?"

"Yes."

No apology. No attempt to make the answer easier for her.

"Good," Nia said, less brightly this time.

He asked about the research maps. Nia explained the road-network problem. Malik listened, asked one useful question, and continued toward his room.

She watched him go.

The sharp feeling from New Year's Eve finally acquired a more honest name.

Loss of possibility.

And perhaps desire.

## Thursday, January 15: No Plan to Interfere

Nia told Keisha after track conditioning.

"I may have feelings for Malik."

Keisha drank half a bottle of water before responding. "May?"

"Fine. I have feelings for Malik."

"And Malik is dating Simone."

"They have been on two dates."

Keisha looked at her.

"I know how that sounded," Nia said. "I am not trying to interfere."

"Good, because I am not helping you build a strategy."

"I did not ask."

"Your face opened a planning meeting."

Nia sat beside her on the bench. "What am I supposed to do?"

"Feel it. Respect their relationship. Keep being honest with yourself. Do not turn friendship into a campaign."

"That sounds deeply inactive."

"It is active restraint. You should recognize the concept by now."

Nia groaned.

Keisha continued, "Also, our proposal is due soon. My code does not care about your romantic reversal."

"The code has poor emotional intelligence."

"The code has boundaries."

Nia opened the research folder.

The spring relationship had begun. So had her feelings. Only one of those facts gave her permission to act.
