# Week Five: Kickoff

The first college homework deadline arrived wearing football-weekend colors.

By Friday morning, indigo banners hung from the balconies of Hart Student Union. Copper herons appeared in shop windows across Bellwether. Alumni tents rose along Cypress Walk, and the Marching Herons rehearsed often enough that drumbeats seemed to travel through the sidewalks.

Inside North Star Learning Commons, Malik and Nia stared at Problem Twelve.

"The graph says the limit is negative infinity," Nia said.

"The graph suggests it. We still have to justify the sign."

"The function falls off the bottom of the page like it owes somebody money."

"That is not justification."

"It is visual evidence with personality."

Malik pointed to the denominator. "Approaching from the left makes this factor negative and extremely small. The numerator stays positive. Positive divided by a small negative number gives a large negative output."

Nia wrote his explanation beneath her sketch. "See? Teamwork. I provide vision. You provide bail money."

Their first limit set was due before the holiday weekend. Both had other places to be: Nia had plans with Julian around the Heritage Kickoff Classic, and Malik had an analytics data review that alternates were technically invited to observe.

Still, neither packed up when the last problem was finished.

Outside, the campus was warming into celebration. Inside, they checked each other's work one unnecessary time.

## Friday, August 29: The Hour They Do Not Name

Add-drop ended Friday, making everyone's schedule feel less like a proposal and more like a contract.

Nia submitted the limit set at 2:06. Malik submitted at 2:08 after correcting a comma in his written explanation.

"We are free," Nia said.

"We have reading for Tuesday."

"You are spiritually exhausting."

They carried their bags to the terrace outside North Star. Students crossed Cypress Walk in game shirts and sundresses, greeting relatives who had returned for the weekend. The air smelled like food trucks, cut grass, and rain that had not yet decided whether to fall.

Nia's phone lit up with a message from Julian: **Band call changed. Meet tomorrow at the stadium gate? I owe you the full tour.**

She smiled, then turned the screen facedown.

"Analytics?" she asked.

"Data review at four."

"Are you presenting the enormous work plan Simone told you not to build?"

Malik looked offended. "She did not tell me not to build it."

"She called it six weeks of work."

"That was an estimate."

"Malik."

"I reduced the scope."

"To five weeks?"

He almost laughed.

For one quiet hour, they sat on the terrace and talked about everything except where they were expected next. Nia described Keisha's first track conditioning session. Malik repeated DJ's complaint that special-teams meetings involved "a disrespectful quantity of sprinting." They debated which Copper Cup pastry was least likely to collapse in a backpack.

Neither said, *I wanted this afternoon with you.*

At three thirty, Nia stood. "We should go."

"Yes."

Neither moved for another minute.

## Saturday, August 30: Waiting for the Moment

Bellwether Civic Stadium, the neutral-site venue for the Heritage Kickoff Classic, filled hours before kickoff.

Indigo covered the stands. Copper flashed in pom-poms and band plumes. Alumni hugged across rows, children wore oversized heron wings, and the public-address announcer stretched every name until it sounded historic.

Julian met Nia at the gate in his Marching Herons uniform. He had ten minutes before report time and used nine of them explaining where she should sit, when the band would move, and which cues would be easy to miss.

"Watch the drum major after third down," he said. "If the defense gets a stop, we go immediately. If the offense converts, we hold."

"So the music is conditional."

"Everything is conditional."

"That is the most mathematical thing you have said voluntarily."

Julian lowered his voice. "Also, when the altos turn toward your section, I am absolutely playing to you."

"How will I know which one you are?"

He looked wounded. "Copper wrist tape. Handsome posture."

"That narrows it to at least twelve people."

He laughed and hurried toward the tunnel.

On the sideline, DJ waited.

He was dressed, taped, warmed up, and ready for a special-teams call that never came. Each time the return unit gathered, he moved closer to the coordinator. Each time, another name was called.

From the student section, Malik watched DJ bounce on his toes to stay loose. The crowd saw a player standing near the bench. Malik saw the 5:15 alarms, the route notes, the foam roller beneath the window, and the chart taped above DJ's desk.

McCall-Hart won by ten.

The stadium erupted. The band played. Nia screamed until her voice cracked and found Julian's copper wrist tape when the altos turned toward her section.

DJ celebrated with the team, helmet raised, smile visible from the stands.

Back in their room, he set the helmet on his desk and sat without turning on the light.

"You okay?" Malik asked.

"We won."

"That was not my question."

DJ gave him a tired look. "You have been waiting to use that on me."

"A little."

DJ leaned back. "I was ready. Nobody needed me to be."

Malik understood that feeling too well.

"They will," he said.

"You know that?"

"No."

DJ nodded. The honest answer helped more.

## Sunday, August 31: The Tendency

Sunday's analytics review opened with game data and closed, according to the agenda, after ninety minutes.

It did not close after ninety minutes.

The team was already preparing for the next opponent. Simone assigned sections of film and data while Malik sat at the end of the table with the other alternate, listening harder than some people spoke.

One chart showed fourth-down attempts by field position. Another separated play direction. A third listed personnel groupings.

Malik noticed that the opponent's apparent aggression near midfield changed when its tight-end-heavy package entered the game. On fourth-and-short, that package strongly predicted a run to the wide side, but the tendency disappeared inside the red zone.

He checked the sample size. Small, but not useless. He checked two seasons to see whether the pattern survived. It weakened but remained visible.

The room had emptied before he finished.

Malik drafted a message to Simone, deleted it, rebuilt the chart, and drafted again.

**Possible tendency, not a rule. On fourth-and-2 or less between the opponent 40 and our 35, heavy personnel has preceded a wide-side run in 8 of 10 available cases. Two clips have missing formation labels. I would use this as a film-review flag, not a standalone prediction.**

He attached the chart and sent it.

Three dots appeared almost immediately.

**Good limitation note. I will check it with Coach Bennett and football staff. Sleep.**

Malik looked at the time. 1:14 a.m.

He closed the laptop.

Then he reopened it to make sure the attachment had been correct.

## Monday, September 1: A Quiet Kind of Official

Labor Day closed the university, but nobody had informed the Marching Herons, the football staff, or the students who considered a free day an invitation to reorganize their entire lives.

Nia and Julian met near the closed radio booth after his morning rehearsal. The union was unusually quiet. Without the usual traffic, their footsteps echoed along the second-floor hall.

They had already spent Thursday evening at the kickoff festival and Saturday at the game. The date had stretched around practices and responsibilities instead of unfolding in one perfect block. Nia liked that. It felt less staged.

They walked outside to Cypress Walk, where rain from an early shower darkened the brick paths and left the leaves shining.

"Did you enjoy the game?" Julian asked.

"I understood at least forty percent more because of you."

"That is a passing grade in some departments."

"Not mine."

He smiled. "I saw you when we turned toward the student section."

"Copper wrist tape. You were right."

"About the handsome posture too?"

"The evidence was inconclusive."

They stopped near the side entrance to the union. Nia could hear the soft hum of the building's air system and a distant drumline practicing somewhere it was probably not supposed to.

Julian's confidence changed when the moment became private. He looked at her, then away, then back.

"Can I kiss you?" he asked.

Nia's heart performed something no limit law had prepared her to explain.

"Yes," she said.

The kiss was gentle, brief, and just awkward enough to be real. Julian's saxophone strap slid from his shoulder halfway through, and Nia caught the case before it hit the ground. They pulled apart laughing.

"Smooth," she said.

"The case is jealous."

When he kissed her again, they both knew where to put their hands.

## Tuesday, September 2: The Trial

Malik received Simone's message Tuesday morning while DJ was searching for a clean practice shirt.

**Your tendency helped staff flag the wide-side run during halftime review. They adjusted the edge alignment in the second half. Dr. Bennett approved a two-week trial seat for you. Meeting at 6.**

Malik read it once.

Then twice.

"Something happen?" DJ asked from inside a pile of laundry.

"I have a trial seat."

DJ emerged holding two shirts. "On analytics?"

"Two weeks."

DJ dropped both shirts and grabbed Malik by the shoulders. "My roommate is moving up in the world!"

"Please stop shaking me."

"Did your chart help us win?"

"It helped identify one second-half adjustment. Football is not a controlled experiment."

"So yes, but nerdy."

At the meeting, Simone placed Malik between two full members instead of at the end of the table.

"A trial seat is not a reward," she said quietly. "It is more responsibility with less certainty."

"Understood."

"And you do not have to bring a new discovery every day to justify the chair."

Malik opened his notebook. "Understood."

Simone looked unconvinced.

## Wednesday, September 3: The Hole in the Graph

Nia arrived at calculus on Wednesday with twelve unanswered club messages, two calendar conflicts, and the dreamy distraction of someone who had replayed a kiss often enough to edit its lighting.

Dr. Brooks gave the class a continuity problem. The function was piecewise, and students had to choose a missing constant so the graph joined without a break.

Nia understood the limit. She set the left and right expressions equal. Then she made an algebra error so basic that she did not notice it until Dr. Brooks returned the warm-up with a question mark beside the line.

"I distributed correctly," Nia whispered.

Amara leaned over. "You changed the sign here."

Nia stared at the page.

The mistake was not mysterious. It was an old weakness she had meant to review during the holiday. Instead, she had answered organization messages, adjusted volunteer schedules, helped draft a radio post, and floated through several hours thinking about Julian.

At North Star, Malik found her attacking algebra exercises with personal resentment.

"What happened?"

"I have been betrayed by negative signs."

He examined the paper. "This is review material."

"I know what it is."

He heard her tone and changed direction. "Do you want help or company?"

Nia looked up, surprised.

"Company," she said. "For ten minutes. Then help."

Malik sat across from her and opened his own work. He did not mention Julian. Nia was grateful, though she did not know whether the silence protected her or him.

## Thursday, September 4: Congratulations

Nia decided Malik should hear the news from her rather than from a photo, a rumor, or DJ performing an unauthorized relationship announcement in the dining hall.

They met beside Founders' Bell after class.

"Julian and I are seeing each other," she said. "Officially, I think. We have not filed anything with the registrar."

Malik's expression barely changed, but Nia knew him well enough now to notice the small stillness in his shoulders.

"Congratulations," he said.

"Thank you."

The space after those words became delicate.

Malik could have lied brightly. He could have acted as if the news meant nothing. Instead he looked down Cypress Walk, where students moved between classes beneath the indigo banners.

"I am happy you are happy," he said. "I may also need a little time to adjust."

Nia's chest tightened. "Adjust to what?"

He met her eyes. For one dangerous second, she thought he might answer fully.

"Our schedule," he said. "You have more commitments. I have the analytics trial. We should make sure the accountability check-ins still work."

It was true. It was not the whole truth.

Nia nodded slowly. "We can move them. Not cancel them."

"Agreed."

"And you can tell me if something is weird."

Malik gave a small, tired smile. "Something is weird."

She laughed, and the tension loosened without disappearing.

That week, Nia had received the relationship she wanted. Malik had received the chance he wanted. Both discoveries carried joy. Both also came with new conditions, hidden gaps, and no guarantee that the next step would be easier than the last.

In calculus, continuity meant the pieces met.

In life, meeting was only the beginning.
