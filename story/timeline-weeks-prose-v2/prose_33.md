# Week Thirty-Three: Spring Break, Old Angles

By Friday afternoon, McCall-Hart sounded abandoned.

Residence-hall doors slammed. Suitcases rolled over brick paths. Cars filled with students, laundry, snacks, and the confidence that every item would fit if somebody pushed hard enough.

Nia stayed.

So did Keisha and Julian. They had two days before Nia left for Huntsville, Keisha joined family outside Bellwether, and Julian traveled with the Marching Herons' postseason group.

The Porter Center lab felt enormous with most of campus gone. Their footsteps echoed. A vending machine offered six varieties of chips and rejected Julian's dollar twice.

"Spring-break luxury," he said.

"We have unrestricted access to the good whiteboard," Nia replied.

"Your standards are concerning."

They opened the presentation files.

## Friday, March 13: Two Days to Rebuild

The symposium version had to do three things at once: explain the mathematics, preserve uncertainty, and remain understandable to someone who had not lived inside the project since January.

Nia's narration ran four minutes too long. Keisha's live demonstration required six setup steps the audience would never see. Julian's animation moved beautifully and labeled nothing long enough to read.

They dismantled all three.

Nia cut historical background she loved but did not need. Keisha recorded a reproducible demonstration with a live fallback. Julian slowed the route transitions and added a visible source for every change.

At lunch, they ate vending-machine crackers and one questionable microwave pizza.

"This is what fellowship candidates eat?" Julian asked.

"The funding begins after selection," Nia said.

Keisha pointed at the pizza. "This may affect our survival to selection."

The work was tiring and unexpectedly fun. Without the pressure of ordinary classes, they argued through details, played music too loudly, and celebrated every removed slide as if subtraction were a sport.

Nia and Julian fell into an easy creative rhythm. He asked what the audience should feel. She translated the mathematical stakes. Keisha interrupted whenever either of them attempted to make uncertainty visually inconvenient.

Their old romance existed in the room as history, not instruction.

## Saturday, March 14: Elegant Models, Messy People

Julian recorded Nia's interview against a plain cream wall.

He asked her to describe the moment the project changed.

"Which one?" she said. "We have changed it every four business days."

"The deepest one."

Nia thought about the community interviews, the delayed baseball alert, and Keisha answering the reviewer more honestly than she had.

"I began with an elegant model of roads," she said. "Roads were easier than people. They had capacities and distances. Then people kept making choices the map did not contain. They waited for family. They trusted different sources. They understood the same message differently."

"So was the elegant model wrong?"

"Not useless. Incomplete. The danger was treating elegance like proof that we had included what mattered."

Julian remained behind the camera. "Does that happen outside mathematics?"

Nia looked directly at the lens.

They both understood the question.

"Yes," she said. "You can have a beautiful picture of a relationship. You can have a story about who two people are together. If the picture becomes more important than how they actually feel, the story stops helping."

Julian lowered the camera after the answer.

"That was about us," he said.

"Partly."

"Fair."

There was no accusation in his voice and no invitation in hers. They had made mistakes without making their entire relationship one.

Keisha returned with lunch and found them laughing because Julian had recorded the first half of the interview with a strip of tape visible on his sleeve.

She took a still image for the production log.

## Sunday, March 15: The Cropped Story

The still image reached Malik through a student-media post.

Nia and Julian sat close beneath the interview lights, laughing over the camera screen. The caption read: **Spring break does not stop symposium season.** Keisha was not visible. Neither were the consent sheets, route maps, or twelve hours of work surrounding the moment.

Malik looked at the image too long.

He knew the facts. Nia and Julian were collaborators. Keisha had been present. The project needed media work. Nia had directly said the relationship was over.

His jealousy arrived anyway.

It offered an old story: Nia and Julian had discovered that safe collaboration was actually reunion. Malik had moved forward only to learn that the possibility he abandoned had become available after he stopped looking.

He did not message Nia.

He did not ask Julian.

He also did not tell Simone what he was feeling.

Instead, he closed the post and carried the silence into their interview-practice call.

Simone noticed within three minutes.

"You are reading questions from a list," she said, "but you are not listening to the answers."

"I am tired."

That was true.

It was not the truth he owed her.

## Monday, March 16: The Cost of Saying Nothing

Spring break officially began Monday, and campus offices shifted to limited hours.

Malik joined Simone's practice session from Gulfport, where he was helping his parents review repair estimates. Simone called from Bellwether before leaving to visit family.

She answered a case question about detecting bias in a credit-risk model. Malik responded, "Good," and moved to the next question.

"What was good?" she asked.

"The structure."

"Which part?"

He could not say because he had barely heard it.

The image of Nia and Julian kept returning, followed by irritation at himself, followed by more distance. Simone had done nothing. Still, she was receiving the shortened answers, flat voice, and missing attention.

"We should stop," she said.

"We have six questions left."

"I am not an item on your schedule. Something is wrong, and you are making me work around it without telling me what it is."

Malik almost said family stress. His father's recovery and the repair estimates were real pressures. Using them to hide another feeling would make truth into cover.

"I saw a picture of Nia and Julian working together," he said. "I am jealous."

Simone went silent.

The confession came late enough that she had already paid for it.

"We will talk tomorrow," she said. "Not while you are still deciding whether to be honest."

She ended the call.

## Tuesday, March 17: The Direct Question

Simone called at noon.

"Are you jealous of Julian because you think Nia wants him," she asked, "or because you still want Nia?"

Malik had spent the morning constructing distinctions. Old feelings. Unresolved possibility. Fear of replacement. None relieved him of the answer.

"Both may be true," he said. "I do not know what Nia wants. I know I still have feelings I did not resolve."

"And yesterday you punished me for them."

"Yes."

He did not say he had not intended to. Intention would not return Simone's wasted practice session or remove the experience of being treated as the obstacle between him and another possibility.

"I chose this relationship," Malik said. "But I have been acting as if choosing should have erased everything else. It did not."

Simone looked hurt rather than surprised.

"I have felt you comparing," she said. "Not every day. Not in every room. But enough."

Malik wanted to deny it. He remembered the basketball game, his jealousy over Julian, and the private relief he sought whenever Nia seemed emotionally available.

"You are right."

"I do not need another apology that becomes a promise to control your feelings better. I need you to understand what relationship you are actually in."

"I will."

"That is still a future answer."

The call ended without resolution. Honesty had stopped the hiding. It had not made the truth harmless.

## Wednesday, March 18: Safe Because It Ended

Nia went home to Huntsville and found her mother reorganizing a kitchen cabinet that had survived perfectly well for years.

Denise Reynolds asked about the symposium, Keisha's track season, Malik's family, and Julian, in that order.

"Julian and I worked together all weekend," Nia said.

Her mother placed a stack of bowls on the counter. "How did that feel?"

"Safe."

Denise waited.

"Not safe like I want him back," Nia added. "Safe because I do not. We can remember what was good without testing whether it should restart."

"Does he know that?"

"Yes. I think so. We interviewed each other about elegant models and accidentally discussed our breakup on camera."

"That sounds like a terrible documentary."

Nia laughed. "The research part is better."

She explained that Julian had changed the animation so she was not the sole hero, that Keisha controlled the code decisions, and that the project had become stronger as Nia owned less of it alone.

"And Malik?" Denise asked.

Nia's smile faded. "He is with Simone. I care about him. I am respecting that."

"Respect is not only what you avoid doing," her mother said. "It is also refusing to build private hope out of somebody else's difficulty."

Nia thought of London.

"I know."

This time, she meant that she was practicing, not that she had mastered it.

## Thursday, March 19: Return With What Changed

Students began returning to McCall-Hart Thursday evening.

Nia carried a revised script. Keisha returned with a clean software build. Julian brought edited interview clips and a signed record of every participant's permissions.

The project was stronger because each person had protected a different truth.

Malik returned from Gulfport carrying repair documents and the knowledge that Simone no longer trusted his choice as completely as before. Simone returned with a stronger interview performance and a clearer understanding that London was not the only pressure on their relationship.

They met outside the Porter Center.

"How was home?" Malik asked.

"Good," Simone said. "How is your father?"

"Improving. The contractor thinks the back rooms may be finished by summer."

They exchanged facts because facts were safer than the conversation still waiting.

Across the courtyard, Nia and Julian carried equipment toward the lab. Malik saw them and made himself remain present.

He did not confront Nia. She had done nothing requiring explanation.

Nia noticed the strain between Malik and Simone but did not ask for private details. Their relationship was not an opening in her calendar.

Dr. Price sent the team its next itinerary: a Delta National Laboratory preview visit after break, with research sessions, communication workshops, and a joint review of symposium projects.

The trip would place Nia, Keisha, Julian, Malik, and Simone inside the same professional space.

They returned with a stronger project, a strained relationship, and old feelings stripped of their easiest excuses.

No confrontation came that night.

The silence was no longer empty. It was carrying everything the next trip would force them to name.
