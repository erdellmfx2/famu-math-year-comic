# Week Forty-Six: The Constraint Is Real

Dr. Okafor gave the class an evacuation problem with too few buses.

The goal was to maximize the number of people reaching safety. The constraints included bus capacity, travel time, staffing, fuel, road limits, and the requirement that no vehicle enter a flooded zone after a certain point.

Every desirable solution collided with something finite.

"Can we request more buses?" Nia asked.

"Not inside this problem."

"Can we extend the time?"

"The water declined to negotiate."

Using Lagrange multipliers, they searched for extrema while respecting the constraint surface. The method did not make scarcity fair. It made the tradeoffs visible enough to examine.

"Optimization does not mean receiving everything you want," Dr. Okafor said. "It means deciding honestly inside what is real."

Nia looked across the room at Malik.

They had constraints too.

## Friday, June 12: Safety With Too Few Resources

The bus problem refused every heroic shortcut.

Prioritizing the largest shelter left one neighborhood without transportation. Sending buses to the most isolated area increased total travel time. Waiting for perfect route data delayed departure.

Malik wrote the expected-risk objective. Nia added a minimum-access condition. Keisha tested whether the proposed schedule could physically run.

Their first answer violated the driver-hour constraint. Their second overloaded one pickup point. The third met every mathematical condition but depended on all passengers arriving exactly on time.

"Human beings have again rejected our schedule," Keisha said.

They added bounded arrival delays and produced a less efficient, more defensible plan.

The exercise prepared them for Saturday's community review. Their own model now proposed routes under limited time, uncertain data, and incomplete transportation access.

One route looked especially efficient.

The advisory group recognized a problem before the team finished describing it.

## Saturday, June 13: The Underpass Everyone Knows

The efficient route crossed the Linden Street underpass.

The model treated it as open because official road records listed it as passable under the simulated rainfall level. Residents rejected it immediately.

"That dip fills before the sensor says it should," Ms. Carter said.

Another advisor described two storms when water collected there despite moderate forecasts. A bus driver said large vehicles slowed approaching the narrow turn, creating backup even when the road remained technically open.

Nia checked the work behind the route. The local approximation was within its error bound. Expected travel improved. Access remained above threshold. Weeks of careful mathematics supported the recommendation.

The recommendation was still wrong for the community test.

"We need to remove the underpass," she said.

"Or represent its local failure history," Malik added.

They did both. The immediate scenario marked it unavailable. The long-term validation plan recorded the mismatch between official status, sensor behavior, and resident knowledge.

The mathematically efficient path had crossed a place the people using it already knew not to trust.

No amount of work invested in the route changed that fact.

## Sunday, June 14: Do Not Defend the Hours

Nia presented the correction to Dr. Okafor.

"The advisory group rejected the route," she said. "The underpass has a failure history our inputs do not represent."

She did not begin with how many hours the team had spent tuning the approximation. She did not argue that official data technically supported the choice. Work already completed was not evidence that a result deserved to survive.

Keisha updated the topology and attached a provenance note describing why. Malik proposed a reliability penalty for roads where local reports and official records consistently diverged, subject to validation rather than automatic acceptance.

Dr. Okafor approved the revision.

"What made this correction difficult?" she asked.

"The model behaved exactly as we designed it," Nia said. "The design was missing knowledge."

"And?"

Nia sighed. "I liked the route."

"There it is."

They preserved the rejected result in the audit log. The route would not disappear merely because it embarrassed the team. Future reviewers could see what changed and why.

Accepting correction did not require pretending the discarded work had taken no effort.

It required refusing to make effort the final judge.

## Monday, June 15: The Marker

Poster preparation stretched into evening.

Keisha left at eight for a remote developer meeting. Dr. Okafor had gone. Nia and Malik remained in the lab arranging the tradeoff curve, motion simulation, and local-approximation results.

One copper marker rolled beneath the table.

They both reached for it.

Their hands met. They looked at each other and laughed because the moment was so obviously written by a less subtle universe.

"We could use another color," Malik said.

"Copper is on brand."

Neither moved away.

The laughter faded. Malik's eyes dropped briefly to Nia's mouth. She noticed, then leaned closer.

For one suspended second, the empty lab, fluorescent lights, fellowship poster, deadlines, and months of missed timing narrowed into the distance between them.

Malik stopped.

He sat back and placed the marker on the table.

Nia's heart continued as if nobody had informed it of the decision.

"I am sorry," he said.

"Do not apologize for stopping."

They did not finish the conversation that night.

They finished the poster section and left before the almost-kiss could become a secret agreement.

## Tuesday, June 16: Outside the Fluorescent Light

Malik asked Nia to walk before work.

"I wanted to kiss you," he said.

"I was present."

"I stopped because I do not want our first decision to happen in an empty lab during a deadline."

Nia understood. Their strongest moments had often formed under pressure: exams, storms, presentations, family repairs. Crisis could make closeness feel inevitable because no one had time to ask what ordinary life required.

"What do you want instead?" she asked.

"One date. Planned as a date. No project materials. No pretending it accidentally happened because we worked late."

"After the Summer A exam."

"After Summer B begins, so the date is not part of surviving this deadline."

The delay felt both sensible and unbearable.

"Okay," Nia said.

They returned to Eliza Moss Hall separately. The almost-kiss remained real. It did not receive control of the schedule.

## Wednesday, June 17: Wanting Is Not Readiness

Nia told Malik what she wanted.

"I want you too," she said. "Not just a date. I have imagined the relationship."

Malik's expression softened.

"But wanting is not enough evidence that we are ready," she continued. "I wanted you while you were with Simone. You wanted me while I was with Julian. We have spent a year learning feelings can be honest and still produce bad timing."

"Agreed."

They named questions a date would not automatically answer. Could Malik disagree without withdrawing? Could Nia receive attention without turning it into proof? Could they maintain separate work? Could they talk about prior relationships without treating Julian and Simone as mistakes on the route toward each other?

"This is a very serious conversation about one bookstore date," Malik said.

"We have a documented history of under-modeling risk."

He laughed.

The humor helped. It did not reduce the standard.

They scheduled Sunday, June 28.

## Thursday, June 18: Put the Date After the Exam

The Summer A examination covered vectors, motion, partial derivatives, gradients, tangent planes, and constrained optimization.

Nia and Malik entered separately. The almost-kiss did not improve either person's understanding of Lagrange multipliers.

Malik checked each constraint before solving. Nia caught a boundary case before declaring an interior maximum. Keisha finished early enough to review every orientation sign in a cross-product problem.

Afterward, nobody turned the date into a reward for performance.

"Still June twenty-eighth?" Malik asked outside.

"Still June twenty-eighth."

"No laptops."

"No project talk."

"What if the bookstore has a mathematics section?"

"We walk past it with discipline."

The date entered both calendars.

Between them stood Juneteenth, the community festival, grades, project responsibilities, and ten days in which momentum would have to tolerate intention.

The constraint was real.

They chose inside it.
