# Week Seven: What the First Test Measures

By Friday night, DJ's forced fumble had acquired music.

The clip appeared on the stadium screen before McCall-Hart's home opener: number thirty-seven racing downfield, slipping past a blocker, striking the returner, and sending the football loose beneath the lights. Someone in campus media had slowed the moment of impact and added a bass drop dramatic enough to suggest DJ had saved the entire city of Bellwether.

The student section exploded.

Beside Malik, DJ's auntie screamed, "THAT IS MY BABY!" with enough force to create weather.

DJ stood on the sideline pretending not to look at the screen. The grin inside his helmet ruined the performance.

Nia waved from several rows below, where she sat near Keisha and the band section. Julian was already in formation with the Marching Herons, copper wrist tape visible beneath his glove.

Malik let the noise wash over him. For one clean moment, every difficult thing seemed to be moving in the right direction. DJ had earned recognition. Nia looked happy. Malik's corrected analytics chart had held up under team review, and Simone had asked him to attend a roster meeting Monday.

Tomorrow morning, he would take his first calculus test.

Tomorrow afternoon, analytics would review opponent data.

Both mattered. Malik had already decided he could fit them into the same day.

## Friday, September 12: The Highlight

McCall-Hart won the home opener under a storm-blue sky that never released its rain.

DJ played on two coverage units. He did not force another turnover, but he stayed in his lane, beat one block, and made a tackle that drew a sharp nod from the special-teams coordinator.

After the game, students crowded around him near the tunnel.

"Number Thirty-Seven!" somebody called.

"Fumble King!"

DJ pointed at Malik. "That man charted the returner's tendencies. Academic contribution."

"I did not," Malik said.

"He keeps me humble too. Full-service roommate."

Nia and Keisha joined them. Julian had a postgame band responsibility, but he sent Nia a selfie from the field, his uniform collar open and his face shining with sweat.

"Look at him," Nia said, showing Keisha.

Keisha glanced at the screen. "Yes. That is Julian. We have confirmed his identity."

"You are no fun."

"I ran intervals this morning. I used my fun already."

DJ replayed his highlight for the group even though all of them had just watched it on a screen forty feet wide.

Malik laughed with everyone else, but his mind kept counting backward from the analytics meeting. If the calculus test began at nine, he could finish by ten fifteen, cross campus, review his notes over lunch, and arrive at Porter Center before one.

The test window lasted two hours.

Malik planned to need seventy-five minutes.

## Saturday, September 13: The Time He Saves

The first page of the calculus test felt familiar.

Evaluate limits. Compare one-sided behavior. Identify discontinuities. Malik moved quickly, writing enough to satisfy himself and not enough to hear Dr. Brooks asking, *What claim are you making? What supports it?*

Halfway through, he checked the clock.

Forty-three minutes remained in the schedule he had created for himself. Ninety-eight minutes remained in the actual test window.

The next problem asked students to determine whether a two-sided limit existed and justify the conclusion from both sides. Malik wrote **DNE** and one line about unequal values.

On another problem, he used a limit law without stating why the required component limits existed.

He knew how to explain both answers. He could feel the complete arguments waiting just behind the shortened versions.

But the analytics meeting was across campus. A full roster decision might be coming. Every minute spent proving something he already knew felt like a minute taken from the opportunity he had not yet secured.

Malik turned in the test with thirty-one minutes left.

Dr. Brooks glanced at the clock, then at him. "Finished?"

"Yes."

"Or leaving?"

The question stopped him.

"Finished," he repeated.

At Porter Center, he arrived early.

The meeting did not begin for another twenty minutes.

Malik sat outside with his analytics notebook open and remembered the empty space beneath **DNE**. He told himself the answer was correct. He told himself correctness mattered most.

Then Simone arrived carrying coffee.

"You look upset."

"I am early."

"Those conditions can coexist."

He looked back at the notebook. "Test."

"How did it go?"

"Fine."

It was the first time he used the word that week.

It would not be the last.

## Sunday, September 14: Borrowed Strength

Nia left the test feeling lighter than she had entered.

She had slowed down. She checked the algebra that had embarrassed her the previous week. On the final graph problem, she explained that one side approached positive infinity while the other approached negative infinity, so the two-sided limit did not exist.

The explanation sounded clear because it was clear.

It also sounded like Amara.

During Sunday's study check-in, Nia found Amara at North Star.

"I used your sign explanation on the test," Nia said.

Amara looked alarmed. "The exact answer?"

"No! Not copied. I mean the way you separated the sides before naming the two-sided limit. It helped me see what I was doing."

"Oh." Amara smiled. "Good."

"I wanted to tell you because I keep thinking about that first workshop. I was so ready to show I understood that I nearly missed the best explanation in the group."

Amara closed her notebook. "You did miss it. Then you came back."

Nia nodded. "Still embarrassing."

"Maybe. But now you listen longer."

They worked through derivative rules together. Nia talked less than usual. Amara talked more.

Later, Nia texted Malik: **How are you feeling about the test now?**

His reply came after six minutes: **Fine. Analytics meeting tomorrow.**

Nia stared at the word.

Fine was Malik's favorite locked door.

## Monday, September 15: The Chair Is His

The analytics roster meeting lasted twelve minutes.

Simone sat across from Malik in the Quantitative Futures Lab. Dr. Bennett stood near the window, reading through Malik's revised chart notes one last time.

"The team has an open development seat," Simone said. "We would like you to take it for the remainder of the fall cycle. Full access, assigned deliverables, and travel eligibility if the team advances."

Malik kept his expression still. "Based on the tendency report?"

"Partly," Dr. Bennett said. "More because of the missing-data correction."

That surprised him.

Simone folded her hands. "Plenty of students can make a polished chart. Fewer can stand in front of a room, name what they did wrong, and rebuild the claim without pretending the mistake never happened."

"You will still need to manage your coursework," Dr. Bennett added. "A team seat is not permission to neglect the classes that make you useful to the team."

Malik heard the warning and thought of the test he had left early.

"Understood," he said.

Simone slid a team folder across the table. His name appeared on the roster beneath hers.

For the first time since arriving at McCall-Hart, Malik had been chosen for something he was not certain he could earn.

He called his parents from the Porter Center steps.

His mother answered on speaker. His father shouted congratulations from somewhere in the background, then asked whether analytics had benefits.

"Experience," Malik said.

"Experience is what people pay you in before money," Marcel replied.

Celeste laughed. "Let the boy enjoy it. We are proud of you."

Malik closed his eyes.

"How are classes?" she asked.

"Good."

The second lie arrived more easily.

## Tuesday, September 16: Sixty-Four

DJ celebrated Malik's full roster seat with grocery-store cupcakes and a handwritten sign taped above the television: **DATA MAN HAS BEEN ACTIVATED**.

"This font is hostile," Malik said.

"This font is joy."

They ate cupcakes from paper towels while DJ replayed the forced fumble and Malik pretended he had not already memorized the clip.

Then his laptop chimed.

**Calculus I: Test 1 grades posted.**

Malik opened the course page.

64.

For a moment, the number did not belong to him. It looked like a score attached to somebody who had forgotten to study, somebody who guessed, somebody whose parents had not just told the whole family that he made a college analytics team.

He opened the feedback.

Two limit arguments had lost nearly all available credit because the justifications were incomplete. A continuity problem contained a sign error he would have caught if he had checked. Dr. Brooks had written one sentence at the bottom: **Your answers show more understanding than your explanations reveal. Please come to office hours.**

DJ leaned over. "What did you get?"

Malik closed the screen.

"Not what I expected."

"Bad?"

"I know what happened."

"That was also not my question."

Malik stood and began clearing the cupcake wrappers. "I rushed. I can correct it."

DJ watched him turn celebration into cleanup.

"You want me to leave it alone?"

"Yes."

DJ did, though concern stayed in the room after the questions stopped.

## Wednesday, September 17: Fine

Nia caught Malik outside calculus.

"Scores posted," she said.

"I saw."

"How did you do?"

"Fine."

She searched his face. "Number fine or emotional fine?"

"I made avoidable mistakes."

"So did I. What number?"

Students moved around them toward the stairs. Dr. Brooks remained inside, beginning office hours in ten minutes.

Malik adjusted his laptop bag. "I have analytics work."

"Malik."

"It is handled."

He left before Nia could ask again.

At North Star, he opened the test correction form. It required students to identify each error, explain the corrected reasoning, and describe what habit would prevent the mistake next time.

Malik completed the mathematics quickly.

The habit question sat blank.

He knew the answer: he had treated time spent explaining as time wasted. He had valued arriving at analytics over showing what he understood. He had made a choice, not suffered an accident.

Dr. Brooks's office hours came and went.

Malik left the correction sheet beneath a stack of scrap paper and went to the lab.

## Thursday, September 18: The Goal and the Bad News

Nia found the correction sheet while gathering their notes after an accountability session Malik had left early.

She saw the score before she could decide not to.

64.

Below it, Malik's corrected arguments were careful and complete. The blank reflection box looked louder than the red marks.

She found him outside Eliza Moss Hall.

"You left this," she said.

Malik saw the paper in her hand and stopped.

"You said the test was fine."

"I said I made avoidable mistakes."

"You also said fine. More than once."

"It is my grade."

"Yes. And we agreed to be honest accountability partners."

"Accountability does not mean you are entitled to every number."

Nia flinched. "I know that."

Malik heard the hurt and wanted to take the sentence back. Shame had already made it sharp.

She lowered the paper. "You trust me with goals. You tell me what you are applying for, what team you want, what you plan to become. Why do you not trust me with bad news?"

He had no answer that did not expose more than the grade.

His phone rang.

**Mom**.

Malik almost silenced it. Then another alert covered the screen: **Gulf Coast storm system strengthens; watches possible.**

He answered.

Celeste did not begin with hello.

"Baby, your daddy is bringing things in from the yard," she said. Wind scraped across the connection. "The forecast shifted. They are saying we could get more water than they thought."

Malik looked at Nia. The correction sheet trembled slightly in her hand.

The calculus grade, so enormous one minute earlier, became one problem among several without becoming any less real.

"Tell me exactly what the weather service said," Malik told his mother.

He opened his laptop on the limestone steps, already reaching for numbers that might make the fear obey.
