# Week Fifty-One: Around the Boundary

Dr. Okafor drew a closed curve around a region.

Green's Theorem connected circulation around the boundary with derivatives accumulated across the interior. One problem could be described by what happened inside or by what happened around the edge.

"Two views," she said. "Equivalent under stated conditions. Not interchangeable without checking orientation, smoothness, and the region itself."

Nia saw the summer project in both views. Inside were code, metrics, interviews, tests, failures, and revisions. Around the boundary were the people encountering the dashboard, the agencies maintaining it, and the conditions under which it could leave the university.

A polished interior result meant little if the boundary excluded everyone needed to sustain it.

## Friday, July 17: Inside and Around

The class used Green's Theorem to convert difficult line integrals into double integrals and back again.

Keisha cared about orientation because one reversed boundary changed a flux sign. Malik cared about assumptions because holes in a region required attention. Nia cared that choosing the easier form did not change the physical interpretation.

For the Summer Scholars preliminary review, she structured the presentation around both interior work and boundary governance.

Keisha would demonstrate software and failure handling. Malik would explain risk and validation. Community advisors would respond before Nia concluded. Julian's media would appear only where permissions allowed.

Nia would not introduce every person as if their role became real through her voice. Each would speak from their own work.

The review would show a team, not a central hero surrounded by evidence.

## Saturday, July 18: The Review Belongs to More Than Nia

Keisha opened the preliminary review.

She demonstrated the time-step simulation, local approximation, data-lineage system, and missing-sensor handling. She intentionally triggered one failed input and showed the preserved log.

Malik followed with the two risk metrics, underdocumented loss categories, and the delayed-feed failure from the emergency drill.

Ms. Carter described how the advisory group had rejected the Linden Street route and changed the project's definition of usable access. A transit representative named the remaining private-vehicle bias.

Only then did Nia connect the pieces. She explained the mathematical evolution from static routes to motion, multivariable sensitivity, constrained optimization, and field-based exposure.

Questions went to the person responsible. Nia did not answer software questions over Keisha. Malik did not interpret community priorities for Ms. Carter.

The distributed structure made the project look less like one student's accomplishment.

It made the evidence stronger.

## Sunday, July 19: Who Maintains It After August?

A reviewer asked one question the team had not answered.

"Who maintains this after the students leave?"

The room became quiet.

The model depended on Keisha's code knowledge, Nia's interpretation, Malik's validation procedures, Julian's media workflow, and community relationships held partly through specific people. Documentation existed, but no outside maintainer had installed or updated the system independently.

"At present, we do," Keisha said. "That is a sustainability gap."

The reviewer asked what happened when a library changed, a data format shifted, or a consent restriction required media removal.

They had partial answers and no complete handoff process.

The project could perform a pilot and still fail the moment its creators graduated, changed courses, or simply became unavailable.

Nia added sustainability to the critical-risk list. She did not promise Keisha would keep supporting the code indefinitely.

Maintenance was not an invisible extension of co-leadership.

It was work requiring ownership, training, and resources.

## Monday, July 20: Keisha's Name on the Documentation

Keisha proposed an open documentation plan.

The public repository would include installation steps, dependency versions, data schemas, test scenarios, failure logs, and role-based maintenance guides. Sensitive community data would remain outside. Media permissions would have a removal process. A new maintainer would complete a reproducibility test before receiving pilot access.

She presented the plan at the student-developer forum under her own name.

Nia and Malik attended from the audience. Neither introduced her. Neither reframed the work as part of Nia's project or Malik's validation system.

Developers challenged Keisha on version support, security updates, and whether "open" documentation could expose system weaknesses. She answered directly and added a security-review step.

The forum invited her to lead a fall workshop on reproducible student research software.

Keisha accepted the invitation provisionally around track and course schedules.

Her technical leadership produced an opportunity belonging to her.

## Tuesday, July 21: Conditions on the Vote

The community advisory group voted to support a limited pilot.

The support came with conditions.

No personally identifiable interview data could enter the operational dashboard. Public reports had to list failures as well as successes. Route recommendations required visible timestamps and human review. Residents needed a process to challenge roads marked usable. The pilot license had to expire unless renewed after review.

The vote was not unanimous. One member abstained because transportation access remained underdeveloped. The team recorded the abstention and reason.

"Support does not mean you can quote us as endorsing the finished system," Ms. Carter said.

"We will call it a conditional limited pilot," Nia replied.

The wording mattered because public approval could become marketing if stripped of its limits.

Community authority remained inside the agreement, not merely inside the acknowledgments slide.

## Wednesday, July 22: Show Us the Failure Live

The Summer Scholars committee granted the team a showcase slot.

One condition surprised them: the committee wanted a live failure scenario.

No polished replay. During the showcase, Keisha would interrupt or corrupt one data source. The team would have to demonstrate detection, fallback behavior, and public explanation in real time.

"They want us to fail on purpose," Nia said.

"They want evidence that failure remains visible," Malik replied.

Keisha began designing a test no presenter could quietly control. Dr. Okafor would select the failure type from an approved set moments before the demonstration.

The showcase slot was not a reward for looking finished. It depended on proving that limits and errors could survive public pressure.

The team accepted.

## Thursday, July 23: Keep the Dinner

Friends celebrated the showcase slot at the student center.

DJ proposed banners. Imani proposed food. Keisha proposed that nobody touch her test environment before Friday. Julian brought a tiny cardboard trophy labeled **MOST TRANSPARENTLY IMPERFECT**.

Afterward, Nia and Malik had a private dinner scheduled.

The team wanted to continue planning the live failure test. Nia almost stayed. Malik almost offered to reschedule.

They did neither.

They trusted Keisha's work plan, closed their laptops, and went to dinner.

For ninety minutes, the project continued without consuming the relationship. They talked about second-year housing, family, books, and whether DJ's cardboard trophy violated visual standards.

The showcase mattered.

So did keeping an ordinary promise after the celebration ended.
