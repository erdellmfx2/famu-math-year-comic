# Week Twelve: The Maximum Is Not the Meaning

Dr. Alana Price opened her colloquium with a map and a warning.

The map showed a fictional river town under evacuation. Roads appeared as thin lines. Neighborhoods became colored regions. Small circles represented households, and arrows pointed toward shelters beyond the projected flood zone.

"Suppose I ask you to minimize total evacuation time," Dr. Price said. "What will your model try to do?"

Students offered answers: choose the shortest routes, reduce congestion, distribute traffic, avoid bottlenecks.

Dr. Price clicked to the next slide.

The fastest solution sent most drivers across one bridge, routed residents without cars to pickup points nearly two miles away, and treated a nursing facility like a cluster of identical households.

"The objective function improved," she said. "The evacuation became less humane."

Nia sat forward.

Only a week earlier, she had treated choosing one service project as proof that she understood limits. Then she had spent Cypress Week measuring connection by how many commitments she and Julian could squeeze around each other.

On screen, the route map glowed with an elegant answer to the wrong question.

## Friday, October 17: People Are Not Points

Dr. Price described the information an evacuation model might need beyond distance: vehicle access, mobility, language, medical support, departure timing, road capacity, trust in official messages, and whether a shelter could actually meet people's needs.

"Optimization does not remove values from a decision," she said. "It hides them inside what you choose to maximize, minimize, and constrain."

Malik wrote the sentence in his analytics notebook.

Nia drew three versions of the route map, each centered on a different goal. Fastest total clearance. Fewest households left without transportation. Most robust plan if one road failed.

During questions, she asked, "How do you keep adding human factors without making the model impossible to use?"

Dr. Price nodded. "You do not add everything. You make the choices visible. You involve the people affected. And you resist calling one output *the* optimum when it is only optimal under one set of priorities."

Afterward, Nia and Malik walked down Eliza Moss Hall's steps.

"That felt personal," Nia said.

"Because of the shuttle project?"

"Because I keep optimizing my schedule like every opportunity and every person can be converted into one measure called 'doing enough.'"

Malik looked at her. "That sounds tiring."

"It is. How is reduced analytics?"

"Also tiring. Less impossible."

"A powerful review. Put it on the brochure."

## Saturday, October 18: The Wrong Objective Function

Saturday's calculus workshop began with returned applications exams and the Mean Value Theorem.

Dr. Brooks drew a smooth curve between two endpoints and marked the secant line connecting them. Somewhere between the endpoints, she explained, the theorem guaranteed at least one tangent line with the same slope as that average rate, provided the function was continuous on the closed interval and differentiable inside it.

"The theorem guarantees existence," Amara said. "It does not tell us where the point is."

"Or whether there is only one," Malik added.

"Exactly," Dr. Brooks said. "Conditions matter. Conclusions have boundaries."

Their test-correction forms required more than replacing wrong answers. Students had to identify the first unsupported step. Nia had earned a strong score but lost points on an optimization problem because she found a critical point and never tested one feasible endpoint. Malik had improved from his first test, yet his linearization explanation still assumed that *nearby* automatically meant *accurate enough*.

They corrected the arguments at North Star. Nia wrote that a critical point was a candidate, not a crown. Malik added an explicit error check and the interval over which his approximation remained useful.

Only after finishing did Nia return to the larger optimization problem waiting in her room.

Nia spread her commitments across the dorm-room floor.

Class deadlines. Math outreach. AWM meetings. Accountability check-ins. Residence activities. Julian's band calendar. Julian's media calendar. The private study blocks Keisha had made her protect.

Keisha stepped around the paper without disturbing it. "Are we solving a crime?"

"Possibly mine."

Nia had been proud of choosing one service project. But the paper showed the deeper pattern. She did not merely say yes to too many events. She tried to maximize how useful she was inside each relationship.

If Malik struggled, she wanted to build the recovery plan. If a club needed a leader, she wanted to become the answer. If Julian was busy, she stretched herself around his schedule, then expected him to notice the stretching.

"I keep maximizing commitments," she said. "And minimizing attention."

Keisha sat on her bed. "Attention to what?"

"The work. The people. Myself. Pick one."

"That sounds like the same problem three times."

Nia placed a blank sheet in the center of the floor and wrote: **What is the goal?**

For academics: learn deeply, not appear endlessly capable.

For service: contribute where she had responsibility, not collect proof that she cared.

For Julian: build a relationship they could actually experience, not one that looked full on a calendar.

"Are you going to call him?" Keisha asked.

Nia looked at the last line. "Not until I know whether I am apologizing or trying to win the argument."

"That may be the smartest thing you have said all semester."

"Rude."

"Accurate."

## Sunday, October 19: Honest, Not Certain

Malik and Simone rebuilt the fourth-down model around intervals.

Instead of one bright recommendation, the interface displayed a range of expected outcomes under different assumptions. A broad neutral band showed scenarios where no option clearly dominated. Users could examine how the recommendation changed when possession value, weather, opponent strength, and sample quality changed.

"It is less dramatic," Malik said.

"Good," Simone replied. "We are submitting analysis, not auditioning for a game show."

The regional semifinal rubric rewarded technical quality, communication, and usefulness. A single answer might look stronger in a screenshot, but any judge could expose its fragility by changing one assumption.

Malik added a note explaining where formation tags remained incomplete.

"Do you think this can advance?" he asked.

Simone rotated her chair toward him. "Yes."

"How likely?"

"That is a different question."

"Do you have a range?"

"I have several opinions disguised as one."

He smiled.

They reviewed the limitations again. Malik no longer felt that every uncertainty label weakened his contribution. Some made the work stronger because they showed the user where judgment had to begin.

"You look more rested," Simone said.

"Seven hours last night."

"A revolutionary methodology."

"Results are preliminary."

Their laughter came easily. Neither treated it as more than the growing trust between teammates, though the warmth stayed with Malik after the screen went dark.

## Monday, October 20: No Camera

Julian invited Nia to dinner at a small table behind Copper Cup, away from the main terrace.

There was no camera. His phone stayed in his bag.

"I took the photo out of the homecoming recap," he said.

Nia had noticed. "You did not have to erase the whole moment."

"I know. But I posted it without asking, and after our argument I did not want the image speaking for us."

He looked tired in a way stage lights usually concealed.

"I am sorry," he said. "I turned our relationship into a closing shot because it made the week feel complete. The week was not complete. We barely saw each other."

Nia traced the edge of her napkin. "Thank you."

"I also used your being late to the showcase as evidence that I was allowed to be unavailable all homecoming. I kept score instead of saying I missed you."

"I did miss your film."

"You missed most of it."

"I am trying to apologize."

He smiled slightly. "Sorry. Continue."

Nia explained the review commitment and the moment she chose not to tell him. She admitted that she had believed the schedule would work because she wanted to be both the dependable tutor and the supportive girlfriend.

"Then when it failed," she said, "I treated being helpful as if it made the promise to you less real. It did not."

Julian reached across the table. "I do not want to compete with everybody who needs you."

"You should not have to."

They held hands in the quiet corner, relieved to be speaking honestly and not yet aware that honesty could identify a mismatch without resolving it.

## Tuesday, October 21: One Evening

Nia and Julian met again Tuesday to make the repair practical.

They chose one private evening each week. No media coverage, club leadership, tutoring, band extras, or relationship posts without discussion. Emergencies could change the plan, but ordinary busyness could not quietly consume it.

"Thursday?" Julian asked.

Nia checked her schedule. "Thursday. Seven to nine."

"Protected."

"Protected."

Then Nia added her own apology.

"I have been acting like love means endless flexibility," she said. "If I move everything for you, I feel devoted. If you move everything for me, I feel chosen. That is not fair to either of us."

Julian considered her words. "I like that you show up."

"I like that you make moments feel important."

"Those are good things."

"Yes. Until they become the only way we know how to care."

He nodded, though the answer seemed more complicated than the calendar block they had just created.

They walked Cypress Walk together without taking a picture.

For the first time since homecoming, the relationship felt quiet enough to hear.

## Wednesday, October 22: Submit

The Heron Analytics regional submission was due at 8:00 p.m.

At 6:15, Andre found a mislabeled formation. At 6:42, Priya corrected a legend. At 7:03, Dr. Bennett removed a paragraph that claimed more transferability than the evidence supported. At 7:21, the export broke one of Simone's interval labels.

Nobody panicked gracefully.

Malik rebuilt the label. Simone checked the final narrative against the charts. Andre and Priya verified the source table. Dr. Bennett read the limitations aloud.

At 7:47, Simone placed the cursor over **Submit**.

"Last objections?"

Malik looked at the model. It did not promise the best fourth-down answer. It showed when evidence favored an option, when the range widened, and when a coach should treat the model as one input among several.

It was honest.

It was competitive.

It might lose.

"Submit," he said.

Simone clicked.

The confirmation page appeared.

For three seconds, the room remained silent. Then Andre cheered, Priya dropped her head onto the table, and Simone raised both hands as if surrendering to the deadline.

Malik took a picture of the team, not the model.

He sent it to his parents with the message: **Regional submission complete. Team effort.**

Celeste replied with six celebration symbols. Marcel wrote: **Proud of you. Call when you can. Repair update not good.**

Joy and worry occupied the same screen.

## Thursday, October 23: A Larger Map

Dr. Price's email arrived Thursday morning.

**DELTA NATIONAL LABORATORY STUDENT TOUR: APPLICATIONS OPEN**

The visit offered a limited number of seats for students interested in applied mathematics, computing, risk, energy, and environmental modeling. Applicants needed a short statement explaining what question they hoped to explore.

Nia found Malik outside calculus reading the message.

"Are you applying?" she asked.

"Yes."

"That was fast."

"The lab studies risk modeling."

"It also has scientific visualization and community systems."

They looked at each other.

"Accountability application session?" Nia asked.

"Saturday. Two hours. No disappearing into other commitments."

"You have become aggressive about constraints."

"Evidence supports them."

They agreed to apply separately and review each other's statements only after drafting their own.

Malik wanted to ask how things were with Julian. Nia wanted to ask what Marcel's repair update meant. Both sensed the other question and let it wait until they had more privacy.

Ahead of them, the Delta visit offered a wider world: supercomputers, scientists, models connected to consequences larger than a classroom or a football field.

Behind them, the house repairs had stalled, and one protected evening could not prove a relationship was healed.

The next opportunity did not erase the current problems.

It gave them a larger map on which to carry them.
