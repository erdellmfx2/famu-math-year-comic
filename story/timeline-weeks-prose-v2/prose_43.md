# Week Forty-Three: Motion in the Real World

The first resident rejected both of the team's routes.

Nia placed the printed map on a folding table inside the Bellwether neighborhood center. One route minimized expected travel time. The other protected access for the area with the fewest alternatives.

"I would start here," the resident said, pointing to neither line. "Then if water reaches the service road, I turn north. If traffic backs up by the school, I call my daughter and use the church lot."

"So you would change routes during the trip?" Nia asked.

The woman looked at her. "Wouldn't you?"

The model assumed a household selected one path and followed it to safety.

People did not.

They watched water, traffic, fuel, messages, children, neighbors, and one another. Their routes moved because the world moved.

## Friday, May 22: The Fixed Path Breaks

The approved interview session used large maps, removable route markers, and no recording until participants made separate choices about consent.

One resident changed routes after hearing a bridge had closed. Another avoided a road long before official closure because she knew where water collected. A parent described beginning toward one shelter, then turning when a family group chat reported that it was full.

The team's fixed-path assumption failed repeatedly.

Malik asked what made participants abandon an initial route. Answers included visible water, stalled traffic, trusted phone calls, low fuel, police direction, and fear based on previous storms.

Keisha documented decision points instead of forcing every story into one category. Nia moved markers across the map as people spoke.

"Your model thinks the route is a promise," an older man said. "A route is what I am doing until I learn better."

Nia wrote the sentence down.

At the end, the two-metric display still mattered. Expected travel and minimum access shaped choices. But neither metric described motion if the system evaluated only the starting decision.

The model needed a path that could change over time without pretending every change happened instantly.

## Saturday, May 23: Position as a Function of Time

In Calculus III, Dr. Okafor represented motion with vector-valued functions.

Position depended on time. Differentiation produced velocity. Differentiating velocity produced acceleration. Curvature described how sharply a path turned.

Nia saw the interviews inside the notation.

A household's location could become a vector function. Traffic and warnings could update desired velocity. Route changes could alter direction. The model should not ask only which path was shortest or most accessible. It should ask how movement evolved as conditions changed.

She brought the redesign to Keisha and Malik.

Keisha divided the simulation into time steps. Malik proposed risk updates tied to new road and warning data. Nia defined position, direction, and route-choice states.

"We cannot let a car jump from one road to another because the recommendation changes," Keisha said.

"Physical continuity," Malik replied.

"And human delay," Nia added. "People need time to receive, trust, and act on an update."

Their static map became a motion model.

It was more complicated, slower, and closer to the decisions residents had actually described.

## Sunday, May 24: Why Averages Make Him Nervous

The community advisory group reviewed the redesign.

Malik explained the two-metric display and why expected travel time could hide severe local harm. A member asked why he cared so much about losses that averages did not capture.

For several seconds, Malik considered giving an academic answer.

Then he told the team about Gulfport.

He described water entering the Baptiste home, Marcel's injury, the insurance documentation, and the months during which rooms remained unusable. The claim recorded damaged property more easily than lost work, disrupted sleep, family strain, or the cost of living around repairs.

"Our documented loss was not our total loss," he said. "That is why a clean average makes me nervous. It can be mathematically accurate and still reward whatever was easiest to record."

Nia had heard pieces privately. Keisha knew the storm had affected his family. The advisory group had not known any of it.

Malik did not display photographs. His parents had not approved that use.

One resident thanked him, then challenged him. "Your experience can help you ask a question. It cannot answer for every family."

"Agreed," Malik said.

He wrote the correction into the meeting notes himself.

Personal stake had entered the project as context, not authority.

## Monday, May 25: A Day Not Available for Data

McCall-Hart closed for Memorial Day.

The team had originally marked the day as an optional interview opportunity because many residents would be off work. The advisory group declined.

Instead, the neighborhood hosted a remembrance event honoring military service members and local residents lost during the year. Families brought photographs. Volunteers placed flags and flowers near a temporary memorial. Names were read without turning the ceremony into university programming.

Nia, Keisha, Malik, Julian, DJ, and Imani attended as participants and volunteers. They set up chairs, served water, and packed supplies afterward.

No clip entered the documentary. No quote entered the dataset. Julian left his recording equipment zipped inside its case.

When a resident began discussing storm evacuation with Malik, he listened but did not open a consent form.

The holiday was not spare capacity in the research calendar.

Pausing data collection showed that community life did not exist to feed the project. Some gatherings deserved presence without extraction.

## Tuesday, May 26: Cars Cannot Teleport

Keisha's first motion simulation looked impressive until twelve hundred vehicles changed direction in the same second.

Onscreen, cars leaped across lanes, accelerated beyond physical possibility, and entered side roads as if traffic laws had been suspended by mathematics.

DJ watched the replay. "I do not drive often, but I believe that is illegal."

"It is computational enthusiasm," Keisha said.

She calculated acceleration whenever the model issued a new route recommendation. Sudden updates produced impossible jumps in speed and direction. The system optimized where vehicles should go without modeling how they could physically get there.

Nia added decision delay. Malik added congestion costs for simultaneous rerouting. Keisha constrained acceleration and turn rates. Updates now spread across time rather than commanding instant obedience.

The new simulation produced a less dramatic improvement in travel time.

It also stopped teleporting cars through buildings.

"A difficult tradeoff," DJ said.

Keisha removed his guest access to commentary.

## Wednesday, May 27: Permission Before the Microphone

Julian returned for an audio session with the community advisory group.

Before recording, he presented four choices. Participants could allow identifiable audio, anonymous edited audio, written quotation only, or no media use. Research participation did not require media permission. A person could change the media choice later without withdrawing the entire interview.

One participant allowed written notes but not her voice. Another approved audio only for community workshops, not public social media. A third wanted to review the final clip before use.

Julian recorded each restriction with the file, not in a separate memory-based list.

Nia asked whether the extra steps slowed production.

"Yes," he said. "Consent is allowed to take time."

The final clips were fewer than he hoped and more trustworthy than a larger collection would have been.

His spring role had taught him that making work public created responsibility beyond visual quality. Summer made that responsibility operational.

Every voice kept its owner's terms.

## Thursday, May 28: Tell the Team, Not Only Me

Nia thanked Malik after the advisory meeting.

"For talking about your family," she said. "I know that was not easy."

They stood outside Eliza Moss Hall in late afternoon heat.

"I used to think telling you was the same as being honest," Malik said. "It was not."

Nia felt the weight of the distinction.

During the fall, Malik had let her see pieces of his fear while hiding them from teammates, faculty, and sometimes his family. Private disclosure had made their bond feel special, but it had also concentrated responsibility in one relationship.

"Trust now means telling the people who need the information," he continued. "Not only the person whose understanding matters most to me."

He did not take back the final phrase. Nia did not turn it into a romantic decision.

"The team needed to know why the metric mattered," she said.

"And they needed permission to challenge my interpretation."

The next test would happen at Delta National Laboratory, where a larger road network could expose failures no private understanding would fix.

They entered Eliza Moss Hall together and joined Keisha before the meeting began.
