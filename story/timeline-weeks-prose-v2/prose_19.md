# Week Nineteen: Everything That Counts

On the last day of Calculus I, Dr. Brooks placed two papers on Malik's desk.

The first was his corrected test from September. The original score, sixty-four, remained visible in red at the top. Beneath it, the correction credit and comments documented what he had repaired.

The second paper was recent work on the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. Malik had built the accumulation function, connected its derivative to the original rate, interpreted the units, and stated the conditions supporting the argument.

Dr. Brooks tapped both pages.

"Do not compare only the numbers," she said. "Compare the reasoning."

Malik did.

The corrected first test showed understanding added after consequence. The recent work showed understanding made visible the first time. It was not flawless. He had lost points for skipping one endpoint interpretation. But the explanation no longer hid behind the answer.

"You improved," Dr. Brooks said. "That does not mean the difficult beginning was secretly good for you. It means you responded to it."

Malik looked at the sixty-four one last time.

The grade still belonged to him. So did everything he had done after it.

## Friday, December 5: Last Class

Fall classes ended with no dramatic speech.

Dr. Brooks reviewed final-exam expectations, reminded students that justification mattered, and asked them to write one concept they understood differently now.

Nia wrote: **A complete explanation leaves room for other people to enter it.**

Malik wrote: **An answer is not complete if its assumptions remain private.**

Amara wrote longer than either of them and covered her response with one hand when Nia tried to peek.

After class, students lingered in Eliza Moss Hall. Some exchanged study times. Others photographed the board before it was erased. The room where Malik and Nia had first argued over limits now held months of accumulated evidence: stress, repair, missed sleep, better questions, and friendships that had survived correction.

"Championship tomorrow," DJ announced when Malik returned to the residence hall. His game bag was packed with enough precision to suggest Malik's influence or panic.

"Finals Monday," Malik replied.

"Let joy have one full sentence."

Malik looked at him. "Championship tomorrow."

"Thank you."

## Saturday, December 6: The Catch Before the Score

The Southern Collegiate Heritage Conference championship stayed close into the fourth quarter.

McCall-Hart trailed by three when the offense faced third-and-eight. DJ lined up in the slot. The defense disguised pressure, then dropped a linebacker beneath his route.

DJ adjusted without abandoning the pattern. He settled in the narrow space behind the linebacker and in front of the safety. The quarterback delivered the ball high.

DJ caught it, absorbed a hit, and held on.

Nine yards. First down.

The catch did not appear on the final scoring graphic. Four plays later, another receiver caught the touchdown. The defense protected the lead, and McCall-Hart won the conference championship by four.

DJ sprinted onto the field with everyone else. Indigo confetti caught in his hair. Imani reached the front rail and screamed his name. Malik, Nia, Keisha, Simone, Amara, and half the analytics team celebrated from the stands.

During the trophy ceremony, cameras focused on captains and senior stars. DJ stood behind them, smiling so hard his face hurt.

He had made the River Classic catch. He had made the third-down catch that kept the championship drive alive. He had not scored the final touchdown.

He did not need to.

In the locker room, the receivers coach found him.

"Good feel against the zone," he said. "And you secured the ball."

DJ nodded solemnly. "I have experienced growth."

The coach stared at him.

"Yes, sir," DJ corrected.

## Sunday, December 7: Leave Before the Song

Campus celebrated the championship through Sunday.

Nia joined Keisha, DJ, Imani, and friends at Hart Student Union. She wore an indigo championship shirt over a long-sleeved top and let herself enjoy the uncomplicated part: DJ's grin, alumni dancing, the trophy reflecting Copper Cup lights.

Julian arrived with band members before their gathering upstairs.

He and Nia saw each other across the terrace. He raised one hand. She returned the gesture.

For a moment, she imagined staying. The band gathering would have music she knew, people who still thought of them together, and the familiar possibility of standing beside Julian until the breakup blurred.

Keisha noticed her watching.

"Want to go?"

Nia nodded. "Before I turn missing him into a reason."

She congratulated DJ again, hugged Imani, and left with Keisha before the band moved upstairs.

Walking away did not feel powerful. It felt sad and responsible.

Imani caught them at the bottom of the union steps.

"You leaving because you want to," she asked Nia, "or because you think being healed means never sharing a room with him?"

Nia looked back through the glass. Julian was laughing with two band members, unaware of the conversation.

"I can share a room with him," she said. "I did at the farewell performance. Tonight everybody is celebrating, people are taking pictures, and I know exactly how easy it would be to stand beside him until the old version feels available."

"So this is strategy, not fear."

"Tonight, yes. Ask me again next month."

Imani nodded. "Good answer. Healing is not attending every event to prove you can survive it."

Keisha linked her arm through Nia's. They walked toward the residence hall and bought convenience-store ice cream on the way. The night became smaller than the championship celebration and more suited to what Nia could honestly carry.

Behind them, the first song began.

Nia did not turn around.

## Monday, December 8: One Argument

Final examinations began Monday.

The Calculus I final required more than separate techniques. One long problem displayed a graph of a function, asked students to analyze its derivative, and then interpret accumulated signed area over an interval.

Students had to connect increasing and decreasing behavior, critical points, concavity evidence, net change, and units in one argument.

During the review briefing, Dr. Brooks had warned them: "A graph, a derivative, and an integral are not three unrelated chapters. They are three ways of asking what changes, how it changes, and what accumulates."

The exam schedule divided sections across several days. Malik's appointment was Tuesday morning. Nia's was Wednesday.

On Monday night, they reviewed separately.

Malik used the map they had built but stopped at ten thirty. Nia redrew regions until the integration bounds felt visible. Neither called the other for reassurance.

At 9:48, Malik texted: **Sleep is part of test preparation. Evidence remains annoying.**

Nia replied: **Agreed. Phone going across room. Good luck tomorrow.**

They honored the message.

## Tuesday, December 9: Leave One Blank

Malik entered the final with a rule: no answer without the reasoning that made it defensible.

He moved more slowly than he had on the first test. He stated one-sided behavior. He checked derivative signs. He wrote what accumulated area meant in context instead of leaving a number alone.

With twenty-two minutes left, he reached a proof-style question he did not fully know how to finish.

The old panic returned. One unfinished part threatened to turn the entire exam into evidence of failure.

Malik read the prompt again. He wrote the conditions he knew, established the first implication, and marked the step he could not justify instead of inventing one.

Then he used the remaining time to check every complete argument.

When the exam ended, the proof remained unfinished.

So did one era of Malik's thinking.

He had not rushed the rest of the work to attack one blank. He had not made confidence perform where understanding ended.

Outside, Dr. Brooks collected his paper.

"How was it?" she asked.

Malik considered the automatic answer.

"Mostly strong," he said. "One part incomplete."

"That sounds like information."

"I am trying to treat it that way."

## Wednesday, December 10: Redraw the Region

Nia froze on the integration setup.

Two curves enclosed a region but exchanged order after their intersection. She knew the process. Find the intersection, identify top and bottom, split the integral, evaluate.

Her mind supplied nothing.

For several minutes, she stared at the equations as if they belonged to a course she had never taken.

Then she stopped trying to remember the finished setup.

She redrew the region.

The first sketch was wrong. She marked test points and corrected it. The intersection became visible. So did the change in which curve lay above the other.

Nia built two integrals.

She checked the bounds and continued.

No glance toward Malik. No imagined explanation from Julian. No need to become the fastest voice in the room. She used the remaining time and the tools she had practiced.

When the exam ended, Nia walked outside and leaned against the limestone wall.

Keisha waited with two snacks.

"How bad?"

"I froze. Then I drew. Then I recovered."

"So which snack?"

"Both. Recovery is hungry work."

## Thursday, December 11: The Only Promises

After the final, Malik and Nia met beneath Founders' Bell.

The semester had begun with them standing near the same place and agreeing to become accountability partners. Now students rolled suitcases down Cypress Walk while residence halls prepared to close.

"How did you do?" Nia asked.

"Mostly strong. One incomplete proof."

"I froze on area between curves. Recovered after redrawing."

Neither inflated the answer. Neither softened it to protect the other.

"What now?" Nia asked.

Malik thought of Gulfport, stripped walls, insurance documents, and a living room that would become his bedroom. Nia thought of Huntsville, family questions, and the first holiday season without Julian in her phone.

"Get home safely," Malik said.

"Tell the truth when grades post."

"Agreed."

No promise to call every day. No confession timed to the end of calculus. No claim that surviving one semester had made them ready for every next step.

They hugged, wished each other safe travel, and walked away carrying everything that counted, including the things no grade would measure.
