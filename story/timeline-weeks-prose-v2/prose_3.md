# Week Three: Rooms, Rules, and First Impressions

Malik had drawn the room before he moved into it.

Not artistically. He had used the residence hall's posted dimensions to make a scale diagram, then tested three furniture arrangements. Layout B gave each roommate equal desk access, kept the vent unobstructed, and created a clear walking path from the door to both beds.

Layout B survived for eleven seconds.

"Television goes here," DJ announced, setting a large box directly in the planned walking path.

Behind him came two duffel bags, a laundry basket, a foam roller, three pairs of football cleats, and an auntie carrying enough snacks to provision the floor through midterms.

Malik looked from the television to his diagram.

DJ looked at the diagram. "Is that our room?"

"It was."

"Why does it look like a tiny courthouse?"

"It is to scale."

"Where is the joy to scale?"

DJ's auntie laughed, handed Malik a container of lemon cookies, and told him he was now responsible for making sure her nephew attended class. Before Malik could object, she hugged both of them and joined the stream of families filling the hall.

DJ picked up a cookie. "Congratulations. You have been hired."

"I did not accept."

"Family appointment. Very powerful."

Malik moved the television box six inches so the door could close.

Early move-in for Bridge students and athletes had begun. The rest of the first-year class would arrive Monday.

## Friday, August 15: Layout B

By noon, Malik understood that sharing a room meant surrendering the fantasy that every object would remain where logic placed it.

DJ's football schedule appeared on the wall above his bed. His alarm sounded at 5:15 each morning, including Saturdays. He needed space to review film, stretch, and store team gear. He also received visitors with the casual frequency of a campaign office.

Malik needed quiet, a reliable sleep schedule, and surfaces that did not become permanent clothing habitats.

They negotiated.

"Headphones after eleven," Malik said.

"Unless it is game film."

"Game film also produces sound."

"Educational sound."

"Headphones."

DJ pointed to the empty section of floor in Malik's diagram. "Television stand here. Both desks on that wall."

"That blocks part of the window."

"Sunlight has had the planet for billions of years. It can share."

They eventually built Layout D, a compromise that existed nowhere in Malik's original file. The desks sat side by side, the television remained visible from both beds, and a narrow strip near the window became DJ's stretching area.

Malik hated how well it worked.

That evening, DJ returned from a team meeting and found labels on two shelves.

"Did you assign me storage?"

"I assigned both of us storage."

"Malik, my brother, we are going to get you through college."

"That is why I came."

DJ grinned. "See? Dry jokes. I knew you had range."

## Saturday, August 16: The Six-Club Emergency

Across campus, Nia's new roommate was saving her from herself.

Keisha Morgan arrived with one suitcase, a desktop computer packed like medical equipment, and a wall calendar showing class blocks, track practices, coding time, and exactly two blank evenings each week.

Nia arrived with four bags, a plant named Katherine Johnson, and a stack of organization flyers collected at Indigo Night.

"How many of those did you join?" Keisha asked.

"Join is a strong word."

"How many gave you a signup confirmation?"

Nia counted. "Six."

Keisha held out her hand. "Phone."

"Why?"

"Because this is an intervention."

Keisha was a first-year computer science major and sprinter from Mobile. She spoke with the calm efficiency of someone who knew exactly how long 200 meters felt at full speed and had no interest in wasting energy elsewhere.

Together they reviewed Nia's list: Association for Women in Mathematics, Math Circle, service council, campus radio volunteers, residence programming board, and a general first-year leadership group.

"You also have Bridge, classes starting soon, and whatever is happening with Saxophone Man," Keisha said.

"His name is Julian."

"That sounds ongoing."

Nia moved campus radio into the maybe column. "I just do not want to miss the thing that could matter."

Keisha sat on the edge of her bed. "Everything matters to somebody. That does not make all of it yours."

The sentence quieted the room.

Nia crossed out two commitments and postponed a third. It felt less like freedom than failure.

Keisha handed back the phone. "You did not close doors. You chose which room to stand in first."

"Do you always talk like a very fast life coach?"

"Only during emergencies."

Nia looked at the reduced list. "Six clubs counts as an emergency?"

"Six clubs before classes absolutely counts."

## Sunday, August 17: The Missing Riders

The Bridge capstone began with a problem that looked made for Malik.

Move-in traffic had increased wait times for McCall-Hart's campus shuttles. Student teams had to estimate demand and recommend temporary routes. The packet included residence-hall counts, parking information, shuttle capacity, and scheduled arrival windows.

Malik built a demand table. Nia sketched the campus. They had practiced combining those methods, and for the first hour they moved with satisfying speed.

Their model sent extra shuttle service to the largest residence halls during the busiest arrival windows. It minimized average wait time and stayed within the available driver hours.

"We are good," Malik said.

Nia traced the route with a copper marker. "We are suspiciously good."

During the preliminary review, another Bridge student named Tasha studied their map.

"Where is the accessible stop for Douglass Hall?" she asked.

Malik pointed to the nearest marked stop.

"That path has stairs."

He looked at the map again.

Tasha used a forearm crutch. She tapped the western side of the building. "The ramp entrance is back here. Your shuttle drops people on the opposite side."

Nia lowered the marker.

Their optimization had treated every route between a stop and a residence hall as equally usable. The average wait looked excellent because the model did not count the extra distance, slope, or barriers faced by some students.

"We did it again," Nia said quietly.

Malik knew what she meant: the food boxes, the tutoring requests, the habit of letting missing context hide outside clean columns.

"The packet did not include accessibility data," he said.

Tasha's expression changed.

Nia looked at Malik. "Then we should have asked."

He heard accusation, even though she was accusing both of them.

"We modeled the information provided."

"And called ourselves done."

Malik closed the laptop harder than necessary. "If you knew the map was incomplete, you could have said so before review."

"I didn't know either. That is the point."

They had built the best-looking first draft in the room. It was also the first draft that embarrassed them most.

## Monday, August 18: Ask Before Defending

Residence halls officially opened Monday, bringing vans, trailers, grandparents, coolers, lamps, rugs, and several impossible attempts to fit queen-size mattresses into twin-size spaces.

Malik and Nia spent the busiest part of move-in outside with clipboards.

Dr. Brooks had given every team a choice: defend the assumptions in the original model or collect better information.

For once, Malik did not need long to decide.

They interviewed students, family members, drivers, and residence staff. They timed routes instead of estimating from the map. Tasha showed them the accessible entrance and identified two curb cuts that were technically present but difficult to use when unloading vehicles blocked them.

A parent pushing a cart explained that the nearest stop was not always the easiest stop. A student carrying an instrument could walk farther but boarded more slowly. A residence assistant pointed out that demand surged after families returned from shopping, not only during assigned arrival windows.

The problem became messier with every answer.

It also became real.

Back in Eliza Moss Hall, Malik added route penalties for stairs, steep slopes, and blocked access. Nia revised the campus diagram to distinguish shortest distance from usable distance. They recommended a temporary west-side stop, an accessible shuttle loop, and two flexible driver periods based on observed demand.

"Average wait goes up in our revised model," Malik said.

"Because we are counting more of the actual waiting."

He nodded. "The first number was better because it was less honest."

Nia looked at him. "Can I put that in the presentation?"

"No."

"Can I put a more professional version?"

"Yes."

She wrote: **A lower reported average can conceal unmeasured access costs.**

Malik read it twice. "That is better."

"We are better," she said.

This time, he let her celebrate.

## Tuesday, August 19: Through Julian's Lens

Julian arrived at their capstone rehearsal carrying a camera and an apology.

"Campus media wants a short Bridge feature," he said. "Dr. Brooks said I could film if the team agrees. If anybody says no, the camera stays in the bag."

Malik appreciated the clarity. Nia appreciated the camera angle Julian found near the route map.

During the interview, Julian asked why their model had changed.

Nia began with the missing accessibility information, but she spoke so quickly that the answer tangled itself.

Malik stepped in. "Our first model optimized the experience we had measured. We mistook that for everyone's experience. Interviews changed the variables and the recommendation."

Julian lowered the camera slightly. "That is the line. Say it again."

Malik did, less comfortably the second time.

After filming, Julian asked Nia if she wanted to attend the Heritage Kickoff with him after the first week of classes. This time, she checked her planner.

He noticed. "Should I be nervous?"

"No. This is personal growth."

She had one evening open.

"Yes," she said. "I would like that."

Julian smiled, and the room seemed to brighten around him.

Malik pretended to adjust a chart that was already straight.

## Wednesday, August 20: One Bad Question

The Heron Analytics screening test included data interpretation, probability, short calculations, and one written response about model limitations.

Malik moved confidently through the first section. He checked his units. He marked assumptions. He even forced himself to leave one question and return later instead of wrestling it into submission.

Then he reached the probability problem.

A team could choose between two fourth-down strategies under changing field conditions. Malik calculated the expected outcomes, reconsidered whether the events were independent, erased half a page, and watched twelve minutes disappear.

When time was called, his final answer sat in a box without the explanation he had meant to add.

Outside Porter Center, Simone leaned against the railing.

"How bad?" she asked.

"One probability problem."

"That was not my question."

"I may have treated dependent events as independent, corrected it, then failed to justify the correction."

"How was the rest?"

"Irrelevant if the cutoff is high."

Simone shook her head. "You are doing that thing."

"What thing?"

"Turning incomplete information into the worst available conclusion, then calling it realism."

Malik looked back through the glass doors as if the test might still be visible.

"Results come next week," she said. "Until then, you do not know."

"Uncertainty is not comforting."

"It is not supposed to be. It is supposed to be accurate."

He wanted to dislike her. Instead he stored the sentence beside all the other useful things people had said that week.

## Thursday, August 21: The First Bell

Opening Convocation began under a sky the color of washed denim.

First-year students gathered at the end of Cypress Walk in academic dress clothes, fresh MHU shirts, polished shoes, and sneakers chosen by people who had correctly predicted the amount of standing involved.

Malik found DJ near the live oaks, answering messages from teammates. Nia arrived with Keisha, who had convinced her not to carry three organization folders to a ceremony. Julian stood farther ahead with campus media, filming the procession.

The four first-years joined the crowd.

DJ leaned toward Malik. "This is a lot of symbolism before breakfast."

"It is ten thirty."

"Athlete breakfast rules. I have lived two days already."

Keisha looked at Nia. "Your people are strange."

"They are growing on me."

At the signal, the Founders' Bell rang.

The sound rolled over Cypress Walk, deeper than Malik expected. Conversations stopped. Phones lowered. For a few seconds, thousands of individual worries shared one silence.

They began walking.

Nia looked toward Eliza Moss Hall, where she and Malik had met over a red number and an empty chair. DJ pointed out an upperclassman receiver he hoped to learn from. Keisha counted how many minutes remained before track paperwork. Malik noticed all of it: the different ambitions moving together, not yet coordinated but no longer separate.

At the midpoint of the walk, Julian stepped backward with his camera raised.

"First-years," he called. "Look like you know where you are going."

"Historically inaccurate," Malik said.

Nia laughed just as Julian took the picture.

The image caught them in motion: DJ pointing, Keisha half smiling, Nia turned toward Malik, Malik looking more hopeful than he felt. Behind them, indigo banners lifted in the breeze.

Classes would begin next. Julian's invitation had become a real date. Malik's analytics decision remained beyond his control. Roommate rules would be tested, schedules would collide, and the mathematics would stop waiting for them to feel ready.

But on Cypress Walk, beneath the fading echo of the bell, readiness seemed less important than movement.

They kept walking.
