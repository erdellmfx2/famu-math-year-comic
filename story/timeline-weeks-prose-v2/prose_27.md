# Week Twenty-Seven: Identity in the Integral

Nia volunteered for the board before Dr. Delgado finished asking.

The integral contained an odd power of sine and an even power of cosine. She knew the pattern. Save one sine factor, convert the remaining sine squared using an identity, and substitute.

She wrote (1+\cos^2 x) where (1-\cos^2 x) belonged.

The error sat in the center of the board, large and confident.

Nia continued for three more lines before Amara raised one careful hand.

"Should that be minus?"

Nia stopped.

Every instinct told her to repair the line quickly enough that the class might forget who had made it. Instead, she stepped aside.

"Yes," she said. "I used the wrong identity. Will you finish from there?"

Amara approached the board with visible surprise. She corrected the sign, completed the substitution, and reached the answer Nia had been charging toward.

Dr. Delgado asked Nia what had gone wrong.

"I recognized the problem type and stopped checking the actual relationship."

"And what repaired it?"

Nia looked at Amara. "Letting someone else see what I could not."

## Friday, January 30: Finish the Solution

At lunch, Nia replayed the board mistake as if embarrassment improved with repetition.

Keisha stabbed a piece of broccoli. "You made a sign error, not a declaration of war."

"I began with tremendous confidence."

"That is your natural habitat."

"Amara had to finish my solution."

"And the university remained open."

Nia knew the embarrassment was not really about trigonometry. She liked being the person who moved first, connected ideas, and made the room less afraid. Stepping aside felt dangerously close to becoming unnecessary.

Later, she sent Amara a photograph of the corrected work with a thank-you. Amara replied that she usually knew the answer but talked herself out of raising her hand.

**Then we both practiced something,** Nia wrote.

In the research lab, the corrected data run finished. Keisha projected a network map covered in route changes. A few uncertain roads caused recommendations to swing across entire neighborhoods.

Nia nearly began assigning tasks. She caught herself.

"What do you think we should examine first?" she asked.

Keisha zoomed toward the bridge corridor. "Not the largest average change. This cluster. It affects fewer routes, but those routes have no close alternative."

Nia saw it after Keisha pointed it out.

Being a co-lead did not mean finishing every solution. Sometimes it meant recognizing whose view made the answer possible.

## Saturday, January 31: Advancement With Notes

The campus research office sent its decision at 10:07 Saturday morning.

Nia was brushing her teeth when her phone began vibrating across the sink. She grabbed it with one wet hand and read the first line.

Their proposal had advanced to the campus presentation round.

Her scream sent Keisha running from the bedroom holding one track shoe.

"We advanced!"

Keisha screamed too. They jumped in a circle, nearly collided with the shower curtain, and called Dr. Price before remembering it was Saturday.

Then they read the reviewer notes.

The question was timely. The sensitivity approach was promising. The division of technical contributions was clear.

The assumptions about human decisions were vague.

Why would residents choose the modeled route? Did access to a vehicle change the recommendation? What happened when families traveled together, waited for relatives, or avoided roads they considered unsafe? The model treated route choice as if every person responded to the same cost in the same way.

Nia sat on the closed toilet lid, celebration temporarily adjourned.

"They are right," she said.

"Probably," Keisha replied. "But we should not invent human behavior because reviewers asked for it."

They called Alexis, who told them not to fix social uncertainty with decorative numbers.

"Separate what your model decides from what a person decides," she said. "Then ask people who understand the difference."

Advancement did not mean approval of everything they had done. It meant the work was strong enough to receive more demanding questions.

Nia printed the reviewer notes and taped them above her desk.

## Sunday, February 1: Not Everybody's Weekend

Nia scheduled a six-hour Sunday rehearsal without consulting anyone.

She created an agenda, assigned sections, and brought enough snacks to make the assumption look generous. Keisha arrived at noon in track warmups.

"I have to leave at two," she said.

"The practice presentation is at four."

"My meet check-in is at three."

Nia blinked. "Today?"

"It has been on the calendar for a month."

"Can you join remotely afterward?"

Keisha stared at her. "After I race?"

Nia heard herself and disliked the sound.

"I am sorry. I assumed we would work through the weekend."

"You assumed *my* weekend belonged to the project."

Keisha packed her laptop. She had already completed the data-audit slides and left notes for the sections she would miss.

"We advanced because of both of us," she said. "That does not make you the owner of my time."

After she left, the room felt too large for Nia's agenda.

Nia shortened the rehearsal. She moved the code demonstration to Monday and sent Keisha a revised schedule rather than an apology disguised as another request.

Then she attended the meet.

She did not bring presentation notes.

## Monday, February 2: A Life Beside the Project

Keisha crouched in the starting blocks beneath white fieldhouse lights.

Nia sat with Imani high in the bleachers. She knew Keisha trained, of course. She had seen the alarm go off before sunrise and watched damp practice clothes colonize their bathroom. But knowing an activity existed was not the same as understanding that somebody had built a life inside it.

The starter raised the pistol.

Keisha exploded forward.

For less than a minute, there was no code, no symposium, no Nia. There was the curve of the indoor track, the tight rhythm of spikes, and Keisha hunting the stagger ahead of her. She crossed the line second in her heat and bent with both hands on her knees.

The board updated.

Personal best.

Nia shouted so loudly that Imani moved one seat away for hearing preservation.

Afterward, Keisha accepted Nia's congratulations but not an instant repair.

"I should have asked before scheduling," Nia said. "And I should know your meet calendar because you matter, not because I need to work around it."

Keisha considered that.

"Better apology."

"I practiced."

"Do not over-celebrate basic respect."

They took a picture beneath the results board. Back on campus, Nia rebuilt the project calendar with shared availability, separate task owners, and explicit approval before major meetings moved.

Keisha's goals did not orbit the research project. The project was fortunate to travel beside them.

## Tuesday, February 3: The Planned Road Trip

Malik sent Simone a road-trip itinerary with departure time, fuel stop, arena parking, dinner options, backup dinner options, and a weather contingency.

She replied with a single question.

**Are we attending basketball or evacuating a nation?**

They were driving two hours to an away game with a student-alumni group. Malik wanted to leave at 2:15. Simone wanted to stop at a bookstore she had found near the highway and decide about dinner after the game.

"After the game, every restaurant near the arena will be crowded," Malik said.

"Then we wait or go somewhere else."

"That is avoidable uncertainty."

"It is dinner, Malik."

The disagreement sharpened in the Porter Center lobby. To Malik, planning protected limited money and time. To Simone, planning every hour made a short trip feel like a supervised field exercise.

"You said you wanted to enjoy things while they happen," she reminded him.

"Enjoyment does not require logistical failure."

"Spontaneity is not failure."

They stood in silence as the group loaded the bus.

Finally, Malik folded the itinerary and tore off the lower half.

"We keep the departure and tickets," he said. "You choose one unscheduled stop."

"One?"

"I am negotiating against my nature."

Simone accepted.

They visited the bookstore, missed Malik's planned dinner reservation, and ate excellent tacos from a truck outside the arena. McCall-Hart lost by four, but the trip did not.

## Wednesday, February 4: Compatible Is Not Identical

On the ride home, Simone admitted that the bookstore had almost made them late.

Malik admitted the tacos were better than his reservation.

"Please document that conclusion," she said. "I may need it in future disputes."

"The sample size is one."

"A historic one."

They laughed quietly while most of the bus slept.

The disagreement had not revealed a fatal incompatibility. It had revealed two reasonable needs that could become unreasonable when treated as universal rules. Malik wanted predictability because surprises had often arrived carrying bills, damage, or bad news. Simone wanted room because ambition already placed enough of her life on schedules built by institutions and deadlines.

"I do not want every trip to be unplanned," she said.

"I do not want every minute assigned."

"That sounded painful."

"It was."

They agreed that future trips would have fixed anchors and open space. Neither described the compromise as one person winning.

Back at McCall-Hart after midnight, Malik walked Simone to her residence hall. She had an internship application due the next evening. He had an integration quiz in the morning.

They did not turn tiredness into a test of affection. They said goodnight and went to do separate work.

Compatibility, Malik was learning, was not sameness. It was the ability to remain curious when difference interrupted the plan.

## Thursday, February 5: Shared Ownership

Nia opened Thursday's team meeting by projecting a new schedule.

Every task had an owner. Every meeting had confirmed availability. Keisha's track meets were marked as commitments, not inconveniences. Nia's mathematical analysis, Keisha's software work, and their joint interpretation were separate colors with equal space.

"You gave me veto authority over code-demo changes," Keisha said.

"Because you own the code demo."

"I accept this constitutional reform."

They turned to the reviewer comments about human decisions. Neither had the training to make a documentary, conduct public interviews, and redesign the model alone. Nia knew who could help with one part without becoming absorbed into all of it.

She met Julian at the campus radio studio.

"We need advice on public visualization," she said. "Only that. We are not asking you to speak for residents or make the research look more certain than it is."

Julian leaned back. "What do you want people to understand?"

"Where route recommendations become unstable and what assumptions cause the change."

"Then stop using three nearly identical shades of blue. Also, animation might show the route changing better than five frozen maps."

He sketched a sequence: one road capacity shifted, the recommended path moved, and a caption named the assumption responsible. He suggested testing the visual with viewers who had not spent six weeks staring at the network.

"That is exactly the scope we need," Nia said.

"Good. I have band travel and two films. I cannot become your media department."

"I know."

And this time, she did.

Nia returned to the lab with Julian's notes. Keisha approved the visualization test and added it to the shared plan.

Outside, February rain silvered the campus walkways. A research conference, a Calculus II test, and the opening of spring sports waited in the next week. Their calendar was crowded, their model was unfinished, and no one person owned the whole answer.

For the first time, that made Nia feel more prepared rather than less.
