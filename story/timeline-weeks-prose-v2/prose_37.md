# Week Thirty-Seven: The Presentation and the No

Dr. Delgado placed a map of concentric warning zones on the screen.

Every point within the outer circle lay the same maximum distance from the broadcast tower. The picture suggested equal access.

Nia raised her hand. "Distance from the source is not distance to a safe route."

"Explain."

She walked to the board and marked two households at the same radius. One had a nearby road leading away from the flood zone. The other sat beyond a rail crossing and a low underpass. In polar coordinates, both shared the same value of (r). In the road network, their choices were not remotely equal.

"A coordinate system makes some relationships easy to see," she said. "It can make others disappear."

Dr. Delgado nodded. "Then choose the system that serves the question, and state what it hides."

The symposium presentation waited the next morning.

Nia wrote the sentence at the top of her final notes.

## Friday, April 10: Equal Radius, Unequal Access

The class calculated polar area for regions shaped like petals, loops, and overlapping circles.

Nia loved how a complicated Cartesian boundary could become a simple radial description. She also recognized the temptation to confuse mathematical convenience with human truth.

For the symposium, the team used a polar overlay to show distance from a warning source. Then they placed the real road network above it. Households at equal radial distance faced different travel times, bridge access, vehicle availability, and warning delays.

Keisha tested the live model one last time. Julian checked the order of every visual. Nia rehearsed the opening until she could say it without rushing.

Malik stayed away, honoring their agreement. He sent one message to the full team: **You have prepared the evidence. Trust each other in the room.**

Nia appreciated the message because it did not become a private promise.

That evening, she packed her conference clothes, calculator, backup drive, printed slides, and enough snacks for a small emergency shelter. Keisha removed half the snacks.

"We are traveling for three days, not establishing a colony."

"Preparedness is part of our research identity."

"Preparedness has weight limits."

Nia finally closed the suitcase.

She had done everything she knew how to do. The next step was not more preparation.

It was entering the room.

## Saturday, April 11: The Other Posters

The Southeastern Undergraduate Mathematics Symposium occupied a university conference center two hours from Bellwether.

The first poster Nia saw used a dataset with millions of records. The second listed a federal grant. A third belonged to seniors who had worked together for two years and already had a journal manuscript under review.

Nia looked at their foam-board poster and suddenly saw every taped edge.

"Do not compare our January to somebody else's second year," Keisha said.

"I am comparing our printer budget to federal funding."

"That comparison is valid and unhelpful."

They attended presentations before their own session. One team predicted hospital demand using carefully anonymized records. Another modeled wildfire evacuation with a cleaner road dataset than anything Nia had imagined possible. The presenters answered questions with practiced ease.

Julian noticed what Nia did not. "That hospital team has no public-facing explanation of their uncertainty."

Keisha noticed that the wildfire model stored only final outputs, not scenario histories.

Every project had strengths and omissions. More funding did not make a team flawless. Less funding did not make McCall-Hart's work secretly superior.

Nia stopped sorting the room into threats and proof.

They pinned up the poster, connected the laptop, and tested the backup.

Their work had its own question to answer.

## Sunday, April 12: The Talk They Built Together

Nia began with one household receiving a delayed warning.

Julian advanced the visual sequence. The audience watched the initial route fill as other households departed earlier. Keisha changed one road-capacity assumption in the live model. The recommended path shifted, and the history panel displayed why.

Nia explained the mathematics only after the decision was visible.

Questions came from every side.

How were trust rates estimated?

"They are not population estimates," Nia said. "We use bounded scenarios informed by a small approved interview set and published studies. The local interviews identify mechanisms, not prevalence."

Could the system predict individual behavior?

"No," Keisha answered. "It tests how different patterns affect network recommendations."

Why animate the changes?

Julian explained that a still map hid the sequence of assumptions, then acknowledged that animation could imply false authority. He showed the source labels and pause controls added after audience testing.

A judge asked whether officials should use the model now.

Nia felt the fellowship hanging inside the question. She could make the work sound ready.

"Not for operational routing," she said. "It has not been validated for that. It can help identify which data gaps and communication assumptions make a route recommendation unstable."

The answer limited the project.

It also told the truth.

When the session ended, Keisha squeezed Nia's hand. Julian whispered, "No one fainted, including the software."

They had given the talk they built together.

## Monday, April 13: Praise and the Gap

The fellowship interview took place in a smaller room.

Three judges asked about public value, technical design, team roles, and future work. They praised the project's integration of mathematics, software, and communication. They praised the decision to expose assumptions rather than publish one authoritative route.

Then they identified the gaps.

The model lacked validation against a real event or exercise. The community interview sample was too limited. Transportation access remained underdeveloped. The route network covered too small an area to establish scalability. The team had not yet tested whether repeated route updates confused users.

Nia answered without defensiveness.

"Those are the next validation questions," she said. "We would not expand the tool before testing them with community and emergency-management partners."

One judge asked who would own the project if the fellowship funded it.

"No one student," Nia replied. "Keisha and I are co-leads. Julian owns the approved media workflow. Community participants control how their contributions are used. Any operational partnership would require shared governance beyond our team."

Afterward, Dr. Price told them they had represented the work well.

Nia wanted to ask whether that meant they would win.

She knew better.

Praise described what the judges valued. It did not erase what remained unresolved or predict their final choice.

## Tuesday, April 14: The Other Name

The awards ceremony began at four.

Nia sat between Keisha and Julian. Her hands were cold despite the crowded ballroom.

Several project awards came first. She applauded each team. When the fellowship category appeared on the screen, the room seemed to narrow around the presenter holding the envelope.

The fellowship went to a team studying heat-risk alerts for outdoor workers.

For one fraction of a second, Nia waited for another name.

None came.

She applauded.

The winning students stood in shock, then hugged. Their work was strong. Their joy did not insult her. Nia smiled when they passed her row.

As soon as the ceremony ended, she told Keisha she needed the restroom.

She walked past it and found a concrete stairwell between conference floors.

The door closed behind her.

Nia sat on the second step and cried.

She cried because she had wanted the laboratory placement, the mentors, the money, the title, and the proof that the better version of herself could receive a better ending. She cried because the judges' gaps were real and because she had still hoped the strength of the talk would outweigh them.

No audience watched. No one needed her to be gracious or inspirational.

The fellowship belonged to another team.

The no belonged fully to her.

## Wednesday, April 15: Wait Outside the Door

Keisha found Nia's location from a short message: **Stairwell B. I am safe. Not ready.**

She entered, sat two steps below, and said nothing.

Julian stayed in the hallway with their bags. Dr. Price handled checkout. The team protected Nia's privacy without pretending disappointment was an emergency to solve.

Malik had traveled to the symposium for the final day with Dr. Bennett and several actuarial students. He heard the result, found Julian outside the stairwell, and stopped.

"Is she in there?"

"Yes."

Malik did not enter.

He waited against the opposite wall. The boundary from last week still existed. Grief did not cancel consent.

Keisha eventually came out.

"She asked if you are here," she said. "I told her yes. She wants to talk to you."

Only then did Malik open the door.

Nia had washed her face, but her eyes were swollen. She shifted to make room on the step.

"I did not win," she said.

"I know."

Malik sat beside her without touching her.

The stairwell remained quiet enough for the truth to stay unperformed.

## Thursday, April 16: No Lesson Tonight

"I did everything better," Nia said.

Her voice broke on the final word.

"I shared ownership. I fixed the model. I admitted uncertainty. I listened to criticism. I gave the best presentation I have ever given, and I still lost."

Malik did not tell her the experience would open another door. He did not say rejection built character, the project still mattered, or the judges might regret their choice.

"Yes," he said. "You did everything better, and you still lost."

Nia looked at him, almost angry that he agreed.

"That is all?"

"The loss is real. It does not need a lesson tonight."

The sentence removed the work of finding a silver lining before she was ready.

Nia leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "I wanted it so much."

"I know."

"I do not want to be mature about it."

"Then do not be mature in a stairwell."

She laughed once through tears.

Malik waited until she asked before holding her hand. The gesture was comfort, not a promise. Keisha remained near the door. Julian guarded the luggage and told anyone approaching that the stairwell was temporarily experiencing advanced mathematics.

Eventually, the team left together.

The project was still theirs. The fellowship was not.

Nothing that night required Nia to pretend those facts weighed the same.
