# Week Twenty-Two: The Year Turns Quietly

Malik finished the analytics postmortem the day after Christmas.

The final page listed the work a future team would need: external data, operational testing, a transferability study, and a version of the interface that remained useful when staff had seconds rather than minutes. He added source links, archived the code, and gave Simone final editorial access.

Then he sent her a message.

**Thank you for refusing to let us call the result unfair before we understood it. Also for cutting the slides. They needed to die.**

Simone replied: **I have waited six weeks for you to admit that. Call me. I need to hear the sincerity.**

Malik called from the Baptiste porch.

The conversation began with deleted slides. It moved to the alumni workshop, spring schedules, DJ's scholarship, Simone's finance internship search, and the strange relief of having a break long enough to discover which people they wanted to talk to when no deliverable required it.

When Malik checked the time, sixty-eight minutes had passed.

"We did it again," he said.

"Replication," Simone replied. "Our finding may be robust."

Malik smiled into the cold Gulfport evening.

## Friday, December 26: The Thank-You

Simone asked about the house without turning the call into an interview.

Malik told her which rooms had been delayed and how his father handled physical therapy like a personal feud. She described her family's holiday debate over whether her international-internship plans counted as "going too far away on purpose."

"Do you want to go that far?" Malik asked.

"If the right opportunity is there, yes."

She did not soften the answer.

He liked that about her.

Before hanging up, Simone said, "We should get coffee when we are back. Not a postmortem. Not a team meeting."

Malik leaned against the porch rail. "Personal coffee?"

"You are an analyst. Interpret the available evidence."

"I would like that."

The invitation created a nervousness distinct from his old hope around Nia. It was not built on waiting for an opening. Simone had asked. Malik had answered.

Inside, Celeste called him to help move a cabinet.

Malik ended the call and carried the new possibility into the house without trying to define it further.

## Saturday, December 27: The Call for Proposals

In Huntsville, Nia received Dr. Price's research announcement.

The Founders' Research Forum was accepting early proposals for a spring flood-resilience track. Strong projects could advance to the Southeastern Undergraduate Mathematics Symposium and compete for a national summer fellowship.

Nia read the message three times.

The Delta laboratory visit returned to her in pieces: water moving through low roads, uncertainty layers on the visualization wall, Dr. Washington saying failure was information about work rather than a complete measurement of the worker.

She opened a new document titled **Bellwether Flood Evacuation Model**.

Her first idea was too large. It included road capacity, vehicle access, shelter choice, warning trust, mobility, traffic flow, and dynamic flood depth. The project would have required an entire laboratory, two government agencies, and possibly a minor deity.

Nia reduced it to a question: **How might missing road-capacity and household-access information change a recommended evacuation route?**

For readiness practice, she graphed a simplified rate of traffic accumulation, recovered antiderivatives, and repaired the algebra she had marked as fragile. The math was review. The application made it feel pointed toward spring.

She texted Keisha: **Research proposal. Flood evacuation. I need a real programmer and somebody who tells me when the question is impossible. Interested?**

Keisha replied: **Define “need.”**

Nia recognized the test.

## Sunday, December 28: Coauthor

Keisha joined the video call from Mobile wearing track gear and holding a bowl of cereal.

"What do you want me to do?" she asked.

Nia shared the draft. "Build the route model, clean data, help decide what the system can actually compute--"

"Stop."

Nia stopped.

"You just listed code, data, and model design as if they are support services around your idea."

"I did not mean it that way."

"Meaning is not the project structure."

Keisha set down the bowl. "I will join if I am a collaborator. That means I help define the question, choose methods, reject bad data, and own the computational work. My name goes on the application from the beginning. I am not emergency technical help after you promise something the code cannot do."

Nia felt the reflex to defend herself. Then she remembered the spring goal written in copper ink: let friends contribute without directing every contribution.

"Coauthor," Nia said. "Shared method decisions. You can veto computational claims we cannot support."

"And track meets remain on my calendar."

"Agreed."

"Then send me the data description. Not the whole giant dream."

Nia smiled. "The giant dream has excellent visual language."

"I am sure it does. Send the data."

The partnership began with a boundary, which made it stronger than a favor.

## Monday, December 29: Delete Reminder

Malik's private calendar contained a reminder he had created in October.

**Tell Nia after finals.**

No details. No explanation of what he intended to tell her. The reminder assumed finals would create a perfect moment, Nia's relationship would resolve somehow, Malik's family crisis would calm, and his patient feelings would become a claim worth presenting.

He stared at it.

Nia did not owe him a moment because he had waited. He did not owe his past self indefinite loyalty to a possibility that had never become a mutual decision.

Malik deleted the reminder.

The action did not erase his feelings. It changed what he planned to do with them.

For several minutes, he let himself grieve the scene that would never happen: finding Nia after finals, explaining that he had cared through every other relationship and crisis, hearing that his loyalty had finally become the right answer. The imagined scene had asked nothing of Nia except to recognize a role Malik had written for her.

He could admit the fantasy had comforted him without treating it as a contract she had broken.

Malik opened their message history, not to search for evidence, but to see the friendship as it actually existed: jokes, study plans, disagreements, storm updates, and honest support. That relationship was real. The promised romantic ending had been private.

Deleting the reminder protected the friendship from becoming an invoice.

He opened his spring calendar and added Simone's coffee without marking it "networking" or "analytics."

Then he completed a registration check. Calculus II, actuarial prerequisites, North Star hours, and one open evening remained visible. He practiced antiderivative patterns for thirty minutes, corrected two algebra errors, and stopped when the timer ended.

Moving forward looked less dramatic than confession.

It looked like making room for the future to contain evidence he did not already control.

## Tuesday, December 30: Warm and Final

Julian messaged Nia before New Year's Eve.

**Hope Huntsville has been good to you. Thank you for the good parts of this year. Wishing you a spring that feels like yours.**

Nia read the message slowly.

No question. No reference to missing her. No disguised invitation to restart the relationship at midnight.

She replied: **Thank you for the good parts too. I hope spring gives you stories worth telling and some you keep for yourself. Happy New Year, Julian.**

His response was a simple heart.

The exchange felt warm, sad, and finished.

Nia placed the phone down and returned to the proposal call with Keisha. They repaired an algebraic relationship in the simplified flow model and compared two graph interpretations. Keisha rejected a variable Nia could not define from available sources.

"The map looked better with it," Nia complained.

"We are not applying for Prettiest Unsupported Arrow."

"That award has prestige."

"No funding."

They removed the arrow.

## Wednesday, December 31: Background Laughter

At midnight, Nia called Malik.

Her family had counted down in the living room. Fireworks sounded several streets away. Nia expected Malik to answer from the Baptiste porch or the temporary living-room bed.

Instead, several voices filled the connection.

"Happy New Year," Malik said.

In the background, DJ shouted something about championship energy. Andre argued with a video filter. Then Simone laughed, clear and close enough to be unmistakable.

Nia's chest tightened.

"Group call?" she asked.

"Analytics team and DJ. Simone started it after somebody posted the wrong time zone."

"Sounds fun."

Her voice became brighter than the moment required.

Malik paused. "You okay?"

"Yes. Just loud here. Happy New Year."

"Happy New Year, Nia."

She ended the call before she could ask why Simone was there, whether they had talked over break, or why any answer mattered.

The fireworks continued.

Nia told herself the sharp feeling was surprise.

## Thursday, January 1: Open the Application

New Year's Day began quietly.

Nia woke late and saw no missed call from Malik. That was reasonable. They had spoken. Nothing had gone wrong.

The sharp feeling remained.

She considered calling Keisha and turning it into a full analysis. She considered calling Malik back with a casual question. She considered checking Simone's social media, which would have produced data and no wisdom.

Instead, Nia opened the research application.

The form asked for the question, team roles, available data, proposed methods, anticipated limitations, and how the work would involve affected communities.

She entered both names: **Nia Reynolds, mathematical modeling and communication. Keisha Morgan, computation and data-method co-lead.**

For the limitations section, Nia wrote that missing road-capacity values might prevent a reliable route recommendation. She did not promise the model would solve what the data could not support.

The work steadied her without erasing the feeling.

Spring would return with two new partnerships: Malik and Simone exploring something personal, Nia and Keisha building something competitive.

The emotional current had reversed before Nia was ready to name it.
