# Week Thirty: The First Infinite Question

Dr. Delgado began the sequences unit without writing an infinity symbol.

Instead, he described a warning system that updated its estimated arrival time every minute. The first message predicted water in forty minutes. The next predicted thirty-four. Then thirty-one. Then twenty-nine and a half.

"If the updates continue," he asked, "are they approaching a stable warning time, or are we only hoping they are?"

Students proposed answers immediately.

"Twenty-eight minutes."

"It depends on the pattern."

"The storm does not care about our sequence."

"An excellent observation," Dr. Delgado said. "The mathematics describes the updates. It does not guarantee the world obeys the model."

He wrote the terms on the board and asked whether the sequence was monotonic, whether it was bounded, and what evidence would support a limit.

Nia thought about messages moving through the evacuation network. One alert was a snapshot. Repeated updates could approach clarity, amplify confusion, or never reach some people at all.

## Friday, February 20: Getting Closer Is Not Arriving

The class built sequences from formulas, graphs, and recursive rules.

One sequence increased forever but never passed a fixed bound. Another bounced above and below a value while moving closer to it. A third looked stable for ten terms and then wandered away.

"Ten calm terms do not prove convergence," Dr. Delgado said. "A convincing pattern is not the same as a result."

Malik liked the definitions. Monotonicity described direction. Boundedness described restraint. Together, under the right conditions, they could guarantee convergence.

Nia liked the movement. A sequence carried its own history: each term related to an index, sometimes to the term before it, always to the question of what happened next.

During group work, she modeled the delay between an alert entering the system and reaching a growing share of recipients. Malik noticed that the percentage approached one hundred only because her recurrence assumed every unreached person had the same chance of receiving the next update.

"That is probably false," he said.

"Definitely false. People without service may remain unreachable across every term."

"Then your upper bound may be below one hundred percent."

The idea was painful and useful. A warning system could converge and still converge to an unacceptable limit.

Nia circled the sentence.

Getting closer did not answer the ethical question. They also had to ask what they were getting closer to.

## Saturday, February 21: The Summer Door

Delta National Laboratory's virtual briefing filled a lecture room in the Porter Center.

The opening slide showed storm-surge simulations layered over roads, power systems, hospitals, and population data. Students from mathematics, computer science, engineering, public health, communications, and social science attended.

A Delta scientist described the summer partnership with McCall-Hart. Teams would examine how storm conditions and infrastructure failures changed evacuation options. No single major could own the problem.

"A fast model that ignores human decisions is incomplete," she said. "A perfect message that arrives after the road floods is incomplete. A community study disconnected from physical constraints is incomplete. We need teams capable of disagreeing across disciplines."

Nia looked at Keisha. Keisha was already taking notes.

The scientists emphasized data governance, reproducibility, uncertainty, and public accountability. Students would not receive unrestricted access to real emergency records. They would begin with carefully managed datasets and document every transformation.

Then Dr. Price explained the path forward. Spring symposium work could inform summer placements, but attendance at the briefing was not an invitation. Strong students might still be rejected. Selected teams would need faculty sponsorship and clearly defined roles.

Nia felt ambition rise without mistaking it for a promise.

During questions, Keisha asked about computation limits. Julian asked how public-facing media would be reviewed. Nia asked whether iterative route updates could be tested against changing warning reach.

The scientist said yes, if the team stated what drove each update and who remained outside the model.

The summer door had opened a few inches. Nobody had been invited through it yet.

## Sunday, February 22: A Map That Can Change Its Mind

Their original project produced one recommended evacuation map for each scenario.

Nia no longer trusted the stillness.

Storm forecasts changed. Road capacities changed. Warnings reached people at different times. A route that was sensible at noon could become dangerous at 12:20, and a static map could not show why the recommendation moved.

She proposed an iterative model.

At each time step, the system would update estimated road capacity, warning reach, and departure volume. It would recalculate route costs and record whether recommendations stabilized. Keisha immediately identified the danger of making the software appear more certain simply because it updated more often.

"Frequent wrong answers are not live intelligence," she said.

"Put that on the poster."

They defined update rules, confidence labels, and stopping conditions. If new data stopped changing the route, the model could report temporary stability. If recommendations continued to oscillate, it should display instability rather than selecting the most recent path as truth.

Julian tested a rough animation. Roads shifted from copper to indigo as conditions changed. A side panel showed which assumption caused each update.

"It looks alive," Nia said.

"Do not fall in love with movement," Julian warned. "Animation can make weak evidence look authoritative."

She added the warning to their design notes.

The new project did not offer one perfect map. It showed a map capable of changing its mind and explaining why.

## Monday, February 23: The City in the Notebook

Simone met Malik in the Porter Center courtyard carrying the copper notebook he had given her.

She opened to the question about opportunities that might make their relationship harder.

"I applied for a summer internship in London," she said.

Malik's first thought was distance. His second was cost. His third was the time difference, which he calculated before he could stop himself.

Simone continued. "It is a financial-data program with placements across banking and public policy. I have wanted it since October. I submitted the final application last week."

Last week.

Malik felt the delay, but he also heard the rest: she had wanted it for months and had been afraid the truth would become a relationship negotiation before the application was even real.

"You should have told me," he said.

"Yes. I should have."

She did not blame his planning for her silence. "I waited because I wanted the application to belong to me before it became about us. That was not fair."

"How badly do you want it?"

Simone met his eyes. "Very badly. If I receive it, I am going."

No softening. No promise that love would automatically solve geography.

Malik took a slow breath. "Then I hope you get it."

He meant that.

The future still rearranged itself inside him.

## Tuesday, February 24: Solving Before Asking

By Tuesday morning, Malik had researched international phone plans, compared flight prices, mapped the five-hour time difference, and built a summer schedule around work, classes, and video calls.

He had not asked Simone whether she wanted a long-distance relationship.

The spreadsheet looked excellent.

That was the problem.

DJ found him staring at it in North Star Learning Commons.

"Is this a budget?"

"A preliminary feasibility analysis."

"For romance?"

Malik closed the laptop.

"Did she ask for one?" DJ said.

"No."

"Then you are doing emotional group work alone."

The phrase was ridiculous enough to be useful.

Malik deleted no information, but he moved the file out of sight. That evening, he told Simone he had started planning before asking what she wanted.

"I do not know yet," she said. "I do not even have the offer. I know I care about you. I also know I do not want my interview to become a referendum on whether we survive the summer."

"It will not."

"And if I get it, we need to decide together whether long distance makes sense. Not assume staying together is the only respectful answer."

Malik wanted to promise they could make it work. Instead, he asked what interview help she wanted.

"Practice questions," Simone said. "No optimization model."

"That boundary feels targeted."

"It is supported by data."

## Wednesday, February 25: Nobody Attends Everything

McCall-Hart's baseball and softball schedules collided with a track meet, a Calculus II problem set, and DJ's offseason film review.

The friend group attempted to support everybody and nearly created a transportation crisis.

Imani wanted someone at the softball doubleheader. Keisha raced across town. Baseball hosted a conference opponent at the same time. DJ had promised to help Malik review sequences before reporting to football conditioning.

"We cannot divide one person into partial fractions," Nia said.

"That is not what partial fractions means," Malik replied.

"It expresses the emotional truth."

They built a coverage plan without pretending attendance measured friendship. Nia and Julian went to Keisha's early heat, then Julian left for baseball media work. Simone attended Imani's first softball game. DJ brought study notes to the second game and reviewed between innings. Malik stayed in the learning commons, where he hosted a shared call for anyone who needed sequence help.

Imani sent score updates. Keisha sent a picture of her relay team. Julian uploaded one baseball clip. Nobody demanded immediate replies.

The day was less cinematic than all of them arriving together beneath one banner. It was also more sustainable.

That evening, Keisha joined Malik's study call from the team van. DJ logged in from a quiet fieldhouse corridor. Imani watched the recording after treatment.

Support did not always mean witnessing the event. Sometimes it meant making sure the person could return to the rest of life afterward.

## Thursday, February 26: Ambition Has a Direction

Nia found Malik and Simone in a Porter Center practice room.

Simone stood at the front answering an interview question about model risk. Malik sat at the table with a list of follow-ups. He did not correct her mid-answer. When she finished, he asked where she could make her role in a team project more specific.

"I designed the monitoring thresholds," Simone said. "The answer makes it sound like I only interpreted results."

"Then claim the decision."

Nia paused outside the partly open door.

The scene resembled evenings when Malik had helped her prepare for calculus or listened to her research ideas. But the care moving through this room belonged to the relationship he had chosen. More importantly, it served Simone's future even though that future might carry her across an ocean.

Simone saw Nia and waved her in.

"We need a hostile interviewer," she said.

"I can be constructively alarming."

Nia asked why the London program should select Simone over candidates with more experience.

Simone answered with clarity, then improved the answer when Nia pressed her.

For half an hour, the three of them worked without turning the room into a romantic contest. When the session ended, Malik gave Simone the marked question list to keep.

Walking back to North Star, Nia understood something she had only partly learned from Julian.

Loving someone's ambition did not mean cheering because their success kept them close. It meant wanting the work to open in its true direction, even when that direction moved away from you.

The thought hurt.

It also made Malik's support look less like loss and more like love practiced honestly.
