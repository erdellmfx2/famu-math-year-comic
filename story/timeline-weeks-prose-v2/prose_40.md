# Week Forty: The Space Between Semesters

Spring grades arrived before breakfast.

Nia opened hers under a blanket, as if fabric could protect her from a number already stored in the university system. Malik checked his at the North Star front desk between returning loaner calculators.

Both had passed Calculus II strongly.

Nia stared at the grade and waited for it to change how she felt about the fellowship. It did not. Malik waited for his grade to make the summer project decision obvious. It did not.

A course result could confirm that they had learned integration methods, series, parametric equations, and polar coordinates. It could not evaluate whether Nia's work deserved national funding. It could not determine whether Malik was ready to work beside her without treating proximity as a promise.

They sent each other brief congratulations.

No hidden message traveled underneath.

## Friday, May 1: The Grade Stays in Its Lane

Final examinations ended, and McCall-Hart changed moods in one afternoon.

Students who had moved silently through the week now shouted across courtyards. Library tables emptied. Residence halls filled with open boxes, discarded hangers, and arguments about whether one microwave belonged to a roommate or to "the room as a community."

Nia celebrated her grade with Keisha and Amara over melting ice cream. Keisha had passed her courses while racing, co-leading research, and protecting her time from Nia's scheduling ambitions. Amara had earned one of her strongest math grades after beginning the year afraid to speak at the board.

"We survived Calculus II," Nia announced.

"We learned Calculus II," Amara corrected.

"That is a much more academic slogan."

Malik called his parents. Marcel joked that two semesters of calculus should qualify Malik to calculate when the insurance contractor would finally finish. Celeste reminded both of them that no equation could make a contractor answer the phone.

The good grade belonged to the course. The unfinished home belonged to another problem. The ended relationship belonged to a third.

For once, Malik did not force the results into one story about whether his life was improving.

Each stayed in its lane.

## Saturday, May 2: Walking Past the Fellowship

Spring commencement filled Founders Lawn with cream chairs, family umbrellas, camera phones, and graduates trying not to lose decorated caps in the wind.

Nia watched several student researchers cross the stage. One had presented at the symposium. Another had worked at Delta National Laboratory the previous summer. Their faculty mentors cheered from the side.

The sight reopened the fellowship grief.

Nia imagined the professional photograph she would not take, the laboratory access she would not receive under that award, and the title that would not appear beside her name. The pain arrived without making her feel fraudulent among mathematicians.

That difference mattered.

In the stairwell after the ceremony, she had felt as if rejection might define the edge of her ability. Now she could recognize the fellowship as a door she had not entered, not a verdict that she did not belong in the building.

Dr. Price passed with a group of graduates.

"Are you considering the summer offer?" she asked.

"Yes. I am still disappointed."

"Those facts do not compete."

Nia watched another mathematics student pose with three generations of family beneath the Founders' Bell.

She wanted a future in this work.

The no had changed its route, not its direction.

## Sunday, May 3: The Semifinal

Residence halls closed while Imani's softball team played its conference semifinal.

Nia and Keisha loaded half their room into Denise Reynolds's car, watched the first innings on a phone balanced against a storage bin, and yelled whenever the screen froze at the worst possible moment.

McCall-Hart entered the seventh inning trailing by one. Imani reached base, advanced to second, and was stranded when a deep fly ball died at the warning track.

The game ended there.

No walk-off came. No postseason invitation waited beyond the scoreboard.

Imani's team had produced its most successful season in years. The seniors had changed the program's expectations. They still lost the semifinal they wanted to win.

On the field, players stood in a long line after shaking hands. Some cried immediately. Others remained composed until the coaches gathered them near the dugout.

Nia did not tell herself the season mattered more than the result. The season mattered, and the result hurt.

She understood the grammar now.

Keisha closed the stream only after every senior left the field.

Then they finished moving out of the room where spring had changed both of them.

## Monday, May 4: Pride Without the Trophy

The softball team held a senior dinner on campus before everyone left.

Imani invited the friend group to help decorate. Photographs covered one wall: early losses, rain delays, bus rides, study halls, wins, taped wrists, and seniors laughing beneath stadium lights.

The coach did not call the semifinal a moral victory.

"We wanted the championship," she said. "We did not win it. We also built a season future teams will inherit. Both deserve honest names."

Each senior spoke about one thing she was proud of and one thing she wished had ended differently. The room held both without correcting either.

Nia wrote the sentence on the back of a program: **Pride does not require pretending the trophy was irrelevant.**

Imani saw it. "Are you turning our banquet into research?"

"Personal reflection."

"That sounds worse."

They laughed, took too many pictures, and danced when the formal program ended. The seniors carried no trophy out of the room. They carried their own records, friendships, and the knowledge that the next team would begin from a higher standard.

Nia left still wishing they had won.

She also left proud.

## Tuesday, May 5: Do Not Keep It Half-Alive

Simone left for international internship orientation with one suitcase, one garment bag, and the copper notebook in her backpack.

Malik met her at the shuttle stop. They had already returned personal items and ended the relationship. This goodbye was not another breakup.

"I want to ask one thing," Simone said.

"Okay."

"Let spring remain meaningful without keeping it half-alive. Do not send messages designed to make me remember us. Do not wait for London to fail. Do not treat friendship as a holding pattern."

Malik felt the precision of every request.

"I will not."

"And I will not send you loneliness as if it means we chose wrong."

They hugged. Simone stepped onto the shuttle and chose a seat near the window. She did not make a dramatic final gesture as it pulled away. Malik did not chase the bus with one more thought.

He watched until it turned beyond the Porter Center, then left.

Their relationship had an ending. Respecting it meant not stretching affection into an unofficial continuation neither had chosen.

London belonged to Simone now.

Spring belonged to both of them as history.

## Wednesday, May 6: Roles in Writing

Malik accepted the summer risk-modeling role.

He told Dr. Bennett that he understood Nia and Keisha would be project co-leads and that his family experience could not be used without consent.

"Good," Dr. Bennett said. "You are joining a research team, not a personal narrative."

Across the hall, Dr. Price handed Nia and Keisha revised appointment letters. Both now held co-lead authority. Nia led mathematical modeling and community integration. Keisha led computation, data governance, and reproducibility. Neither title reduced the other's decisions to support.

Keisha signed first.

Nia signed second.

Only afterward did Dr. Price tell them Malik had accepted.

Nia's heart reacted before her face. She let the feeling pass.

"What is his scope?" she asked.

Risk metrics, validation, loss assumptions, and documentation. A professional role with edges.

"That can strengthen the project," Keisha said.

"Yes," Nia agreed.

Neither discussed what it might mean romantically because the appointment letters promised work, not resolution.

## Thursday, May 7: Leave Without a Promise

Before the short break, Malik and Nia met on Founders Lawn.

Campus looked temporary. Chairs were gone. Move-out bins waited near residence halls. Summer students had not yet arrived.

"I accepted," Malik said.

"So did Keisha and I."

"I know."

They reviewed what that meant. They would take Calculus III together, work on the same project, and spend more time in Eliza Moss Hall than either considered medically advisable.

"No private promises before the work begins," Nia said.

"No assuming proximity is progress."

"No pretending we do not have feelings."

"And no letting the feelings make team decisions."

They agreed.

Nia left for Huntsville. Malik left for Gulfport. Keisha traveled to family with her laptop closed for the first forty-eight hours under threat from her track teammates.

For one brief week, no class met and no model ran.

The space between semesters did not resolve anything.

It gave each person room to return by choice.
