# Week Twenty-One: Christmas Without the Old Version

Nia found Julian's playlist in a box labeled **DESK / IMPORTANT / DO NOT LOSE**, which guaranteed it had been packed beneath three less important boxes.

The playlist was not digital. Julian had made it by hand after their first official month together: twelve songs written on a copper card, each with one sentence explaining why he chose it. Beside track four, he had written, **This sounds like you explaining an idea faster than the rest of us can think.**

Nia sat on her childhood bedroom floor and played the songs in order.

The list held their good parts without including the arguments that followed. It remembered Copper Cup, band rehearsals, campus rain, the Heritage Kickoff, and one late-night radio segment when Nia laughed so hard she snorted on air and Julian promised to delete the recording.

He had not deleted it.

She smiled at the memory.

Then she cried.

Missing those moments did not mean she should call him. Loving what had been good did not make the relationship compatible after all.

Nia let the playlist finish.

## Friday, December 19: The Good Parts

When the final song ended, Nia placed the card back in the box instead of throwing it away or leaving it on top.

Her mother found her downstairs making tea.

"Julian?" Denise asked.

"Playlist."

"That sounds like Julian."

Nia looked into the mug. "He was thoughtful. People act like breakups reveal the relationship was fake. Ours was not fake."

"No."

"It was also not working."

"Both facts get to stay."

Nia had spent weeks trying to sort the relationship into a clean result: good or bad, right or wrong, worth it or mistake. The playlist resisted the categories. It had been loving. The public jokes had been hurtful. The first kiss mattered. The ending was still necessary.

She carried her tea to the living room and helped untangle holiday lights with her cousins.

The good memories came with her. They did not drag her backward.

## Saturday, December 20: Chosen

Denise asked the harder question Saturday morning.

They were driving to Nia's aunt's tutoring program, where families were collecting holiday learning kits and donated books.

"Do you miss Julian," Denise said, "or do you miss being chosen?"

Nia looked out the window. "That feels rude before ten."

"We can reschedule truth for noon."

"No."

Nia thought about Julian asking her to be his girlfriend beneath Founders' Bell. The label had made her feel visible and wanted. She had liked belonging to an *us* that other people recognized.

"I miss him," she said. "His jokes when they were not defensive. How serious he was about band. The way he listened when he forgot to perform listening."

"And being chosen?"

"Yes. I miss that too. I cannot separate them yet."

Denise nodded. "You do not have to separate them today. Just do not call one feeling by the other's name."

At the tutoring program, Nia helped children build paper bridges and estimate how much weight each design could hold. She did not volunteer to redesign the entire event. She stayed at her assigned table.

Her mother noticed and said nothing.

## Sunday, December 21: Dependence

In Gulfport, Malik helped his father complete physical-therapy exercises.

Marcel sat in a sturdy chair while Malik counted knee bends and checked the printed range-of-motion guide.

"Ten," Malik said.

"That was twelve."

"That was ten."

"College has damaged your counting."

"Again."

Marcel bent the knee. Pain crossed his face.

"Stop if it is sharp," Malik said.

"It is not sharp."

"You made the sharp face."

"I have one face."

They completed the set. When Marcel tried to stand, he refused Malik's hand, failed on the first attempt, and became angry at the chair.

Malik waited instead of grabbing him.

"Want help?" he asked.

His father stared at the floor. "Yes."

Malik offered his forearm. Marcel stood.

The humiliation in his father's expression unsettled him more than the weakness. Marcel had built his identity around providing, repairing, and being the person others called. Dependence made him feel as if his usefulness had been removed along with the damaged walls.

"You are still my father when I help you stand," Malik said.

Marcel looked at him. "I know that in my head."

"The rest takes time?"

"Apparently."

It was the closest they came to discussing fear.

## Monday, December 22: Which Rooms Wait

The contractor called Monday with another delay.

A material shipment would not arrive before January. One subcontractor had overbooked holiday work. The estimate for the back rooms had increased after hidden moisture damage appeared behind the remaining wall.

Repairs would continue into spring.

The family gathered around the dining table with floor plans and costs. They could complete the kitchen and one bedroom first, then delay Malik's room and the utility area. Or they could spread work across all rooms and leave everything unfinished longer.

"Finish Mom and Dad's room," Malik said. "I am at school most of the time."

Celeste objected. Marcel objected louder.

"This is not about whose room matters," Malik said. "It is about which space gets used every day."

He used a rough accumulation estimate to compare temporary housing and storage costs under each schedule. Small weekly expenses, extended across months, changed which delay was actually cheaper.

Micah watched Malik shade weekly cost bars on a hand-drawn timeline.

"Is that another integral?" he asked.

"A rough preview. If the cost rate changes over time, total cost is what accumulates under the rate. Right now we only have weekly estimates, so we are adding pieces."

"Like the flood-water math?"

"Related idea. Different thing we wish we did not need to measure."

They compared two schedules. Finishing every room slowly looked fair, but it extended storage and temporary-living costs. Completing essential rooms first created an earlier drop in the weekly expense rate. The area represented by those later weeks became smaller.

Malik explained the result to his parents without pretending the estimates were exact. Material delays could change. New damage could appear. The calculation clarified the tradeoff; it did not make the decision for them.

The calculation offered no painless choice.

They prioritized the kitchen, primary bedroom, and safe utility access. Malik's room would wait.

He agreed because the decision made sense.

He grieved because the room was his.

## Tuesday, December 23: The Pie Model

Micah decided to bake a pie from an online recipe and treated measuring cups as nonbinding suggestions.

The crust emerged from the oven shaped like a continent nobody recognized. Filling leaned heavily toward one side.

Malik photographed it.

He started to text Nia, **How are you doing? Are you lonely?**

Then he stopped.

The question was not wrong, but he recognized the habit beneath it. Care could become surveillance. Checking on Nia's heartbreak every day could turn him into her primary emotional witness and make his reliability feel like a claim on what happened next.

He sent the photograph instead.

**Micah has invented non-Euclidean pie. Volume may require Calculus II.**

Nia replied with a photograph of a paper bridge collapsing beneath canned beans.

**Structural engineers unavailable. Recommend whipped cream stabilization.**

They exchanged three more jokes and no emotional inventory.

Malik put down the phone and helped Micah reshape the next crust.

Nia returned to wrapping gifts with her cousins.

Connection did not require constant evaluation.

## Wednesday, December 24: Phone in the Other Room

The Reynolds family held its main Christmas dinner on Christmas Eve.

Nia's phone vibrated through appetizers: club messages, grade celebrations, holiday posts, and a notification that Julian had added a new radio recording.

She carried the phone to her bedroom and left it on the dresser.

For the first time all fall, she sat through dinner without being reachable.

Her younger cousins performed a chaotic holiday song. An uncle told the same college story everyone had heard. Denise brought out a dessert that cracked down the center and blamed the oven with great confidence.

Nia was present for all of it.

She still wondered whether Julian had messaged. She still thought about Malik's pie and Keisha's family gathering in Mobile. Being present did not require every other connection to disappear from her mind.

It meant she stopped leaving the table to maintain them.

After dinner, she checked the phone.

There was no message from Julian.

The absence hurt. It also let the boundary remain intact.

## Thursday, December 25: Spring Goals

On Christmas night, Malik and Nia separately wrote spring goals.

Malik used a plain notebook page. His first list began with grades, actuarial preparation, work hours, and financial support for home. He crossed it out and started again.

**People:** call home on planned days when possible; ask before assuming what help is needed; make room for friendships that are not projects.

**Sleep:** protect six and a half hours as a minimum, seven as the goal; reduce something if the schedule makes that impossible.

**Uncertainty:** state what is unknown before building a plan; do not treat incomplete control as personal failure.

Nia wrote in copper ink.

**People:** ask what others need; say what I need before resentment; let friends contribute without directing every contribution.

**Sleep:** stop treating midnight as free real estate.

**Uncertainty:** an open opportunity is not an emergency; a difficult feeling is not a decision deadline.

Neither wrote the other's name.

That omission was not distance. It was space.

The new year would begin with Malik more willing to move forward and Nia still learning that grief could coexist with curiosity about what came next.

Neither knew yet how much she would mind when forward movement stopped meaning her.
