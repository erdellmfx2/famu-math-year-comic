# Week Twenty-Eight: The Room Beyond the Classroom

Dr. Delgado placed the Calculus II test face down on every desk.

"Before you turn it over," he said, "understand the first instruction."

Nia had never enjoyed a sentence that began that way.

"For each integral, identify the method you would use and justify the choice before computing. A correct answer with an unsupported method may lose credit. A correct setup with incomplete computation may earn substantial credit."

Someone near the windows whispered, "Substantial is not a number."

"Neither is panic," Dr. Delgado replied. "Yet many of you assign it great value."

He started the clock.

Nia turned over the paper. The first problem used substitution. The second required integration by parts. The third was a rational expression waiting to be decomposed into partial fractions. The fourth asked for a numerical approximation and an error comparison.

Her mind wanted to sprint. The instructions demanded that it choose a direction first.

## Friday, February 6: Commit Before Comfort

Malik wrote his method choices in the margin before touching a single antiderivative.

Nia wrote one, began computing, doubted herself, and nearly erased everything. She stopped with the eraser pressed against the paper.

Dr. Delgado had not asked them to predict whether the method would feel comfortable. He had asked them to justify why its structure fit.

She left the line and continued.

The numerical-integration problem described changing water depth along a roadway. Exact measurements existed only at fixed intervals. Students could use left endpoints, right endpoints, trapezoids, or Simpson's rule, but they had to explain what information the approximation preserved and what it might miss.

Nia drew the road segments. The problem felt uncomfortably close to her research. One clean number could hide rapid changes between measurements. A method that performed well on a smooth curve might misrepresent a sudden dip beneath an underpass.

She chose Simpson's rule, wrote the conditions under which she trusted it, and named the risk.

Across the aisle, Malik caught himself beginning integration by parts with a choice that made the remaining integral worse. He crossed out the setup and restarted. The clock continued without sympathy.

When Dr. Delgado called time, the room released one collective breath.

"I would like to file a complaint against method selection," Nia said in the hallway.

"On what grounds?" Malik asked.

"It forces me to reveal my decisions before I know whether they work."

"That may be the point."

"I withdraw my request for your opinion."

## Saturday, February 7: The Honest Blank Space

The final partial-fractions problem had survived Nia's test.

She had factored the denominator, written the correct decomposition, and solved two of the constants. Then time ended. Half the page remained white.

At breakfast, students compared answers with the energy of people investigating a crime they had all witnessed differently. One group insisted the last coefficient was negative. Another said the entire denominator had been copied wrong.

Nia refused to check.

"You are showing maturity," Keisha said.

"I am showing fear with excellent posture."

Dr. Delgado returned the tests electronically that afternoon. Nia's score was strong. The incomplete problem lost points, but her setup and explanation earned most of the credit.

His comment read: **Correct structure. Incomplete is preferable to unsupported invention.**

Nia stared at the blank portion of her scanned page.

In the fall, she had filled uncertain spaces with words because an empty answer felt like public evidence that she did not belong. This time, she had shown exactly what she knew and where time had stopped her.

Malik received a slightly higher score and a note warning him that his restart had cost too much time.

"We have opposite problems," Nia said.

"You commit before checking. I check until commitment becomes expensive."

"Together, we form one adequately timed student."

They laughed, then separated for the weekend. Nia and Keisha had conference bags to pack. Malik had already promised Saturday to Simone.

## Sunday, February 8: Imperfect Work, Serious Questions

The Southeastern Mathematics Futures Conference occupied three floors of a downtown convention hotel and smelled like coffee, carpet cleaner, and printer ink.

Nia expected polished graduate research presented by people who had never made sign errors in public. Instead, the first poster she visited belonged to a first-year student studying bus arrival predictions with incomplete location data.

The student's model failed during rush hour.

She showed the failure on the poster.

"Why not remove that plot?" an audience member asked.

"Because that is when riders most need the estimate," the student replied. "If I only report the quiet hours, I am answering an easier question."

At another session, two undergraduates modeled food-pantry demand and admitted that their training data covered only one semester. A judge asked whether the model should guide purchasing. They said not yet, then described the validation they would need.

Nobody laughed at the unfinished work. The questions became more serious because the presenters were honest about it.

Nia filled pages with notes: define the decision, show the failure, name who is missing, do not confuse a visual with evidence, explain why uncertainty matters to a real person.

Keisha attended a session on reproducible computing. She returned carrying a sample data dictionary and the expression of someone who had discovered twelve new ways their project could be inadequate.

"Having fun?" Nia asked.

"Extremely. We need versioned datasets."

"That sounded less like fun and more like a threat."

They stayed until the final student poster closed, surrounded by imperfect work taken seriously.

For the first time, Nia could imagine their project in the room without needing it to pretend it was finished.

## Monday, February 9: Mathematics in the Decision Room

The national-laboratory panel began with a storm map projected across a ballroom screen.

A Delta National Laboratory scientist explained how teams combined weather observations, infrastructure data, computation, social science, and emergency-management knowledge. The mathematical model did not command officials to evacuate. It clarified scenarios, uncertainty, and possible consequences so people could make decisions under pressure.

"Communication is not the decoration added after the mathematics," she said. "If a result cannot be interpreted, challenged, or connected to a decision, the work is unfinished."

Nia wrote the sentence in capital letters.

Another panelist described a technically accurate flood forecast that local officials misunderstood because the uncertainty range appeared as a faint shaded band. Residents saw one bold line and treated it as a guaranteed boundary.

Julian's advice about color suddenly felt less cosmetic. Keisha's insistence on data lineage felt less like documentation and more like trust.

During questions, Nia asked how early-career researchers could contribute without claiming expertise they did not have.

"Join a team where your limits are visible," the scientist said. "Learn to ask what decision your work affects and who carries the cost when it is wrong."

Afterward, Nia stood beneath the conference banner while people streamed toward lunch.

She did not merely want to visit rooms like this. She wanted to become capable of contributing inside them.

The fellowship connected to the spring symposium could place an undergraduate with a national research team. Until that moment, Nia had treated it as a bright possibility attached to good work.

Now it had shape.

She wanted it.

## Tuesday, February 10: Both Parts of the Story

Imani's first softball game began with a routine ground ball rolling beneath her glove.

The error put a runner on base. Two batters later, the runner scored.

Imani returned to the dugout furious, but she did not throw her glove or search the stands for sympathy. She reviewed the play with the infield coach, adjusted her starting depth, and took the field again.

In the seventh inning, McCall-Hart trailed by one with two runners aboard. Imani drove a pitch into the right-center gap. Both runners scored. She reached second standing, shouting toward the dugout as her teammates spilled against the rail.

The Herons won.

A student reporter met her near the field afterward.

"How does it feel to erase that early error with the game-winning double?"

Imani wiped dirt from one forearm. "It did not erase it. The error happened, and the run counted. The double happened too. I adjusted and helped us win. Both are part of the game."

Nia, listening behind the fence, felt the answer settle beside her test and research notes.

Public stories loved replacement: one heroic moment canceling one bad moment, one award proving every earlier struggle worthwhile. But an achievement did not travel backward through time. It added to the story without deleting what came before.

Imani joined the group and pointed at Nia's conference badge. "How was the math convention?"

"Conference."

"Did anybody hit a double?"

"A first-year student defended a failed bus model."

Imani nodded solemnly. "So yes."

## Wednesday, February 11: Say How Much You Want It

Nia and Keisha spread conference notes across their room.

They had collected ideas about validation, reproducible code, audience testing, uncertainty, and multidisciplinary teams. Every page suggested more work. The symposium fellowship application now looked less like a reward at the end of a good presentation and more like a narrow doorway surrounded by excellent people.

"I want it," Nia said.

Keisha continued typing. "We established that in December."

"No. I said I wanted the experience. I said presenting would be valuable either way. Those things are true, but they were also protective."

Keisha looked up.

"I want the fellowship badly enough that losing it will hurt," Nia said. "I want to work with people making infrastructure decisions. I want access to the lab, the mentoring, all of it."

The confession made her feel ambitious and childish at once.

Keisha did not tell her awards were unimportant. "Good."

"Good?"

"Now we can build for the actual standard instead of acting like wanting less makes us noble."

She pointed to their project plan. "Wanting the fellowship means we test harder. We document what fails. We do not overclaim because a dramatic result feels competitive. And if we lose, we call it a loss instead of pretending the experience was secretly all we wanted."

Nia exhaled. "You make emotional honesty sound like project management."

"Everything improves with version control."

They created a new milestone for the campus pitch. Honest ambition did not guarantee the outcome. It clarified what the work required.

## Thursday, February 12: A Choice Without a Scoreboard

Malik had missed the conference.

Months earlier, that fact would have become a private competition in his mind. Nia would have gone somewhere important; he would have felt left behind; every enthusiastic detail would have sounded like evidence that she was moving into a future without him.

This time, he had chosen a planned day with Simone.

They visited a museum exhibit on Black business history, ate lunch without checking the time, and worked on separate applications in a quiet cafe. Malik did not spend the day refreshing conference posts to measure what he was missing.

Thursday evening, Nia showed him her notes at North Star.

"This panelist said communication is part of the mathematics," she said, turning pages quickly. "And one presenter kept the failed rush-hour plot because that was the exact situation the project was supposed to serve."

Malik read the notes with real interest.

"You should use this question," he said, pointing to a line about decision consequences.

Nia waited for him to explain why he had not attended, or for herself to imply that serious students would have gone.

Neither did.

"How was your day with Simone?" she asked.

"Good. She found an exhibit on a mutual-aid bank and spent twenty minutes correcting the wall text."

"That sounds like her."

Malik returned the notebook. "I am glad you went."

"I am glad you had your day."

The choices did not need a scoreboard.

Still, when Nia later passed a campus store overflowing with red hearts, she felt the reversed shape of spring. Malik had chosen someone else without failing her. She wanted a future he did not have to witness for it to be real.

Both truths followed her into Valentine's weekend.
