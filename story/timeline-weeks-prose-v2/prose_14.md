# Week Fourteen: Riemann Sums and Missing Pieces

Nia wore a copper cape to teach Riemann sums.

The cape was not mathematically necessary. She had found it in a costume bin at North Star and decided an outreach night held on Halloween deserved commitment.

At her table, middle-school students received irregular paper masks shaped like heron wings. A square grid lay beneath each mask. Their challenge was to estimate the area using rectangles, then compare estimates made with different widths.

"The mask has curves," a seventh grader named Zoe said. "Rectangles do not fit."

"Exactly," Nia replied. "So we let them fit imperfectly on purpose."

She showed how left-endpoint rectangles missed some regions and covered others that were not part of the mask. Smaller rectangles followed the shape more closely.

"So we are wrong a lot of times," Zoe said, "but in smaller pieces?"

Nia laughed. "That is one way to describe numerical approximation. We make the error visible, then refine it."

Across the room, paper bats hung from the ceiling, volunteers dressed as famous scientists, and Malik helped students calculate candy-bag volume while wearing the smallest possible concession to costume: an indigo tie printed with tiny silver equations.

For two hours, the semester's tension loosened.

Nia remembered that mathematics could feel like an invitation.

## Friday, October 31: Smaller Rectangles

The first outreach session ended with masks taped across the wall in a bright flock.

Zoe's group compared estimates using four, eight, and sixteen rectangles. The numbers approached a stable range without becoming perfectly exact.

"Which estimate is the answer?" another student asked.

"The best one we can defend with this method," Nia said. "And we should say how we made it."

She pushed the idea one step farther. If they kept shrinking the rectangle width while increasing the number of rectangles, the sums could approach a definite integral: an exact mathematical object built from the limit of approximations.

"But our measurements are still imperfect," Zoe said.

"Right. The integral does not magically improve the data we started with. A precise method can still produce a questionable total if pieces are missing."

They tested that warning by removing two width measurements from the mask. One group filled the gaps using nearby values; another left the region uncounted. Their totals differed enough to matter.

Nia wrote both results on the board and labeled the assumptions. Estimating a total from incomplete data was not only about adding rectangles. It was about reporting which pieces had been observed, which had been inferred, and how those choices changed the answer.

She heard Malik's influence in the sentence.

He heard it too. "Reasonable."

"That is the nicest thing you have ever said about my teaching."

"It is not."

"Let me have the moment."

The two of them reset the table for Saturday's family session. Nia told Malik about Dr. Washington's description of model failure. Malik told her the insurance company had accepted two pieces of documentation and requested more for the compressor.

Neither solved the other's problem. The conversation still helped.

When Nia checked her phone, Julian had sent an apology for the laboratory argument and a note saying he would film Saturday's outreach session.

She replied: **Come see the cape before judging it.**

His response arrived immediately: **I am already judging the cape. Affectionately.**

For one evening, the joke felt like them again.

## Saturday, November 1: The Edge in the Joke

Julian arrived forty minutes late to the family outreach session.

Band rehearsal had run long, and campus media had needed equipment returned before he could collect the camera. By the time he entered North Star, Nia and Malik were demonstrating how the rectangle estimates changed with partition size.

Julian filmed from the doorway.

Zoe returned with her mother and pointed at them. "Those are the two math people."

Julian stepped closer. "They do come as a matched set."

His tone was light enough for the family to laugh.

Nia heard the edge.

Malik did too.

"We are accountability partners," Nia said.

"I know," Julian replied, still smiling behind the camera. "Very accountable."

The session moved on, but the joke stayed in the room. Nia became overly formal with Malik. Malik moved to another table. Julian filmed more footage than necessary and disliked himself for every extra minute.

After the families left, he lowered the camera.

"That came out wrong."

"It came out exactly how you meant it for about two seconds," Nia said. "Then you regretted it."

Julian rubbed one hand over the back of his neck. "Maybe."

"I am not doing this here."

She carried the mask supplies toward storage.

Malik watched her go, then looked at Julian. "Can we talk?"

## Sunday, November 2: Not a Competition

Malik met Julian outside the union Sunday afternoon.

He had no interest in winning an argument about Nia. Winning would be the wrong goal.

"I am not trying to interfere in your relationship," Malik said.

Julian leaned against the terrace rail. "I did not say you were."

"You implied that our friendship needs explaining."

"You two have a connection."

"Yes. We are friends. We study together. We care about similar work."

"And you have feelings for her."

Malik's silence answered too much.

Julian looked away toward Cypress Walk. "I am not imagining everything."

"No," Malik said carefully. "But Nia is with you. I have respected that. She should not have to make our friendship smaller to prove your relationship is real."

"Easy for you to say. You get to be the understanding friend while I am the jealous boyfriend."

"I am not especially understanding. Ask anyone."

Julian almost smiled.

Malik continued. "This is not a competition I am entering. If you are worried about what Nia needs, ask her. Do not make her defend me."

"And what do you need?"

Malik thought of the photo beneath the Delta sign, the bus conversation, and every private hope he had treated like patience.

"Better boundaries," he said.

It was not a complete answer. It was the answer he could responsibly give.

## Monday, November 3: The Drop

The rivalry game against Bayland State began with DJ dropping a pass.

The ball hit both hands on a short third-down route and fell to the turf. The crowd groaned. DJ returned to the sideline with every camera in the stadium apparently pointed at his failure.

His receivers coach met him near the bench. "What happened?"

"Looked upfield before I secured it."

"Fix your eyes. Next play."

No speech. No punishment. No promise of another target.

DJ spent two quarters blocking, running clear-out routes, and trying not to beg the quarterback with his body language.

In the fourth quarter, McCall-Hart trailed by four. On fourth-and-five, DJ ran a crossing route beneath the linebackers. The pass arrived low. He bent, caught it cleanly, and absorbed the tackle beyond the marker.

First down.

The drive stayed alive. Two plays later, McCall-Hart scored the winning touchdown.

DJ's conversion did not erase the drop. The drop did not erase the conversion. Both belonged to the same game and the same player.

In the locker room, he texted Malik: **I HAVE EXPERIENCED THE FULL EMOTIONAL RANGE OF RECEIVING.**

Malik replied: **Secure the message before looking upfield.**

DJ sent back twelve angry symbols and saved the text anyway.

## Tuesday, November 4: Advance

The Heron Analytics team received its regional decision at 10:03 Tuesday morning.

**Selected: Regional Semifinal Presentation**

Simone read the message aloud in the lab. Andre shouted. Priya covered her face. Dr. Bennett permitted exactly thirty seconds of noise before asking when the presentation file was due.

Malik opened the schedule.

Nine days.

His first instinct was not celebration. It was calculation.

Presentation revision, rehearsals, classes, North Star shifts, family calls, make-up work, travel preparation. Nine days became blocks, then hours, then a narrowing path between obligations.

Simone closed his laptop.

"We advanced," she said. "Experience the event before you convert it into a countdown."

Malik looked around the room. The model had survived review because the team showed uncertainty instead of hiding it. They had earned the chance to be questioned in person.

He allowed himself one full minute.

Then he reopened the schedule.

"Hopeless," Simone said.

"Prepared."

"Those words are cousins, not twins."

## Wednesday, November 5: Public Laughter

The basketball exhibition at Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse introduced the men's and women's teams before the season.

Nia attended with Julian and several of his media friends. She wanted an ordinary night: snacks, music, friendly arguments about which team had the better entrance.

Instead, one of Julian's friends mentioned the Delta photo.

"Your girl looked real happy at the lab," Marcus joked. "Math husband in the frame and everything."

Nia waited for Julian to stop it.

He laughed once. "Apparently accountability is a full-contact partnership."

The group laughed.

Nia did not.

"Can we not?" she said.

Julian noticed her expression. "It is a joke."

"It is our argument performed for your friends."

"Nobody knows our argument."

"They know enough to laugh at me."

Marcus began apologizing, but Julian tried to smooth the moment with another joke about mathematicians taking everything literally.

Nia stood.

"Where are you going?"

"Somewhere I do not have to become content before I can have a feeling."

She left before the women's team scrimmage began.

Julian remained in his seat, surrounded by friends who suddenly found the court very interesting.

## Thursday, November 6: Known in Private

Keisha found Nia sitting on the floor between their beds, still wearing the exhibition wristband.

"Do you need soft or useful?" Keisha asked.

"Soft first."

Keisha sat beside her without speaking.

After a while, Nia said, "Julian tells people he is proud of me. He posts me. He introduces me like I am brilliant. Everybody thinks he adores me."

"Does he?"

"I think so."

"That was not the whole question."

Nia pulled at the paper wristband. "Being loved in public is not helping me feel known in private."

She explained the joke, the friends, and how quickly Julian had protected the mood of the group instead of the dignity of their conversation.

"I also keep score," Nia admitted. "I count every late arrival and missed date. Then I act like I am above the performance because I am the one hurt by it."

Keisha leaned back against the bed. "Do you want the relationship, or do you want to prove it can work?"

Nia's eyes filled.

"I do not know anymore."

Outside, campus workers removed the last Halloween decorations. Ordinary November returned, carrying a withdrawal deadline, a semifinal, and choices that could no longer be disguised as scheduling problems.
