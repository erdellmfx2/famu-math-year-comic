# Week One: Two Kinds of Plans

The first thing Malik Baptiste discovered about Bellwether, Alabama, was that the heat did not simply sit on you. It negotiated.

It followed him from the rideshare drop-off to the limestone steps of Eliza Moss Hall, offering sweat in exchange for every box he carried. It fogged the edge of his glasses while he tried to balance a laptop bag, a duffel, and the promise he had made before leaving Gulfport: *I have the money part handled.*

Inside, the Quantitative Summer Bridge welcome room was cool enough to make him forgive the entire city.

Malik chose a table near an outlet, opened his laptop, and pulled up the family budget he had finished three nights ago. He only meant to check one thing. Then he found the mistake.

The repair estimate for his mother's car had been entered in the monthly column instead of the emergency-expense column. Correcting it pushed the August balance below zero.

Not far below zero. But zero did not care how close you came.

He stared at the red number as students entered around him in bright shirts, fresh sneakers, and the nervous confidence of people pretending not to look for somewhere safe to sit.

"That spreadsheet looks like it just told you a family secret."

Malik looked up.

The girl beside the chair across from him wore copper-colored headphones around her neck and carried a notebook thick with tabs. Her smile was curious, not cruel.

"It made an accusation," Malik said.

"Serious one?"

"Misplaced expense."

She pressed a hand to her chest. "In broad daylight?"

He almost smiled. Almost.

She pointed at the chair. "Can I sit here, or is it reserved for the audit committee?"

"Go ahead."

She slid into the seat. "Nia Reynolds. Mathematical sciences. Huntsville. I like graphs when they tell the truth and people when they try to."

"Malik Baptiste. Actuarial science. Gulfport. I like complete introductions."

"That was complete. You know my major, my hometown, and my entire moral philosophy."

This time, he did smile.

Around them, the room filled with the sound of new beginnings: chairs scraping, families calling with last instructions, staff passing out indigo folders stamped with a copper heron. Through the tall windows, McCall-Hart University glowed honey-gold in the morning light. Malik had studied the campus map for two weeks, but a map had not prepared him for how old and alive the place felt.

Nia tilted her head toward his screen. "Do you need help?"

His first instinct was no. His second was also no, but with better manners.

"I can fix it," he said.

"I didn't ask if you could."

That answer stayed with him longer than it should have.

## Friday, August 1: The Red Number

The Bridge program opened with introductions, placement details, and a challenge from Dr. Camille Brooks, a calculus professor with silver-framed glasses and the calm voice of someone who could hear weak reasoning from across a building.

"This program is not here to prove whether you belong," she told the room. "Admission settled that question. We are here to learn how you think, where your preparation is strong, and where we need to build."

Malik wrote the sentence down exactly.

Nia drew a box around *where we need to build* and added a tiny crane beside it.

Their first activity was simple: examine a table showing hours worked, transportation costs, and weekly take-home pay. Identify the variables. Describe a possible relationship. State what the table did not reveal.

Malik listed the inputs, output, units, and likely errors before Nia had finished drawing arrows.

"Why are you decorating the data?" he asked.

"I'm showing the situation."

"The situation is in the columns."

"No, the numbers are in the columns. The situation is a person trying to get to work."

Malik glanced at the prompt. "It doesn't say that."

"Exactly. We don't know whether the transportation cost is gas, a bus pass, rideshare money, or paying somebody's cousin. That changes what choices are possible."

He disliked how quickly her answer improved his.

When Dr. Brooks asked for observations, Malik explained the numeric pattern. Nia followed by naming the missing context. Dr. Brooks nodded at both of them.

"A clean calculation built on an invisible assumption is still an unstable model," she said. "Make your assumptions visible."

Malik underlined that sentence too.

At lunch, Nia found him correcting the family budget.

"You fixed the accusation?"

"I moved the expense, reduced two categories, and added a warning rule."

"So your spreadsheet can yell before the problem gets bad."

"It does not yell. It changes color."

"Corporate yelling."

He showed her the repaired balance. He did not show her the text from his mother asking whether everything still worked. Some truths felt easier in formulas than in conversation.

## Saturday, August 2: Room at the Table

Saturday's Bridge session was optional, which meant Nia arrived ten minutes late carrying iced coffee and insisting that optional mornings should begin at noon.

Malik had already claimed the same table.

"Look at us," she said, sitting across from him. "A tradition."

"It has happened twice."

"Every tradition starts before somebody puts it on a T-shirt."

They worked through readiness problems until Nia noticed Malik checking the budget between questions. He minimized it.

"You do that every time somebody walks by," she said.

"Do what?"

"Hide the sheet."

"It is private."

"Private is fine. Panicked is different."

Malik's shoulders tightened. Nia saw it and leaned back.

"Sorry," she said. "That came out like I had earned access. I haven't."

The quick apology surprised him more than the question.

He reopened the file. "My family budget was balanced when I left. Now it isn't. I told them it was."

"Was that a lie?"

"No."

"Then it was a model with new information."

"That sounds like something people say to feel better about being wrong."

"Sometimes. It can also be true."

She took out her notebook and drew two boxes. In the first, she wrote *what changed*. In the second, *what can change next*.

Malik looked at the page. "That is not a budget."

"Correct. It is me stopping you from staring at one red number like it has been elected president of your life."

He laughed loudly enough that two students turned around.

Together they found a duplicate textbook estimate and a subscription Malik could pause. The balance returned to black, but that was not the part he remembered. He remembered that Nia had known when to ask, when to back up, and when to make him laugh.

## Sunday, August 3: Draw It First

Their diagnostic modeling task arrived Sunday afternoon.

A neighborhood food cooperative wanted to offer produce boxes at several prices. Students had to create a model showing how the number of subscribers could affect weekly revenue. The prompt included a tiny data table and just enough ambiguity to start an argument.

Malik built formulas immediately.

Nia turned the page sideways and drew three families standing near a bus stop.

"We have forty minutes," he said.

"I know."

"Then why are there people?"

"Because people buy the boxes."

"The model needs variables."

"And the variables need meaning."

He pointed to his screen. "Subscribers, price, revenue."

She pointed to her drawing. "Distance, pickup time, household size. If the pickup site is hard to reach, lowering the price might not increase subscribers the way we expect."

"None of that is in the data."

"Then we write that it is not in the data."

For five stubborn minutes, they worked beside each other instead of together. Malik's table became neat and uselessly confident. Nia's sketch became thoughtful and mathematically unfinished.

Finally, Malik rotated his laptop toward her. "Suppose we label my formula a first model and list your factors as limitations."

Nia rotated her notebook toward him. "Suppose we also explain what information we would collect next."

"That is outside the prompt."

"It is also a good idea."

He sighed. "Fine."

Their final answer was the strongest in the room.

Dr. Brooks praised the explicit function, then tapped the limitations section. "This is where calculation becomes judgment."

Nia nudged Malik with her elbow.

"Do not celebrate," he whispered.

"I am celebrating us. Different thing."

He pretended to review the equation so she would not see how much he liked the word *us*.

## Monday, August 4: Why Math Matters

On Monday, Dr. Brooks closed the classroom door and asked everyone to put away their calculators.

"Today," she said, "I want the answer nobody can compute for you. Why does mathematics matter?"

The room went quiet in the special way classrooms do when a question becomes personal.

Students spoke about engineering, medicine, money, teaching, technology, and being the first in their families to pursue a technical degree.

When Malik's turn came, he folded his hands on the desk.

"My father can repair almost anything," he said. "Engines, roofs, appliances. But one unexpected bill can undo months of planning. I want to understand risk well enough to help families and businesses prepare without pretending every loss can be prevented."

The answer came out more honest than he intended. He looked down after finishing.

Nia did not make a joke.

When her turn came, she said, "My aunt runs a neighborhood tutoring program. Every year she has to prove the same thing to different people: that kids are learning, that the program matters, that the money should continue. I want to use math to make important things harder to ignore."

After class, they walked down Eliza Moss Hall's broad staircase.

"You want to make risk visible," Nia said.

"You want to make people visible."

"That sounded like the end of a movie trailer."

"I regret saying it."

"Too late. I heard the music."

## Tuesday, August 5: The Campus Has a Memory

The campus tour began at Jubilee Library, moved through the glass-walled North Star Learning Commons, and ended beneath the live oaks along Cypress Walk.

Nia photographed everything: the copper-framed windows of Eliza Moss Hall, the old reading room inside Jubilee, the wall of whiteboards at North Star, even a campus cat sleeping under a bench as though it had tenure.

Malik photographed the map.

"You know they give us that online," Nia said.

"Phones lose signal."

"Paper maps exist."

"Paper gets wet."

"Do you prepare for joy like this too?"

He considered it. "I have not had to."

Their guide led them to Founders' Bell, greened with age and suspended beneath a limestone arch. It had once called students to assembly, she explained. Now it rang at Opening Convocation and commencement, marking the entrance and exit of a McCall-Hart education.

Nia looked up at it. "Imagine hearing that on your last day here."

Malik looked down Cypress Walk, where indigo banners shifted in the humid breeze. Four years seemed both enormous and dangerously brief.

"We have to make it to the first day first," he said.

"You really refuse to skip steps."

"Steps exist for a reason."

"So do leaps."

He glanced at her. "Leaps are just steps with poor documentation."

Nia laughed so hard the tour guide turned around.

## Wednesday, August 6: Indigo Night

Indigo Night transformed Hart Student Union into a festival of music, food, club tables, and first-year students trying out possible versions of themselves.

Nia moved through it like a spark in dry grass. She signed an interest card for the Association for Women in Mathematics, took a flyer from Math Circle, promised to visit campus radio, and nearly volunteered for a service event before Malik removed the pen from her hand.

"You cannot join the whole university tonight."

"Watch me."

"I am watching. It is concerning."

Across the terrace, an alto saxophone climbed above the crowd. A small group from the Marching Herons was performing beside the radio booth. The saxophonist played with his eyes half closed, then opened them at the exact moment Nia stepped closer.

After the set, he introduced himself as Julian Cross, second-year media arts student, campus radio host, and, according to him, "occasional rescuer of boring events."

"This event was not boring," Nia said.

"Then I can take the night off."

His confidence should have been irritating. Somehow, it was not.

Julian told her about a radio showcase on Friday and invited her to stop by. Nia said yes with the speed she used for nearly every interesting opportunity.

At another table, Malik found a sign for the Heron Analytics Challenge. Fourth-down decisions. Player performance. Ticket demand. Real datasets, competitive teams, regional presentations.

He read every line twice before writing his name.

When he and Nia met again near the Copper Cup Cafe, they both had news.

"I got invited to the radio showcase," Nia said.

"I signed up for an analytics information session."

"Look at us choosing things."

"You chose six things."

"Seven, technically."

He shook his head, but his attention drifted to Julian across the room. Julian saw Nia and lifted two fingers in a casual salute. She smiled back.

Malik did not yet have a name for the small drop inside his chest. He treated it like a number too minor to enter.

## Thursday, August 7: Accountability

Their final Bridge presentation returned to the budget model Malik had repaired, expanded into a scenario for a first-year student managing books, meals, transportation, and emergencies.

Malik explained the spreadsheet logic. Nia told the story of what happened when one assumption changed. They showed the original error, the revised categories, and a sensitivity table that made visible how quickly a small surprise could become a larger problem.

"A budget is not a promise that nothing will go wrong," Nia told the room. "It is a way to notice change early enough to make choices."

Malik advanced the slide. "And a model should show what it assumes, so the person using it knows when the answer no longer applies."

Dr. Brooks smiled from the back row.

Afterward, Malik and Nia carried their laptops to the Founders' Bell. The campus was copper in the late sun, and somewhere near the union a drumline practiced the same eight measures until they became part of the air.

"We work well together," Nia said.

"After arguing."

"During arguing. The arguing is part of the system."

"That sounds unhealthy."

"Only because you hate being challenged by someone delightful."

Malik leaned against the limestone arch. "We should keep each other on schedule when classes start. Weekly check-in. Assignments, deadlines, major commitments."

Nia held out her hand. "Accountability partners. Academic honesty, honest schedules, and emergency snack intervention."

"Define emergency."

"If you have to ask, you are already in one."

He shook her hand.

Then Nia mentioned that Julian had sent her the details for Friday's showcase.

"I think he might actually be asking me out," she said, trying and failing to sound casual.

Malik released her hand and adjusted the strap of his laptop bag.

"You should go," he said. His voice was kind, steady, and almost completely convincing. "You wanted to see the radio station."

Nia studied him for half a second, then smiled. "I did."

They walked away from the bell together, carrying two kinds of plans into the next week: the ones they had said aloud and the ones they had not yet admitted, even to themselves.
