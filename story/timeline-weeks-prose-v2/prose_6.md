# Week Six: Continuity Under Pressure

Malik's first mistake as a trial member looked beautiful.

The chart used McCall-Hart indigo for successful plays, copper for failures, and pale gray for neutral outcomes. Labels aligned. Axes began at defensible values. The title stated exactly what the image claimed to show: third-down conversion rate by distance and formation.

Malik had checked every calculation twice.

He had not checked why one stretch of the season contained almost no data.

Simone enlarged the chart on the Quantitative Futures Lab screen. The team sat around the long table with laptops open and Friday-afternoon fatigue settling over the room.

"What happened here?" she asked.

Malik followed the cursor to the gap.

"Low sample size," he said.

"Why?"

"The source file has fewer tagged plays for those games."

"Why?"

He opened the raw table. Several rows included play descriptions and outcomes but no formation label. His filter had removed them automatically.

"Missing category values," he said.

"How many?"

Malik counted. The room seemed to grow quieter with each row.

"Forty-three."

Nearly one fifth of the relevant plays had disappeared from the visualization. The chart was mathematically accurate about the records it included and dangerously silent about the ones it did not.

Simone stepped away from the screen.

"Explain that to the team."

Malik wished she had yelled. Anger would have let him defend himself. Her calm left him alone with the facts.

## Friday, September 5: The Missing Fifth

Malik stood beside the screen.

"I filtered out rows without formation labels," he said. "I treated the result as a complete subset instead of reporting how much information the filter removed. The conversion rates are correct for labeled plays, but the chart does not show whether the missing plays differ systematically."

Dr. Bennett folded his arms. "What could make them systematic?"

"Tagging changed between staff exports. Certain game files may be less complete. If the missing labels cluster by opponent, game situation, or personnel group, the displayed rates could be biased."

"And did you check?" Simone asked.

"No."

The word hurt more than a complicated explanation would have.

A senior team member named Andre asked whether the chart should be discarded.

Malik wanted to say no. He wanted to defend the hours behind the colors and labels.

"Not necessarily," he said. "But it cannot be used as submitted. I need to map the missing rows, recover labels from film where possible, and show uncertainty where they cannot be recovered."

Dr. Bennett nodded. "Do that."

After the meeting, Malik closed his laptop with careful hands.

Simone remained by the screen. "You think I embarrassed you."

"You asked me to explain an error in front of the team."

"Yes. Because the team might use your work. They need to know what failed and whether you understand it."

"You could have told them."

"Then they would know that I understand your mistake. The point was to find out whether you do."

Malik looked at the blank screen.

"You explained it clearly," Simone said. "Now rebuild it clearly."

That was not forgiveness. It was something more useful: another chance with conditions.

## Saturday, September 6: Before the Shortcut

On Saturday, Dr. Brooks filled the board with secant lines.

She began with two points on a curve and the familiar formula for average rate of change. Then she moved one point closer to the other. The secant line shifted. The interval narrowed. The average rate approached something students recognized but had not yet been allowed to name casually.

"The derivative," someone said.

Dr. Brooks turned. "Eventually. First, tell me what has to happen."

The class worked through the difference quotient. Every time someone tried to jump to a memorized rule, Dr. Brooks returned them to the limit.

Malik's patience thinned.

"If we know the power rule is coming," he said, "why spend this long deriving it?"

Dr. Brooks rested the chalk against the board. "Because a shortcut is only trustworthy when you understand the road it shortens."

The sentence struck the sore place left by his chart.

She reduced the interval again. The secant line approached the tangent line, and average change approached instantaneous change.

"We are not plugging in zero," Nia said, thinking aloud. "We are asking what the quotient approaches as the interval becomes arbitrarily small."

"Exactly," Dr. Brooks said. "The expression at zero may be undefined while the limiting behavior gives us the rate."

Malik copied each step. His chart had offered a shortcut too: a polished picture that skipped the road from raw data to conclusion. He had trusted the destination because it looked right.

After class, Nia waited while he packed.

"You were quiet today."

"Analytics error."

"Bad?"

"Visible."

She nodded as if that answered the real question. "Want help?"

"Not with the chart."

"I can provide company first and help later. I hear that is a service we offer."

Malik smiled. "Company would be fine."

## Sunday, September 7: The Choice Nobody Requested

Nia had reserved Sunday afternoon for calculus review.

At 1:40, Julian texted that the band had unexpectedly gained rehearsal time in the stadium. At 1:42, he added: **No pressure, but if you are nearby, I can see you during break.**

Nia looked at the derivative problems on her desk.

She imagined Julian alone during break, though he had not said he would be alone. She imagined him disappointed, though his message had contained no disappointment. She imagined a good girlfriend would show up.

At 1:48, she closed the book.

The stadium was hot, the rehearsal ran late, and Julian's break lasted nine minutes. He was happy to see her. He shared his water, asked about calculus, and returned to the field when the whistle blew.

Nia sat for another hour waiting for the next break that never came.

By dinner, she was behind, hungry, and irritated.

Julian met her outside the stadium. "I thought you had a study block."

"I moved it."

"You did not have to do that."

"I know."

"Then why do you sound mad at me?"

"I do not."

Julian gave her a look that made clear his audio training could detect tone.

Nia shifted her bag. "I came all the way over here and barely saw you."

"I said I had rehearsal."

"You said I could come."

"I also said no pressure."

The conversation stood at the edge of becoming an argument.

Julian lowered his voice. "I was glad you came. But I did not ask you to give up your afternoon."

Nia knew he was right, which made her feel worse.

"I need to study," she said.

"Okay."

He did not chase her. She resented that for half a block before realizing how unfair the resentment was.

## Monday, September 8: Availability Is Not Affection

Keisha found Nia asleep over an open calculus book Monday morning.

"This is not studying," she said. "This is decorative unconsciousness."

Nia lifted her head. A limit expression had printed itself faintly on her cheek.

"I went to rehearsal."

"Were you performing?"

"No."

"Then why?"

Nia explained the text, the nine-minute break, and the argument that technically had not become an argument.

Keisha listened while packing her track bag.

"You volunteered your study time," she said, "then charged Julian emotionally when the thing he told you would happen happened."

"That sounds harsh."

"Do you want soft or useful? I can schedule soft tonight."

Nia dropped her face into her hands. "Useful."

Keisha sat across from her. "You keep treating availability like proof of affection. If you are always reachable, always helping, always rearranging, then nobody can say you did not care."

"I do care."

"I know. But care is not the same as having no boundaries."

Nia looked at her crowded planner. "What if I say no too often and people stop asking?"

"Then they wanted access, not you."

The answer was simple enough to frighten her.

Keisha tapped the missed study block. "Restore it. Tell Julian. Do not make it dramatic."

Nia moved the review session to Thursday evening and marked it in indigo.

"Why indigo?" Keisha asked.

"Protected time."

"Good. Now wash the equation off your face."

## Tuesday, September 9: Number Thirty-Seven

McCall-Hart's first road game unfolded badly.

The offense stalled. The defense spent too long on the field. By the third quarter, the Herons trailed by thirteen and the visiting section had reduced its optimism to stubborn noise.

DJ stood near the special-teams coordinator wearing number thirty-seven, waiting through every return call.

Then a second-year player cramped after a long coverage sprint.

"Cole!" the coordinator shouted.

DJ's body moved before his fear could vote.

He ran onto the field for the kickoff. Across the formation, the returner shifted his weight. DJ remembered the film: this unit liked to create a seam behind the second blocker, then cut toward the sideline.

The ball rose beneath the stadium lights.

DJ sprinted, avoided the first block, and saw the cut before it happened. He reached the returner near the numbers and struck through the tackle exactly as coaches had drilled. The ball came loose.

For one impossible second, it rested on the turf with twenty-two people changing direction around it.

A McCall-Hart player recovered.

The visiting section erupted.

The Herons failed to turn the possession into enough points. They lost by six. There was no miracle comeback, no score that transformed the night into a clean victory.

But in the locker room, the coordinator found DJ.

"Good read. Better finish," he said. "Be ready next week."

DJ texted Malik from the bus: **WE LOST BUT I FORCED IT OUT. TELL THE ROOM I AM A COLLEGE ATHLETE NOW.**

Malik replied: **The room was already aware. Your laundry confirms it.**

Then he watched the replay link three times and smiled every time number thirty-seven crossed the screen.

## Wednesday, September 10: Show the Weakness

Malik rebuilt the analytics chart from the raw data outward.

He recovered eleven formation labels from game film. He separated the remaining missing rows by opponent and date. He added a visible bar showing data completeness for each game and replaced single conversion rates with intervals where sample sizes were small.

The new chart was less beautiful.

It was also more honest.

At Wednesday's review, Malik stood beside the screen again.

"The missing labels cluster in two early exports," he explained. "That means the original omission was not random. The revised view shows observed rates, available sample sizes, and the percentage of plays with usable formation tags. Where the evidence is weak, the chart says so."

Andre asked whether the result could still guide decisions.

"It can guide what film to review," Malik said. "It should not independently determine a play call."

Simone studied the uncertainty labels. "What did you give up?"

"A simpler story."

"And what did you gain?"

Malik looked at the team. "A claim we can defend."

Dr. Bennett nodded.

After the meeting, Simone caught up with Malik on the Porter Center steps.

"That was good work."

"The first version was not."

"I am aware. I was there."

He glanced at her.

"Respect is not believing somebody never makes mistakes," she said. "It is knowing what they do after one."

Malik felt the sentence settle somewhere deeper than praise.

## Thursday, September 11: Protected Time

Nia met Julian outside Bellwether Media House on Thursday with her planner in her bag and Keisha's voice in her head.

"I was unfair Sunday," she said.

Julian nodded slowly. "A little."

"You told me rehearsal might only give us a few minutes. I canceled my study block anyway, then acted like you made the choice."

"I could have been clearer about how busy it would be."

"You said 'no pressure.' That is clear in at least two languages."

He smiled, but let her continue.

"I like showing up for you," Nia said. "But I cannot organize my whole schedule around surprise rehearsal time. I have a protected study block tonight. If you get free, I am still studying."

Julian considered this for so little time that Nia wondered whether he had heard her.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Yes. You should study. I have editing to do anyway."

"You are not upset?"

"Nia, I like you. I do not need you sitting alone in stadium seats to prove it."

She stared at him.

"What?"

"I prepared for that to be harder."

"I can argue if it helps."

"No, thank you."

Julian took her hand. "Tell me when time is protected. I will believe you. I need you to believe me when I say I am busy too."

"Deal."

At North Star that evening, Nia completed the review problems she had postponed. Malik sat two tables away, revising analytics notes. Their first calculus exam had been announced, and neither knew yet whether the lessons they had learned outside class had arrived early enough to protect their grades.

For the moment, the room was quiet.

Nia kept the time she had promised herself. Malik labeled the weakness in his model instead of hiding it. Across campus, DJ replayed his forced fumble while pretending not to.

Continuity did not mean nothing changed. It meant that when pressure arrived, the pieces still connected.
