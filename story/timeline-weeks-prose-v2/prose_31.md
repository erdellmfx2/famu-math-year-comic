# Week Thirty-One: What Converges

Nia drew a warning network across the whiteboard.

The first alert reached half the households. If each reached household informed one unreached neighbor, the next round reached half of whoever remained. Then half of the new remainder. Then half again.

"Geometric series," Malik said.

"Exactly. One-half plus one-fourth plus one-eighth, continuing toward one."

On paper, the warning reached almost everyone as the number of rounds increased. The total approached one hundred percent with satisfying obedience.

Keisha turned her laptop around.

"The real access survey does not behave like your board."

Households with stable internet access received alerts at much higher rates. Households relying on prepaid mobile service, shared devices, or intermittent connections did not simply join the next round at the same probability. Some remained outside every update.

Nia erased the final arrow pointing toward one hundred percent.

"So the series converges," she said, "but to the wrong promise."

## Friday, February 27: The Missing Remainder

Dr. Delgado introduced geometric series by dropping a rubber ball.

Each bounce reached a fixed fraction of the previous height. Students calculated the total vertical distance the ball traveled if the pattern continued forever. The answer was finite, though the motion was described with infinitely many pieces.

"Infinite activity does not require an infinite total," he said. "But you must justify the ratio and its range."

Nia adapted the structure to repeated warning reach. The first model assumed every unreached household had the same chance of receiving the next message. It was elegant, computable, and unsupported.

Keisha separated the population into access groups. Julian's message test suggested that receiving an alert did not guarantee understanding it. A warning filled with unfamiliar abbreviations could arrive technically and fail practically.

"We need at least three states," Keisha said. "Not reached, reached but unclear, reached and understood."

"And trust," Nia added. "Somebody can understand a message and still not act on it."

Their clean series became several interacting sequences with uncertain transition rates.

Alexis reviewed the revision and asked where the rates came from.

"Campus volunteers and published communication studies," Nia said.

"Then do not call them community behavior. Campus volunteers are not a substitute for Bellwether neighborhoods."

Nia added a limitation in bold.

The model could demonstrate sensitivity. It could not claim to predict every household. The remainder was not merely a mathematical quantity. It represented people the system might repeatedly fail to reach.

## Saturday, February 28: What People Actually Trust

Dr. Price approved a small set of recorded community interviews under strict limits.

Julian explained the project and consent form before turning on any equipment. Participants chose whether their names, voices, or only anonymous notes could be used. They could stop at any time. The team asked about how people received severe-weather information, what made a warning credible, and what details they needed before changing plans.

The answers disrupted nearly every convenient assumption.

One resident trusted local radio more than phone alerts because storms often interrupted service. Another waited for a sister who worked at the hospital to confirm official messages. A parent said an instruction to "evacuate immediately" was useless without information about transportation and where children could be collected. An older man kept a weather radio but distrusted maps that did not show street names he recognized.

"If City Hall tells you to leave, would you?" Nia asked one participant.

"Depends who at City Hall is talking and whether my neighbor got the same message," she replied.

Trust was not a switch. It had history.

Julian recorded only what each person allowed. Keisha kept consent status separate from research notes. Nia resisted translating every answer into a parameter before the speaker finished.

Afterward, Julian packed the microphone.

"Your model keeps asking when information arrives," he said. "They keep answering who it arrives from."

Nia looked at the interview cards. "We treated the source like a label."

"To them, the source is part of the message."

The project had gathered richer evidence and become less certain. That was not a contradiction. It was what listening had done.

## Sunday, March 1: Winning With the Unknowns Visible

The campus semifinal took place in a lecture hall with a timer large enough to threaten the entire front row.

Nia opened with the original problem: uncertain road capacities changed route recommendations. Keisha demonstrated the iterative model. Julian's animation showed alerts arriving at different times and routes shifting as congestion grew.

Then Nia displayed a slide titled **WHAT WE DO NOT YET KNOW**.

They did not know reliable trust rates for Bellwether neighborhoods. Their interviews were too small to estimate community behavior. They could not represent every transportation constraint. They had not validated the update model against a real emergency.

One judge asked whether those gaps made the project premature.

"They make any broad prediction premature," Nia said. "But the sensitivity analysis is useful now because it shows which assumptions can reverse a recommendation. We are not presenting one evacuation answer. We are identifying where an answer becomes unstable and what evidence would be needed to improve it."

Keisha explained how the software preserved scenario histories instead of overwriting inconvenient runs. Julian described message testing without claiming to represent all residents.

The judges advanced them to the regional presentation stage.

For several seconds, Nia forgot every lesson about dignified academic conduct and bounced beside Keisha in the hallway.

"We won by listing everything wrong with us," she said.

"We advanced because we knew what the model could support."

"That is the mature wording. Mine has more rhythm."

The celebration was real. So were the unknowns. Neither canceled the other.

## Monday, March 2: One Final, Two Endings

The rivalry basketball doubleheader filled Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse in indigo and copper.

The women's game came first. Imani led chants from the student section while Keisha, Nia, Julian, Malik, Simone, and DJ crowded one row. McCall-Hart's women defended relentlessly, forcing a late turnover and scoring in transition to secure the win.

The fieldhouse shook.

Thirty minutes later, the men's rivalry finale began with the same crowd expecting the same ending.

It did not arrive.

McCall-Hart trailed by one with six seconds left. Their guard drove, kicked the ball to the corner, and a senior took an open shot at the buzzer.

The ball struck the rim and bounced away.

For one confused moment, cheers from the visiting section collided with the silence around them. The senior stood beneath the basket staring at the floor. His teammates reached him before the cameras did.

DJ exhaled. "Same building. Completely different walk home."

The women's team crossed the court carrying their rivalry trophy. The men's team disappeared into the tunnel.

Campus social media immediately combined the results into one graphic: **HERON BASKETBALL SPLITS RIVALRY FINALE.**

Nia disliked the word *splits*. It made triumph and disappointment sound like equal halves of one tidy object.

The people living each result did not experience a split. They experienced a win and a loss, each complete.

## Tuesday, March 3: Let Both Results Be Full

The next day's celebration honored the women's team in the student center.

Some students complained that the event should be quieter because the men's team had lost. Others spoke about the men's missed shot as if the women's trophy repaired it.

Imani rejected both ideas.

"The women do not have to shrink their win to protect anybody," she said. "And their win does not mean the men should stop hurting."

She helped hang a banner for the women's team, then delivered handwritten notes to two men's seniors whose final home game had ended at the rim.

Nia thought about convergent series. Mathematics could combine infinitely many terms into one total. Human experience resisted that kind of compression. A shared crowd could hold two results without averaging them into emotional neutrality.

At lunch, Simone congratulated Nia on the regional advancement.

"You should be proud," she said.

"I am. I am also aware that our trust model is currently held together by caveats."

"Those can both be true."

Nia noticed how naturally Simone made room for more than one truth. She understood why Malik valued her.

The realization no longer felt like a challenge to win. It felt like evidence Nia had to respect.

## Wednesday, March 4: The Interview Becomes Real

Simone received the London interview invitation between classes.

She read the email once, walked directly to Malik, and handed him her phone.

"Next Wednesday," she said. "Video interview. Two case questions and a behavioral panel."

Malik hugged her before speaking. "Congratulations."

She held him tightly, then stepped back. Excitement made her eyes brighter. Fear sharpened everything else.

"I need to tell you something before we start planning," she said. "I cannot promise what I will decide about us this summer before I even have an offer."

Malik felt the old spreadsheet opening inside his mind.

"I am not asking for a promise tonight."

"Not tonight. But you keep talking as if distance is a problem with a solution, and I do not know whether I want our first months together to become a maintenance project across time zones."

"So you are already deciding against it?"

"No. I am refusing to decide from fear before I know what the actual choice is."

Malik wanted certainty because uncertainty felt passive. Simone wanted room because premature certainty felt like surrendering ownership of her future.

They scheduled interview practice for the weekend.

The calendar entry was easy.

Everything beneath it was not.

## Thursday, March 5: Pleasant Semester or Shared Plan

Malik asked the question during a walk along Cypress Walk.

"Are we planning a relationship," he said, "or preserving a pleasant semester until your internship begins?"

Simone stopped beneath the bare branches.

"That is not a neutral question."

"I know."

"It makes every good thing we have done sound temporary unless I promise the future you want."

"I do not know what future you want."

"Neither do I. That is what I keep telling you."

Malik looked away. "I do not want to invest in something you have already decided to leave."

"And I do not want affection to become evidence that I owe you continuation."

They stood close enough to touch and did not.

Simone opened the copper notebook to the unanswered question about opportunities. "We have evidence that we care about each other. We have evidence that we work through disagreement. We do not have evidence about distance, London, or what either of us will need by summer."

"So we wait?"

"We keep being honest. We stop pretending uncertainty means one of us is secretly disloyal. And we gather more evidence."

Malik almost laughed. "You turned our relationship into a research design."

"You started it."

They continued walking, hands linked but not relaxed.

Some series converged. Some diverged. At that moment, Malik and Simone had only terms, not a theorem.
