# Week Fifteen: What Can Still Be Changed

During advising week, honesty spread across campus like a weather system.

Students who had spent months saying "good" and "busy" began using more precise language. A chemistry major admitted she was failing lab. A senior learned one missing prerequisite could delay graduation. Two roommates compared emergency-aid forms over lunch. Outside Eliza Moss Hall, an adviser explained to a tearful student that withdrawing from one course was not the same as withdrawing from a future.

The deadline sharpened every conversation: remain, withdraw, change strategy, accept the grade, ask for help.

Malik sat across from Dr. Brooks with his corrected first test, completed make-up quiz, and applications-exam feedback arranged in a careful stack.

"Your work is improving," she said. "Your schedule is not."

He had earned back some credit through corrections. He attended office hours. His explanations now showed the reasoning he once assumed answers could carry alone.

He was also working later, preparing for the analytics semifinal, and answering family calls whenever the insurance company produced a new request.

"Can I stay in the course?" Malik asked.

"Yes," Dr. Brooks said. "The question is whether you will continue making the changes that allow you to succeed in it."

Remaining was not a passive decision.

## Friday, November 7: Stay, Leave, Change

Nia attended her own advising appointment expecting praise and received a question instead.

"What are you protecting in your schedule?" her adviser asked.

Nia named calculus, outreach, sleep, and one weekly evening with Julian.

"And what are you protecting emotionally?"

That answer was harder.

Across campus, DJ met with an athletics academic adviser who reminded him that increased playing time did not lower assignment expectations. Keisha adjusted a programming-course deadline around travel for her first indoor-track preparation meet. Students were not choosing between being successful and unsuccessful. They were choosing which consequences they could responsibly carry.

At North Star that evening, Malik checked in a student who had just withdrawn from calculus.

"I feel like everybody is going to know," the student said while returning a calculator.

Malik looked around the busy lobby. "Most people are trying to survive their own decisions."

"Did you ever think about dropping?"

"Yes."

It was not entirely true, but he had thought about whether the course would drop him.

"I am staying," Malik added. "That means I have to change how I am doing it."

The student nodded. "I am leaving this one so I can change how I do the next one."

They handed over the calculator like a small ceremonial object.

## Saturday, November 8: More Hours

Marcel's orthopedic appointment ended with another delay.

The knee had improved, but not enough for him to return to repair work safely. He needed physical therapy, more healing time, and a follow-up evaluation.

"How long?" Malik asked over the phone.

"They do not know," Celeste said.

"What did the doctor estimate?"

"Weeks, not days."

Malik opened the family budget. Reduced income had begun to eat through the emergency balance. Temporary aid helped with immediate costs, but insurance had not resolved the equipment claim and repairs required payments before reimbursements.

He emailed Ms. Alvarez and requested two additional North Star shifts.

Then he told Dr. Bennett he could still complete his semifinal rehearsal section.

Dr. Bennett read the schedule Malik had submitted.

"No," he said.

"The shifts are evening hours."

"Your body does not distinguish paid hours from academic hours when deciding whether it needs sleep."

"My family needs the money."

"I am not telling you not to work. I am telling you the current analytics assignment must shrink if work increases."

Malik agreed to remove one data task.

Afterward, he accepted both additional shifts and kept a smaller task Dr. Bennett thought had been reassigned.

It was not a dramatic lie. It was a quiet failure to subtract.

## Sunday, November 9: Accumulated Loss

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connected rate and accumulation.

Dr. Brooks drew a rate function, shaded the area beneath it, and showed how a definite integral measured net change over an interval. Then she differentiated an accumulation function and returned to the original rate.

"These ideas are not strangers," she said. "Accumulation and instantaneous change describe the same process from different directions."

Then she introduced substitution through an accumulation problem whose rate contained a function nested inside its own derivative. Instead of expanding everything, the class renamed the inner expression, transformed the differential, and recognized a familiar antiderivative.

"Substitution is not a trick for making symbols disappear," Dr. Brooks said. "It identifies the structure governing the change."

Nia checked the result by differentiating. Malik evaluated the endpoints and interpreted the sign of the net change rather than reporting only its magnitude. A positive contribution in one part of the interval and a negative contribution in another both affected the final total.

Malik understood the proof.

He struggled with the example.

A small negative rate sustained over time produced a large total loss. On paper, the effect appeared clean. At home, losses had accumulated through missed workdays, replacement meals, transportation, medical copays, cleaning supplies, temporary storage, and damaged equipment. No single cost had destroyed the budget. The area beneath all of them had.

Nia saw him stop writing.

"Home?" she asked.

"Every day my father cannot work is another change in the total."

"Do you want to talk after class?"

He looked at the semifinal notes in his bag. "I have rehearsal."

Nia nodded. She did not ask him to cancel.

"Later, then."

Malik appreciated the offer and the absence of pressure. He also recognized how easily *later* became his hiding place.

## Monday, November 10: Three Slides Too Many

Malik's semifinal section contained fourteen slides.

He presented the source data, missingness audit, model structure, sensitivity analysis, uncertainty intervals, opponent adjustments, decision thresholds, edge cases, limitations, and three alternative visualizations.

At minute eight, he had not yet stated what decision the model supported.

Simone held up one hand. "Stop."

Malik stopped mid-sentence.

She walked to the display and deleted three slides from the sequence.

"Those explain the sensitivity design," he said.

"The appendix explains it."

"A judge may question the intervals."

"Then we answer the question. Right now your need to prove rigor is burying the decision."

"If I simplify it, the model may sound weaker."

"If nobody knows what the model is for, rigor becomes camouflage."

Andre looked down at his notes. Priya pretended to adjust the timer.

Malik stared at the missing slides.

Simone softened her voice. "Lead with the decision context. Show the range. Name the limitations. Let the judges ask for depth. You do not have to force every piece of evidence through the front door at once."

He thought of his schedule, his family calls, his unfinished task, and every fact he used to prove he was not falling apart.

"Again," Simone said.

Malik began from the first slide.

This time, the decision appeared before minute two.

## Tuesday, November 11: The Score Between Them

McCall-Hart marked Veterans Day with a morning observance near Founders' Bell. Students read names from the university's history, the band played a restrained arrangement, and Julian recorded oral histories with alumni who had served.

That evening belonged to Nia and Julian.

Their protected time had survived on the calendar. It did not survive contact with everything they had not said.

They ordered dinner from a small restaurant off campus and sat on the Jubilee Library terrace.

For ten minutes, the evening was gentle.

Then Julian asked why Nia had told Keisha about the basketball argument before speaking to him.

"Because I needed to understand what I felt," Nia said.

"But when I talk to Marcus, it becomes public performance."

"You joked about our conflict in front of him. I talked privately to my roommate."

"After you left me sitting there in front of everybody."

"You made me the joke."

They began counting.

Nia counted the late homecoming date, the missed five minutes after Battle of the Bands, the photograph, the Delta argument, and the exhibition.

Julian counted the missed showcase, unanswered lab messages, the photo with Malik, and every time Nia treated his media work as less serious than her academic commitments.

"I never said your work was less serious," she said.

"You do not have to say it. You explain your work to Malik. You tell me the headline."

"Because when I try, you turn the story into how it makes you look."

The protected evening gave them privacy. They used most of it to present evidence against each other.

By nine, the food was cold and neither felt heard.

## Wednesday, November 12: No Performance

Nia and Julian met beside the closed radio booth Wednesday afternoon.

No camera. No friends. No beautiful campus backdrop.

"We keep apologizing," Julian said, "and then using the apology as proof the next problem should not count."

Nia nodded. "We fix the event and keep the pattern."

"Do you want to break up?"

The directness hurt.

"I do not know," she said.

"Neither do I."

They agreed to several days apart. No relationship posts, no public explanation, no friends sent as messengers. They would attend their responsibilities and decide what they wanted when the argument was not still speaking for them.

Julian took her hand once before leaving.

Nia returned to the residence hall and told Keisha, "We are taking space."

Keisha opened her arms.

Nia stepped into the hug.

For once, nobody asked her to explain the decision immediately.

## Thursday, November 13: What Support Looks Like

Malik noticed Nia's silence during calculus.

She answered when called on and took complete notes, but none of her usual side comments appeared in the margins of his page. After class, she left with Keisha instead of waiting for him.

DJ knew enough from Imani to say only, "Relationship situation. Do not be weird."

"That instruction lacks operational detail."

"It means do not turn into a romance speech while she is hurting."

Malik had an analytics rehearsal in twenty minutes. His instinct pulled in two directions: abandon the rehearsal to prove he cared, or attend it and pretend he had not noticed.

He chose neither performance.

He sent Nia one message:

**I can tell today is hard. I have rehearsal until seven. What support do you want after: food, company, help with calculus, or space? No response needed right away.**

Then he silenced his phone and entered the lab.

At 6:42, during a scheduled break, he checked.

Nia had replied: **Company. No fixing. Maybe fries. Good luck at rehearsal.**

Malik wrote back: **Fries are within scope.**

He returned to the presentation.

Nia was hurting. Malik did not use the hurt as an opening. He kept the commitment he had already made and offered support on her terms.

Within days, the analytics team would learn whether honest uncertainty could survive a semifinal. Nia and Julian would learn whether affection could survive an honest decision.

For one evening, Malik focused on the next responsible thing.
