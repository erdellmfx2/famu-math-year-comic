# Week Thirty-Eight: What Remains

Nia opened the project folder and selected everything.

The computer displayed one question: **Move 487 items to Trash?**

She hovered over the button.

The fellowship version contained months of code, maps, interviews, drafts, mistakes, permissions, and the presentation that had failed to produce the result she wanted. Deleting it would not change the ceremony. It would remove the evidence that she had cared enough to be hurt.

Nia canceled.

She created an archive folder named **SYMPOSIUM_FELLOWSHIP_VERSION_FINAL**. Then she opened a new project file.

For the title, she typed:

**COMMUNITY VALIDATION**

She did not feel hopeful.

She simply was not finished.

## Friday, April 17: Archive, Do Not Erase

Keisha found the new file during their final full class week.

"You want to continue?" she asked.

"I want to find out whether the model can become useful. I do not want to act like that makes losing okay."

"Good. Because it does not."

They wrote no triumphant comeback plan. Instead, they listed validation needs from the judges: more representative community input, transportation constraints, repeated-message testing, larger-network performance, and governance beyond the student team.

Julian joined later and separated the fellowship media package from raw interview materials. Every participant's permissions remained attached. Reuse would require the same limits, not a vague claim that the project had evolved.

Nia changed the project status from **FELLOWSHIP FINALIST** to **RESEARCH IN REVISION**.

The new label felt smaller.

It was also accurate.

At calculus, Dr. Delgado reviewed polar coordinates, integration methods, and series. He asked students to explain not only how to solve a problem but when the chosen representation stopped helping.

Nia understood the question differently now.

The fellowship version had taken the project as far as it could. Archiving it preserved the work without requiring the future to remain inside its boundaries.

## Saturday, April 18: Useful After Ownership

The guest seminar on public mathematics drew a smaller crowd than the free-lunch announcement deserved.

Dr. Laila Monroe described a rejected transit study she had carried for years. The original research belonged to an academic team, used inaccessible language, and assumed public agencies would adopt the model because its predictions were accurate.

They did not.

The work became useful only after researchers shared decision-making with riders, drivers, disability advocates, and local planners. Those partners changed the questions, removed features they did not need, and refused several recommendations the academic team considered elegant.

"Relinquishing ownership did not mean abandoning expertise," Dr. Monroe said. "It meant accepting that being skilled at the mathematics did not make us sole authors of the problem."

Nia asked whether rejection had motivated the change.

"Partly," Dr. Monroe replied. "But do not romanticize it. I would have preferred the grant. Rejection gave us fewer resources, not secret wisdom. People gave the project a different life because we eventually learned to listen."

That answer mattered.

Nia did not need to thank the fellowship judges for saying no. She could dislike the outcome and still refuse to make it the final authority on the work.

After the seminar, she added community decision rights to the validation plan.

The project could continue only by becoming less exclusively theirs.

## Sunday, April 19: A Question Julian Is Allowed to Ask

Julian met Nia in the radio studio to finish the symposium documentary archive.

They watched the final interview clip, the one where Nia compared elegant models with beautiful relationship stories. When the screen went dark, Julian remained quiet.

"Can I ask something personal?" he said.

"Yes."

"Working together went well. Better than I expected. Does any part of you think we should try dating again?"

Nia felt no panic, anger, or secret relief. Only affection for someone who deserved a direct answer.

"No," she said. "What we had mattered. What we built this spring matters. I do not want to restart the relationship."

Julian nodded slowly. "I thought that was the answer. I needed to hear it rather than turn collaboration into a sign."

"Thank you for asking instead of producing a documentary called *Signs Nia Missed*."

"The working title was stronger."

They laughed.

Nia did not punish him by withdrawing warmth. Julian did not bargain with the success of the project or invoke their good memories as evidence. They finished the archive, confirmed future media responsibilities, and walked out as collaborators whose past no longer required a sequel.

Closure was not silence. Sometimes it was a question answered clearly enough that neither person had to keep interpreting.

## Monday, April 20: Return Without Rewriting

Simone and Malik exchanged the remaining pieces of spring.

He returned a finance book and a scarf she had left in North Star. She returned his museum ticket, which had been tucked inside the copper notebook, and a folder of family-photo scans she had backed up.

They met in the Porter Center lobby again.

"London orientation starts after commencement," Simone said. "I leave for the program in May."

"Do you need the international phone-plan research?"

She smiled faintly. "Send me the comparison without the relationship schedule."

"Agreed."

They discussed practical details and mutual friends. Neither used friendliness to suggest reunion. Neither turned the breakup into evidence that dating had been a mistake.

"I am glad we had spring," Simone said.

Malik felt sadness arrive without emergency. "So am I."

She kept the notebook. He kept what its questions had taught him.

When they parted, Malik did not watch her leave and imagine Nia waiting somewhere beyond the door. He walked to Dr. Bennett's office to prepare for finals.

Meaning did not require continuation.

## Tuesday, April 21: One Finale Wins, One Series Does Not

Imani's softball team needed one victory in its home finale to secure a conference-tournament place.

They won behind disciplined pitching, two sacrifice bunts, and a sixth-inning double that scored the decisive run. Imani fielded the final ground ball and threw to first. The team rushed the circle as the tournament berth became official.

Across campus, baseball lost its home series and fell out of realistic postseason contention.

The two outcomes arrived within an hour.

The friend group celebrated softball at the field, then visited baseball friends packing equipment after the loss. No one asked the baseball seniors to become inspirational about it. No one told Imani to mute her joy.

"This is becoming a recurring campus lesson," DJ said.

"Apparently people keep needing it," Imani replied.

Nia cheered until her throat hurt. The softball win did not repair the fellowship rejection. That made the celebration easier, not harder. It asked nothing from her story.

For one evening, she could be happy because her friend had earned a future game.

## Wednesday, April 22: Different Times Are Not Enough

Malik and Nia met in daylight, on purpose, with no research materials between them.

"I wanted you in fall," Malik said. "You were with Julian."

"I wanted you in spring," Nia replied. "You were with Simone."

The symmetry sounded neat. Their lives had not been.

Malik had waited too long and treated patience as a private future claim. Nia had mistaken his availability for permanence. Both had formed real relationships with other people. Both had used jealousy to discover feelings they should have examined directly.

"If timing were the only problem," Nia said, "we would be together now."

"Timing is not enough."

Trust had changed. So had their understanding of themselves. Malik did not know whether he could choose Nia without making her the resolution of spring. Nia did not know whether she wanted Malik as he was or as proof that the reversed year had finally become fair.

"I do care about you," she said.

"I care about you too."

Neither converted the admission into a plan.

They had finally arrived at the same emotional moment.

That did not mean they were ready to travel from it together.

## Thursday, April 23: Nobody Waiting in the Wings

Nia and Malik chose finals.

The decision sounded boring enough to be healthy.

They would not schedule a first date after exams, promise to revisit the relationship in summer, or wait for the other person to become ready. They would study, finish their responsibilities, and allow the next semester to reveal who they were without romantic suspense organizing every interaction.

"No private promises," Malik said.

"No hidden countdowns," Nia added.

"No asking Keisha or DJ for intelligence reports."

"I have never called them intelligence reports."

"Your face has."

She laughed.

They returned to Dr. Delgado's review session and sat at different tables. Nia studied polar area with Amara. Malik reviewed integration methods with DJ. Keisha joined both groups only after completing track recovery.

No one waited in the wings.

The unresolved future remained open without becoming anyone's assignment.

Finals were next.

For once, the exam was the simpler uncertainty.
