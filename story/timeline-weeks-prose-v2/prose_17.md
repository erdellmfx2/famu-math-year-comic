# Week Seventeen: The Catch and the Quiet After

The spare radio-booth key weighed less than an ounce.

Nia carried it all Friday morning as if it were made of iron.

Julian had given it to her in September after she began helping with occasional math segments. The key did not open the building, only the booth, and she had rarely used it without him. Still, it had represented access to his world: the microphones, music beds, handwritten schedules, and small red light that transformed ordinary speech into broadcast.

His final fall performance with the Marching Herons would come during River Classic weekend. After that, band activity would slow for exams and the holiday break.

Nia found him near Media House after rehearsal.

"I should give this back," she said.

Julian looked at the key in her palm. "You could keep it through finals."

"I do not think I should."

He nodded.

Their fingers touched during the handoff. Neither made a joke.

"Good luck at the Classic," Nia said.

"You too. I mean, you are not playing."

"I understood."

"Right."

They hugged briefly. Julian walked toward rehearsal, the key disappearing into his pocket.

Nia remained composed until she reached the second-floor bathroom in Jubilee Library. Then she locked herself in a stall and cried hard enough to miss ten minutes of calculus review.

## Friday, November 21: The Returned Key

By afternoon, Nia had washed her face and joined a cumulative review session.

Dr. Brooks asked students to explain how definite integrals appeared beyond geometric area. Groups considered accumulated distance from velocity, total cost from a marginal-cost rate, water volume from changing flow, and population change from a growth rate.

"Units tell the story," Nia said when her group reached the water-flow problem. "Rate in gallons per minute, integrated over minutes, gives gallons."

Her voice sounded normal.

Amara did not ask why her eyes looked swollen. She handed Nia the marker for the next explanation.

Nia drew the accumulation graph and communicated the solution in three layers: the equation, the units, and one sentence about meaning. She did not fill every silence. She did not perform extra brightness.

Afterward, Keisha met her outside with two drinks.

"Key returned?"

"Yes."

"How bad?"

"Quiet."

Keisha handed her the sweeter drink. "Those are sometimes the worst ones."

Nia nodded and let herself be known without producing a better ending.

## Saturday, November 22: The File Stays Open

Malik returned to the analytics postmortem Saturday morning.

His first instinct had been to bury the semifinal folder beneath a new directory named **SPRING DEVELOPMENT**. Instead, he opened Simone's four-part document.

He added evidence from the judge feedback and linked each limitation to a possible test. External validation required data from other teams. Operational testing required coaches or analysts completing time-limited scenarios. Transferability needed more than replacing inputs; the team had to examine whether model relationships remained stable across styles of play.

Then Malik wrote the part that felt worst:

**Our interface communicated uncertainty more effectively than our study established generalizability. The presentation invited users to trust a process we had validated only in one institutional context.**

Simone read the sentence over his shoulder.

"Keep it," she said.

"It sounds like we built the wrong project."

"We built a useful project with a smaller evidence boundary than we wanted."

"The judges may have thought it was not useful."

"Do not invent additional rejection. We have enough real rejection."

Malik added a clean diagram showing where evidence ended and proposed testing began. He uploaded the postmortem to his portfolio beside the polished semifinal slides.

The failure would not become a secret file he never opened again.

It would remain visible without becoming the only thing the portfolio said about him.

## Sunday, November 23: The River Classic Catch

River Classic tied McCall-Hart and Bayland State at twenty-four with less than two minutes remaining.

The rivalry had filled Bellwether Civic Stadium in two colors and several generations of argument. Bands traded songs. Alumni shouted across sections. Every tackle seemed personal to someone whose graduation year began with nineteen.

McCall-Hart faced third-and-long near midfield.

DJ lined up wide. His defender played tight coverage toward the sideline, daring him to win the boundary.

At the snap, DJ released inside, forced the defender to turn, then broke back outside. The pass sailed high and farther toward the sideline than planned.

DJ jumped.

He caught the ball with both hands, twisted in the air, and brought one foot down inches inside the white line. His second foot dragged across the turf before the defender drove him out.

For one second, nobody knew.

The official raised both arms to stop the clock, then signaled complete.

First down.

The McCall-Hart side detonated.

Malik grabbed the shoulders of a stranger. Nia screamed beside Keisha. Imani nearly climbed over the row in front of her. Julian's alto section launched into the fight song before the stadium had finished replaying the catch.

The gain placed McCall-Hart in range for the winning score. Three plays later, the Herons crossed the goal line.

Final: McCall-Hart 31, Bayland State 24.

DJ's catch did not score the winning touchdown. It made the winning touchdown possible.

He lay on the turf after the final whistle, looking up at the stadium lights while teammates piled onto him.

## Monday, November 24: Call Home First

By Monday morning, DJ had appeared on three campus accounts, one regional sports show, and a highlight reel titled **CATCH OF THE CLASSIC?** with a question mark designed to create arguments.

He completed two interviews before breakfast.

Reporters asked about the catch, the route, the sideline awareness, and whether he expected more playing time. DJ thanked the quarterback, the receivers coach, and the teammates whose blocks kept the play alive.

Then he stepped away and called his mother.

"They said this could affect my scholarship review," he told her.

Her voice changed. "How?"

DJ's current aid package required careful family budgeting. A larger football scholarship would mean fewer loans and less money his mother sent each month.

"No promises," he said. "Coach said the staff will review roles after the season."

"Then we do not spend imaginary money."

"I know."

"But I am proud of you right now. No review needed."

DJ went quiet.

Attention was fun. The possibility of easing pressure at home mattered more.

After the call, he returned to the residence hall and found Malik editing the analytics postmortem.

"You have a national celebrity in the room," DJ said.

"Regional, possibly."

"Hater."

Malik looked up. "Good catch."

DJ smiled. From Malik, those two words carried a complete scouting report of affection.

## Tuesday, November 25: The Last Fall Song

The Marching Herons performed a farewell set Tuesday evening for students leaving early for Thanksgiving.

Nia considered staying away.

Then she decided avoiding every place Julian mattered would turn the breakup into a new schedule controlling her life.

She stood with Keisha near the back of the crowd. Julian played with the same focus that had first drawn her toward him. The final song slowed into a warm arrangement of the university hymn. Alumni joined softly. Copper lights reflected from the instruments.

Afterward, Nia waited until the band had finished packing.

"You sounded beautiful," she told Julian.

"Thank you. I saw you."

"I figured. Small campus, large cape reputation."

He smiled. "I am glad you came."

"Me too."

They stood close enough to remember what closeness had meant.

"Have a good Thanksgiving," Julian said.

"You too."

Nia walked away before respect became an excuse to reopen the decision. Closure did not feel clean. It felt like choosing not to turn pain into another invitation.

## Wednesday, November 26: Stripped Walls

Malik returned to Gulfport for Thanksgiving recess.

The damaged lower walls had been stripped to the studs. Dehumidifiers hummed where the couch once sat. Repair estimates covered the dining table, each one higher than the family had hoped and lower than the worst number Malik feared.

Marcel walked through the room slowly, his injured leg stiff.

"Physical therapy says bend it more," he said. "The knee says mind my business."

Malik watched him grip a doorframe while stepping over an exposed section of floor.

"Sit down."

"I have been sitting for two months."

"You have not. That is part of the problem."

Marcel gave him a look, then accepted the folding chair.

They reviewed estimates together. Insurance would cover part of the structural work but remained uncertain about several tools. Family members had contributed labor. A church fund had helped with temporary materials. The remaining gap was smaller than before and still beyond easy reach.

"Your job money helps," Celeste told Malik quietly. "But school is still your job."

"I can do both."

She looked at him.

Malik corrected himself. "I am working on doing both."

It was less impressive and more true.

## Thursday, November 27: Separate Kitchens

On Thanksgiving evening, Nia called Malik from her mother's kitchen in Huntsville.

Behind her, relatives argued about card-game rules and whether macaroni and cheese counted as a vegetable. Behind Malik, Micah searched for foil while Celeste insisted leftovers required a system.

"How is home?" Nia asked.

"Stripped walls. More estimates. My father is walking better than he admits and worse than he claims."

"That sounds like him."

"You met him for zero minutes."

"I understand the genre."

Malik laughed.

He asked about Huntsville. Nia described her aunt's tutoring program, her mother's interrogation about sleep, and the cousin who had asked whether college had made her famous.

Neither mentioned the North Star couch directly. Neither used holiday distance to confess feelings that had not become responsible simply because Nia was single.

"I am glad you called," Malik said.

"Me too."

"You okay?"

Nia looked toward the family noise. "Not all the time. More than last week."

"Same."

They stayed on the phone while washing dishes in separate states, talking about finals, food, and nothing that demanded a decision.

The call ended warm, unfinished, and exactly where it needed to end.
