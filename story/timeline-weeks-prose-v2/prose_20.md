# Week Twenty: After the Grades

Fall commencement made finishing look simple.

Names were called. Graduates crossed a stage inside Ruth Ellis Fieldhouse. Families screamed from the stands, flowers crowded the lobby, and every program reduced years of work to a name, degree, and line of honors.

Malik sat with Nia, Keisha, and DJ near the back.

They watched a mathematics graduate hug Dr. Brooks after crossing. They saw a father lift a toddler above his head when the child's mother received her degree. One older graduate stopped midway across the stage, closed her eyes, and pressed a hand to her heart before continuing.

"I wonder what her semester was like," Nia said.

"Probably more than the program says," Malik replied.

Commencement displayed the answer. It concealed most of the work before it.

The first-years understood that now.

They also understood they were still near the beginning.

## Friday, December 12: Invisible Work

After the ceremony, the group moved through the fieldhouse lobby while families took photographs beneath indigo banners.

DJ carried his travel bag for the Heritage Bowl. Residence halls closed Saturday, so the football team had moved directly into bowl preparation.

"Imagine our graduation," he said. "They will need additional applause time for me."

Keisha looked at him. "You have completed one semester."

"A decorated semester."

"One paper almost took you out last week."

"The public does not need every detail."

Malik laughed, then watched another family surround a graduate.

His own parents had talked about his future as if the degree were a bridge to stability. They did not see every correction form, overnight shift, or moment he considered whether the path still fit. Today reminded him that invisible difficulty did not make completion less real.

Nia noticed him watching.

"Four years," she said.

"Approximately."

"Do not add uncertainty to graduation."

"Evidence requires it."

She smiled. The joke felt like an old piece of their friendship returning without the pressure to become anything else.

## Saturday, December 13: The Block

The Heritage Bowl came down to one decisive play.

McCall-Hart led by three late in the fourth quarter and needed a first down to protect the clock. The offense called a run toward the edge. DJ lined up wide, far from where television viewers expected the action to matter.

His assignment was to block the cornerback.

At the snap, DJ released as if beginning a route, forced the defender backward, then engaged cleanly. The running back cut outside and passed behind him. DJ kept his hands inside, moved his feet, and avoided holding.

The runner gained eleven yards.

First down.

McCall-Hart drained the remaining clock and won the Heritage Bowl.

The highlight replay focused on the runner. The announcer praised the offensive line. DJ appeared at the edge of the frame for less than two seconds.

In the film room later, the coach paused on those two seconds.

"Cole seals the corner," he said. "No edge, no first down."

DJ nodded.

The ball had gone elsewhere. His work still shaped the result.

He texted Malik: **I WON A BOWL GAME WITHOUT RECEIVING A PASS. EXPLAIN THE STATISTICAL INJUSTICE.**

Malik replied: **Your sample contains one excellent block. Report the correct variable.**

DJ saved that message too.

## Sunday, December 14: More Than Imaginary Money

The head coach asked DJ to stay after Sunday's team meeting.

DJ sat across from him expecting a discussion of spring training.

"Your role changed this fall," the coach said. "Special teams, offensive snaps, River Classic, championship. Your academic reports are solid. We are increasing your athletic scholarship for spring."

The new amount would not make college free. It would reduce the gap enough to change his family's monthly decisions.

DJ thanked the coach, left the office, and called Malik before the athletics department announced anything.

"It increased," DJ said.

Malik sat up on Aunt Renee's couch in Gulfport. "How much?"

DJ told him.

"That covers most of the remaining housing gap."

"I know."

For once, DJ's voice held no joke.

"You earned it," Malik said.

"The team earned the season."

"Your scholarship is not a group assignment."

DJ laughed, then became quiet again. "My mom can breathe a little."

That mattered more than the public post that appeared an hour later.

## Monday, December 15: B

Malik opened his final grade from the living room in Gulfport.

Calculus I: **B**.

In August, he had expected an A. He had arrived with a budget, a roadmap, and the belief that preparation could protect him from outcomes that belonged to less disciplined people.

The letter on screen first looked like distance: from his expectation, from the image his family held, from the transcript he had planned.

Then he opened the grade details.

Sixty-four on the first test. Improved applications exam. Strong corrections. Completed make-up work. A final exam that confirmed recovery without perfection.

The B did not mean the storm was good for him. It did not reward exhaustion. It did not transform the poor first test into an inspirational requirement.

It measured a semester in which he had nearly hidden himself out of the course and then learned to ask for structure.

Celeste looked over his shoulder. "Is that bad?"

"No."

"You made the face you make when a number insults you."

Malik looked again. "It is not the grade I expected."

"Is it the grade you earned?"

"Yes."

"Then greet it properly."

Malik laughed. "Hello, B."

"See? Manners."

Later, Malik opened his spring registration audit. Calculus II required more than the letter grade that unlocked enrollment. His final feedback identified concrete prerequisites: reliable algebraic manipulation, confident substitution, interpretation of definite integrals, and the ability to explain why a method applied.

He created a two-week review plan, then deleted half of it. Winter break was not a hidden semester.

The revised plan contained three short sessions: algebra patterns that had slowed him, integral setups he still confused under pressure, and one explanation exercise using words rather than compressed notation. He also blocked North Star work hours only after confirming his spring class schedule.

For the first time, transcript review did not become a judgment of who Malik was. It became evidence about what the next course would ask him to carry.

## Tuesday, December 16: A-Minus

Nia's A-minus arrived in Huntsville while her mother was reorganizing holiday decorations and asking questions from another room.

"What did you get?"

"A-minus."

Denise Reynolds appeared in the doorway. "That is excellent."

"It is."

Nia waited for the grade to erase the exhaustion.

It did not.

The A-minus reflected strong understanding, improved listening, and a final exam recovery after freezing. It did not change the missed showcase, the overcommitted review, the breakup, or the nights she confused being needed with being valued.

"You are doing the face too," Denise said.

"What face?"

"The one where good news has to defend itself in court."

Nia put down her phone. "I am proud. I am also tired. And sad."

"Those feelings are not fighting for one chair."

Nia hugged her mother.

She celebrated the grade that evening with family. She did not post it as proof the semester had gone beautifully.

The next morning, she reviewed her own spring prerequisites. Calculus II would move quickly into integration techniques and applications. Her A-minus showed conceptual strength, but the exam history showed that emotional overload made routine algebra less dependable. She wrote one goal beneath the transcript: **Practice the fragile skills before they become emergency skills.**

Then she closed the portal. Reflection had produced a next step. It did not need to consume the holiday.

## Wednesday, December 17: Easy Without a Deadline

An alumni actuarial workshop brought Malik back to Bellwether for a day.

The session covered exam pathways, internships, professional communication, and how early-career analysts should discuss model limitations with people who wanted certainty.

Simone attended for the finance-and-data panel.

Afterward, she and Malik found a quiet table at Porter Center.

"B in calculus," he told her.

"Congratulations."

"It feels complicated."

"Most earned things do."

They debriefed the analytics loss without slides or a countdown. Simone admitted she had known transferability was weak but hoped clear uncertainty communication would carry more weight. Malik admitted he had blamed his answer because personal failure felt easier to fix than a limited study design.

"You cannot rehearse your way into evidence," Simone said.

"I tried."

"Aggressively."

Their conversation moved from analytics to spring classes, career goals, and the terrible workshop coffee. Without a deadline, Simone's directness felt less like pressure and more like ease. Malik's dry humor appeared often enough to surprise both of them.

When they finally checked the time, ninety minutes had passed.

"We are better at talking when nobody needs a deliverable," Simone said.

"Preliminary result."

"We may need replication."

They smiled, leaving the possibility unnamed.

## Thursday, December 18: The Living Room

Malik returned to Gulfport after dark.

His old bedroom had new lower drywall but still smelled of removed wallboard, sealant, and damp wood. The floor remained unfinished. Boxes occupied the bed.

"You can sleep at Aunt Renee's," Celeste offered.

"I want to be here."

They set up an air mattress in the living room beside the dehumidifier. Micah brought three blankets and claimed one was "structural support."

Malik lay awake beneath the ceiling fan, hearing his parents speak quietly in the kitchen about repair costs.

He had earned a B. DJ had earned more scholarship support. Nia had earned an A-minus. The Herons had won two championships.

None of those answers rebuilt the room.

Still, they mattered.

The semester had ended without the competition victory Malik wanted. It had also ended with work he could defend, people he trusted, and a transcript that showed recovery rather than collapse.

He turned onto his side as the dehumidifier hummed.

Spring would begin from here, not from the version of home he remembered.
