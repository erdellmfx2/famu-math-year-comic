# Week Twenty-Nine: Valentine's Data

By Friday morning, McCall-Hart had been attacked by paper hearts.

They hung from residence-hall doors, bookstore windows, dining-hall napkin holders, and one deeply confused bronze statue near Cypress Walk. The campus store sold stuffed herons holding signs that read **YOU MAKE MY HEART TAKE FLIGHT**, which Nia considered an institutional failure.

"If anybody gives me that bird," she told Keisha, "I will release it into the wild."

"It is polyester."

"Then it will learn resilience."

Nia joked through breakfast, class, and three different student-organization rose tables. Humor kept every display at a safe distance. It worked until Julian's campus-radio show played a dedication from a student celebrating two years with her girlfriend.

The message was simple, affectionate, and public.

Nia stopped walking beneath the radio speaker outside the student center.

## Friday, February 13: The Dedication

The student on the radio thanked her girlfriend for learning her late-night study order, attending dance showcases even when she was tired, and making ordinary Tuesdays feel chosen.

There was no proposal, marching band, or drone carrying roses. Still, everybody crossing the plaza heard it.

Nia remembered how badly she had once wanted visible romance. With Julian, public celebration had first felt like proof that love was real. Later, the pictures had become a place to hide what they were not saying in private.

Now Malik was dating Simone quietly. He had left overtime to keep a dinner promise. He attended her panel without taking the room. Their relationship did not need Nia to observe it, which somehow made it harder to dismiss.

Julian's voice returned after the dedication and introduced the next song without adding commentary.

Nia continued toward class.

She did not want Julian back. She did not want Malik to perform affection for the campus. What hurt was remembering that she had once stood at the center of someone's public choice while Malik waited nearby, and she had not understood the cost of treating his feelings as a possibility she could revisit later.

At the calculus review, Dr. Delgado returned their test corrections. Every student had to explain one wrong method and the evidence that should have changed the decision.

Nia selected her rushed partial-fractions work.

Recognizing a pattern was not the same as understanding what it required. Valentine's week, apparently, had assigned the same lesson twice.

## Saturday, February 14: Questions for Later

Malik gave Simone a notebook.

It was small, copper-colored, and completely free of glitter. On the first page, he had written:

**Questions we have not had time to answer yet.**

Below it were six prompts.

What place do you want to visit without turning it into a career opportunity?

What does support feel like when you are disappointed?

Which part of your future are you afraid to say aloud because it may change someone else's plan?

Simone read the final question twice.

"This is either thoughtful or an extremely sophisticated homework assignment," she said.

"No due date."

"Extra credit?"

"No points."

"Now I know you are serious."

They spent Valentine's afternoon at a used bookstore and dinner at a small restaurant away from campus crowds. Simone had brought Malik a set of archival sleeves for the storm-damaged family photographs he kept sorting, plus a card that said she admired how he was learning to care without immediately controlling the outcome.

Neither gift was expensive. Both revealed attention.

At dinner, Simone wrote one question of her own in the notebook:

**What opportunity would you want me to take even if it made our relationship harder?**

Malik looked at it, then at her.

"You do not have to answer tonight," she said.

He appreciated the permission.

He also understood that the question had not appeared by accident.

## Sunday, February 15: The Missing Warning

Nia began the campus research pitch with confidence.

She introduced Bellwether's flood-route problem, showed how uncertain road capacities could change a recommended path, and used Julian's animation to reveal each shift. Keisha demonstrated the scenario engine. The audience followed. Dr. Price nodded once from the back row.

Then a visiting reviewer raised her hand.

"Your model recommends a route after an official warning. Why do you assume every driver receives the warning at the same time?"

Nia moved to the next slide. "Our current focus is network capacity rather than message distribution."

"But the route recommendation depends on when people leave."

"We account for changing congestion."

"After equal notification. Do residents without reliable mobile service receive the same alert? Do they trust it? What if a message is only in English? What if one neighborhood hears through family before the official system?"

The questions opened a hole beneath the presentation.

Nia knew the reviewer was right. She also knew everyone in the room could see that she had not prepared for it.

"Those factors are outside the current model," she said, too quickly. "Every model requires scope."

"Certainly," the reviewer replied. "But a boundary should not hide an assumption that changes the decision."

Nia thanked her and called for the next question.

The pitch ended on time.

For Nia, it did not end at all.

## Monday, February 16: The Answer Nia Would Not Give

During the feedback session, Nia defended the scope again.

They could not model everything. Communication data was difficult to obtain. Their proposal concerned route sensitivity. Adding trust, language, device access, and social networks could make the project impossible to finish before the symposium.

Each statement was true.

Together, they sounded like a wall.

The reviewer turned to Keisha. "How does the current software represent the warning?"

Keisha answered without looking at Nia. "It treats notification as simultaneous and universal. That is not evidence-based. It was a simplifying assumption we did not make visible enough."

Nia felt betrayed before she had time to feel ashamed of feeling betrayed.

"Would you remove the project from consideration?" the reviewer asked.

"No," Keisha said. "I would test how delayed or missing information changes departure timing, then state what we still cannot represent."

That was the answer the team needed.

Nia heard only that Keisha had given it in public.

After the session, she packed the laptop too aggressively.

"You could have discussed that with me first," she said.

"The question happened during the pitch."

"You made it sound like we ignored the problem."

"We did ignore the problem."

Keisha's voice stayed level. "I am your coauthor, not your agreement generator."

She left for track practice.

Nia remained beside the empty presentation screen, furious at criticism, furious at Keisha, and most furious because the honest answer had not belonged to her.

## Tuesday, February 17: The Alert That Arrived Late

The baseball opener began beneath a sky that could not make up its mind.

Sunlight crossed the infield while dark clouds gathered beyond left field. Nia sat with DJ and Imani, still carrying the argument with Keisha like a stone in her coat.

During the fourth inning, DJ's phone buzzed with a campus weather alert warning of lightning nearby. Nia's phone remained silent. Imani had received a text from her softball coach five minutes earlier. Students in the upper rows began leaving, but the public-address system said nothing.

"Did you get an alert?" DJ asked.

"No."

The umpire stopped play several minutes later. Only then did the stadium announcement instruct everyone to move indoors.

Rain arrived while people crowded the concourse.

Nia looked around. Some students had left before the delay. Some learned from friends. Others waited until the official announcement. One older visitor asked what the phone message had said because she did not use the campus app.

Same storm. Different information. Different departure times. Different congestion.

DJ tilted his screen toward Nia. "Your research face is happening."

"The warning did not reach us equally."

"My phone is on the football-team system too. That may be why mine came first."

Imani added, "Coaches get alerts through athletics. Students get the campus app. Visitors get whatever somebody tells them."

The reviewer had not asked the team to model everything. She had exposed one assumption that controlled the entire sequence.

Nia texted Keisha: **You were right. I was defensive. I want to explain without asking you to forgive me immediately.**

Keisha replied after practice: **Tomorrow. Bring the alert times.**

## Wednesday, February 18: A Role With Edges

Julian had already heard about the weather-delay confusion.

Campus radio received the official notice after athletics but before the stadium announcement. Several volunteers had reposted the alert on personal accounts, each using different wording.

"We can test message designs," he told Nia and Keisha. "Not emergency behavior. Just whether people understand what an alert asks them to do, how urgent it feels, and what information they think is missing."

Nia looked at Keisha before answering.

Keisha nodded. "If participation is voluntary and we do not collect personal emergency histories."

Julian suggested recruiting campus-media volunteers who could evaluate sample messages and explain how wording, audio, captions, and timing affected understanding.

"I will need a defined role," he said. "I do not want to become the person responsible for every public-facing decision because I know where the microphones are."

They wrote the role together.

Julian would advise on message design, coordinate a small comprehension test after Dr. Price approved it, document revisions, and manage the presentation animation. He would not conduct community interviews, interpret evacuation behavior, or alter the mathematical model without the co-leads.

Nia added authorship and credit terms.

"This is impressively specific," Julian said.

"Keisha has constitutionalized the project."

"Because your monarchy failed," Keisha replied.

Nia accepted the joke because she had earned it.

They sent the written role to Dr. Price. The project had gained another collaborator without pretending collaboration meant unlimited access to his time.

## Thursday, February 19: Criticism Is Not Disloyalty

Nia apologized in the research lab.

She did not begin with intention.

"When you answered the reviewer honestly, I treated it like you had embarrassed me instead of protected the project," she said. "I wanted loyalty to mean agreeing with my defense. That was unfair to you as a coauthor."

Keisha closed the data window but did not soften the moment for her.

"You also made me absorb the criticism you would not accept," she said.

"Yes."

"And if that happens at the symposium, I will answer honestly again."

"You should."

Keisha waited long enough to see whether Nia would add a condition.

Nia did not.

They rebuilt the proposal around information flow. The model would no longer begin with every driver receiving one warning at the same instant. It would test several notification patterns: immediate official alerts, delayed delivery, partial reach, and staggered word-of-mouth spread. They would not claim the patterns represented every neighborhood. They would show how communication failure changed departure timing and route congestion.

The new design made the project harder. It also connected the road network to the people trying to use it.

Keisha restored Nia's access to the presentation controls, an action she described as "conditional democratic reform."

Nia laughed, then became serious. "Thank you for not protecting my confidence at the expense of the work."

"That is not what confidence is for."

On the whiteboard, Nia drew three alert sequences spreading through the same network at different rates.

The next mathematical unit would ask whether an infinite sequence approached a stable limit. Their project now had a more urgent question: after repeated updates, did useful information reach everyone soon enough to matter?
