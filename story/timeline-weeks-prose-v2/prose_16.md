# Week Sixteen: Two Honest Losses

The radio booth sounded different when it was off.

No music filled the pauses. No control-board lights blinked beneath Julian's hands. No red **ON AIR** sign gave either of them permission to perform.

Nia sat in the guest chair where she had corrected his domain-and-range joke in August. Julian sat at the console, turned away from the microphone.

Outside the glass, Bellwether Media House moved through a normal Friday. Students carried cameras down the hall. Somebody laughed near the editing rooms. A printer made the same grinding noise every thirty seconds.

Inside, Nia and Julian faced the question they had postponed for several days.

"I missed you this week," Julian said.

"I missed you too."

The answer did not solve anything.

Friday was the final day to withdraw from fall courses, and campus had spent the morning discussing which difficult things could still be left. Nia had not expected the question to follow her into the booth.

## Friday, November 14: Compatible

Julian placed both hands flat on the desk.

"I keep thinking about the good parts," he said. "The first rehearsal. Copper Cup. The game. You making fun of my math on air."

"Your math deserved it."

He smiled. So did she.

Then the smile hurt.

Nia remembered his first kiss, the way he asked permission. She remembered him giving verified storm information instead of chasing attention. She remembered the copper tape around his wrist, quiet dinners, and the care he took with stories when he was not trying to make their relationship into one.

"We do love each other," she said.

Julian looked down. "I think so."

"That sounds uncertain."

"Not about caring. About what word makes this better instead of harder."

Nia understood.

They needed different things from closeness. Nia wanted private conversation deep enough to make her feel known. Julian experienced connection through shared moments, visible celebration, and being welcomed into the stories that mattered. Under pressure, she withheld conflicts until she believed she could solve them. Under pressure, he turned private tension into public ease.

Neither need was shameful.

Together, the pattern exhausted them.

"Affection has not made us compatible," Nia said.

Julian's eyes shone. "No."

The word was quiet.

It still changed the room.

## Saturday, November 15: No Villain

They met once more Saturday beneath the arcade outside Media House.

Julian brought the scarf Nia had left in the radio booth. Nia brought a book he had loaned her about documentary storytelling.

The exchange looked too ordinary for the end of a first love.

"I am sorry about the jokes," Julian said. "Especially at the exhibition. I made you carry my jealousy in front of other people."

"I am sorry I kept deciding what you could handle instead of telling you the truth," Nia replied. "And for making every conflict a test of whether you understood me without explanation."

"I wanted to understand."

"I know."

"I also wanted you to need me in the part of your life where Malik already understood the language."

Nia could have argued about Malik. There was no reason now.

"That was not fair to you," she said. "Or to us."

Julian gave her the scarf. Their fingers touched.

"So this is it?"

Nia wanted him to have betrayed her. She wanted one terrible act she could point to whenever she missed him. Instead she had memories of two people who had tried, apologized, repeated patterns, and reached an honest limit.

"Yes," she said.

They hugged for a long time.

When they separated, Julian wiped his face and laughed once at himself. "I had a better exit planned."

"Of course you did."

"It had lighting."

Nia laughed through tears.

They walked in opposite directions without anger strong enough to carry either one home.

## Sunday, November 16: The Space Between

Test three asked students to find the area between two curves.

Nia sketched both functions, found their intersections, determined which curve lay above the other, and built the integral of top minus bottom.

Every difficult step created the same impulse: text Julian.

When she found the second intersection after an algebra mistake, she wanted to send him a victory message. When the substitution simplified cleanly, she wanted to tell him calculus had briefly decided to behave. When another student dropped a calculator, she wanted to share the joke she knew he would make.

Her phone remained zipped inside her bag.

The area problem changed where the curves crossed. One expression stayed on top over the first interval; the order reversed over the second. Nia split the integral instead of allowing signed area to cancel the total region she was asked to measure.

She checked the limits, evaluated each part, and added the positive areas.

Grief did not behave so neatly. Good memories and painful ones did not cancel into zero. Missing Julian did not prove the breakup was wrong. Knowing the breakup was right did not make the missing smaller.

Nia finished the test with four minutes left.

Outside, she texted Keisha: **Done. Please tell me there is food.**

Keisha replied: **Food, silence, and one legally binding ban on checking his page.**

Nia smiled for the first time that day.

## Monday, November 17: Transferability

The Heron Analytics regional semifinal took place in a conference center two hours from Bellwether.

McCall-Hart presented third.

Simone opened with the decision context. Priya explained the data. Andre described missingness and tagging. Malik presented the uncertainty intervals, showing when the model favored a fourth-down attempt and when the evidence left multiple defensible options.

The timing held. The visualizations worked. Nobody hid the limitations.

During questions, a judge from another university leaned toward the microphone.

"Your model incorporates opponent-specific tendencies and McCall-Hart possession values," she said. "What evidence supports transferring these decision thresholds to a team in another conference with different personnel and game pace?"

Malik knew the honest answer: not enough.

He also knew the answer he wanted to give.

"The framework is general," he began. "Another program could replace the inputs with its own data and estimate team-specific thresholds."

"Have you validated that process on an external team?"

"Not yet."

"Then how do you know the structure transfers rather than only the interface?"

Malik paused.

The room waited.

"We do not know," he said. "We would need external validation across programs and probably a revised model for differences in pace, roster strength, and data quality."

The answer was honest.

It was also incomplete because the work itself was incomplete.

After the session, Malik replayed the question until every possible better response arrived too late.

## Tuesday, November 18: Does Not Advance

The finalist announcement appeared on a screen in the conference lobby.

Bayland Institute advanced. So did two other universities.

McCall-Hart did not.

For several seconds, nobody on the team spoke. Around them, another group shouted and embraced.

Malik stared at the list.

"I lost it on transferability," he said.

Simone turned toward him. "We did not have transferability evidence."

"I could have explained the adaptation process better."

"That would not create validation we did not perform."

"The judge asked me."

"Because you presented that section. The model belonged to all of us."

Dr. Bennett gathered the team for a brief debrief and told them not to diagnose the loss in the lobby. They would review scores and comments when available.

On the bus home, Malik opened his presentation notes and rewrote the answer.

Then he rewrote it again.

Every version sounded better than the one he had given. None supplied the missing evidence.

He closed the file only when Simone reached across the aisle and lowered his laptop screen with one finger.

"You are trying to turn grief into editing," she said.

"It was a competition."

"It was also work you cared about. You are allowed to be disappointed before becoming educational."

## Wednesday, November 19: What Failed

The official feedback praised McCall-Hart's uncertainty communication and decision framing. It questioned external validation, sensitivity to data quality, and whether the model would support real-time use under limited staff capacity.

Andre called the scoring inconsistent with another team's presentation.

Malik wanted to agree.

Simone did not.

"We are not calling the result unfair because we dislike it," she said. "If there was a procedural error, document it. If not, document the weaknesses."

She created a postmortem file with four headings: **What Worked**, **What Failed**, **What We Do Not Know**, and **What We Would Test Next**.

Malik read the headings. "Can this wait?"

"The emotions can take time. The evidence should not. We will forget details and rewrite history to protect ourselves."

The team listed what worked: honest intervals, strong visual hierarchy, clear decision context, disciplined timing.

Then the failures: no external validation, limited evidence across conferences, incomplete real-time workflow testing, and an interface easier to understand than to operationalize.

"We can discuss next year after this is complete," Simone said.

The loss remained a loss.

It also became legible.

## Thursday, November 20: Opposite Ends

Malik found Nia on a couch at North Star after closing.

She sat at one end with her shoes tucked beneath her. He sat at the other, leaving the middle cushion empty.

"We broke up," Nia said.

"We did not advance," Malik replied.

Neither offered congratulations on honesty or speeches about better things ahead.

Nia looked toward the dark tutoring rooms. "I keep wanting to text him when something happens. Then I remember the person I would tell is the person I am grieving."

Malik nodded. "I keep rewriting one answer as if the right sentence would change the evidence we did not collect."

"Do you want to try again next year?"

"I do not know. Do you want to be friends with Julian?"

"I do not know."

They sat with two futures that had existed a week earlier and no longer did.

Malik did not move closer. Nia did not turn his loss into perspective about hers. They shared vending-machine pretzels and the kind of silence that did not demand improvement.

When Ms. Alvarez told them the building was closing, they stood.

Outside, River Classic banners had begun appearing along Cypress Walk.

The campus was already preparing to celebrate something else.
