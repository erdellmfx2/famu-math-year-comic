# Week Thirty-Nine: No Shortcut Through Finals

The Calculus II final began with a page titled **METHOD SELECTION**.

Before computing anything, students had to identify a technique for six problems and justify each choice. Substitution. Integration by parts. Partial fractions. A convergence test. Parametric arc length. Polar area.

No answer from a later page could rescue a method chosen without reasoning.

Nia read the full set before writing. Malik did the same.

All year, both had tried to use action as reassurance. Start calculating. Build the schedule. Enter the relationship. Give the presentation. Movement felt safer than uncertainty.

The exam demanded a commitment before computation offered comfort.

They began.

## Friday, April 24: Choose Before the Work Begins

Nia labeled each method in the margin.

For the rational function, she factored the denominator before choosing partial fractions. For the series, she noted that factorial growth made the ratio test efficient. For polar area, she sketched the interval to avoid tracing the region twice.

Malik identified integration by parts, then checked which factor should become (u). The obvious-looking choice was not always the one that simplified under differentiation.

Dr. Delgado moved silently between rows.

The last full spring class week ended with no speeches. Students carried notes, snacks, and exhaustion toward finals. Outside, organizations advertised move-out drives and summer storage. The campus had begun preparing to empty while everyone still had work left.

Nia saw Malik at the water fountain during a break. They exchanged one nod.

No whispered predictions. No emotional check-in. Their agreement held.

The final was not a metaphor they could solve for their relationship. It was an exam, and each needed to complete it independently.

That fact felt almost restful.

## Saturday, April 25: Restart While the Clock Moves

Malik reached the integration-by-parts problem and moved too fast.

He selected (u) poorly, differentiated, integrated the remaining factor, and produced a new integral worse than the original. In October, he might have forced the method forward rather than admit the first choice had failed.

The clock displayed fifty-two minutes remaining.

He crossed out the setup.

Restarting cost time. Continuing would cost the problem.

Malik chose a logarithmic factor for (u), simplified correctly, and completed the integral. He marked the abandoned attempt clearly so Dr. Delgado could see the correction rather than mistake it for competing work.

The restart changed his pace. For the next problem, he paused before moving.

He thought briefly of Simone, then returned to the page. Their relationship could not be restarted with a better method. The lesson was not that every wrong choice deserved another attempt. It was that honesty about a failing process mattered before the clock decided for you.

Malik completed the final line with nine minutes left.

He used all nine to check assumptions.

## Sunday, April 26: Distrusting the Right Instinct

Nia recognized the divergent series immediately.

The terms did not approach zero. The nth-term test settled the question.

She wrote the conclusion, then doubted it.

The fellowship rejection had made confidence feel suspicious. She had given a strong talk and still been judged incomplete. Now every clear instinct seemed like possible evidence that she had missed something.

Nia attempted a comparison test. Then she considered the ratio test. Neither was necessary. Minutes disappeared while she tried to prove the result three different ways.

Finally, she heard Dr. Delgado's review-session warning: use the simplest test that resolves the question.

She crossed out the extra work and restored the original argument. If the terms of an infinite series failed to approach zero, the series diverged. No elaborate proof made the conclusion more true.

Confidence was not certainty that every instinct was right. It was the ability to test an instinct against an appropriate standard and stop when the evidence was sufficient.

Nia moved to the next problem with less time than she wanted and enough time to finish.

## Monday, April 27: Work Without Points

When the exam ended, students flooded the hallway and began reconstructing questions from memory.

"Was the polar interval zero to pi or zero to two pi?"

"What did you get for the ratio limit?"

"Did anybody finish the arc length?"

Dr. Delgado stepped into the doorway. "I will not grade by hallway vote. Stop conducting statistical analysis on a sample selected by panic."

He refused every request to estimate scores.

Instead, he asked one final question.

"What work from this course would you still choose if no points were attached?"

Amara named tutoring another student through identities because teaching had made her less afraid to speak. Malik chose risk problems where the method changed a decision. Nia chose the evacuation research, though the fellowship had assigned it no reward.

"Then protect that answer from grades," Dr. Delgado said. "A course evaluates specific work under specific conditions. It does not own every reason you learned."

Outside, Nia did not ask Malik how he performed. He did not ask her.

They left the result where it belonged: with the exam.

## Tuesday, April 28: One Uncomplicated Celebration

The softball rivalry game reached the bottom of the seventh tied.

McCall-Hart needed the win to secure a stronger conference-tournament seed. Two outs. Runner on second. Imani at the plate.

The first pitch missed outside. The second became a foul. The third came low and fast.

Imani drove it through the left side.

The runner rounded third. The throw arrived late. Safe.

The dugout emptied.

Imani disappeared beneath teammates, dirt, and an indigo sports-drink shower. The crowd shouted until every difficult conversation, rejected fellowship, final exam, and uncertain summer briefly lost volume.

Nia hugged Keisha. DJ nearly climbed the fence. Malik laughed when the celebration carried Imani's helmet in one direction and Imani in another.

The walk-off did not teach them how to handle loss. It did not symbolize convergence, trust, or second chances.

It was simply wonderful.

For one night, nobody translated it.

## Wednesday, April 29: Continue Under New Conditions

Dr. Price invited Nia and Keisha to her office.

The summer Quantitative Scholars program had space for the evacuation project to continue. The invitation came with conditions.

The team would not repackage the symposium model and pursue another award. They would build a community-validation plan, obtain appropriate approvals, expand transportation assumptions, and share governance with local partners. Dr. Renee Okafor would oversee the mathematical work alongside Dr. Price.

"This is not the fellowship," Dr. Price said.

"I know," Nia replied.

The program was campus-based, paid less, and offered no national title. It also provided time, mentoring, and a path to make the rejected work useful.

Nia looked at Keisha. "Co-leads?"

"In writing," Keisha said.

Dr. Price slid the draft appointments across the desk. Nia was project lead. Keisha was computation co-lead.

Keisha raised one eyebrow.

Nia did not accept.

"Both titles need to say co-lead," she said. "Different responsibilities, equal project authority."

Dr. Price nodded and marked the revision.

They took the offer home to consider.

The work had continued because its questions remained. It had not transformed the no into a yes.

## Thursday, April 30: A Day Before the Answer

Dr. Bennett invited Malik to join the same summer project as a risk-modeling assistant.

"Your actuarial perspective could strengthen the uncertainty and loss assumptions," he said. "You would report to the project co-leads and Dr. Okafor."

Malik understood immediately that Nia and Keisha were the co-leads.

He also understood what accepting would mean: daily work with Nia, unresolved feelings, and a project tied to storms like the one that had damaged his family home.

"I am interested," he said. "Can I have a day before answering?"

Dr. Bennett smiled. "That is why I did not hand you a pen."

Malik reviewed the role rather than the romantic possibility. The project needed scenario risk, documentation of uncertain loss, and validation that did not privilege only insured property. His family experience could inform questions, but he would not turn private damage into evidence without their permission.

He called his parents. He spoke with DJ. He did not call Nia.

Across campus, Nia reviewed her own offer with Keisha. Neither knew whether Malik had been invited.

The summer path offered proximity.

It offered work.

It offered no romantic resolution, and for the first time, Malik waited a day because the decision deserved more than the answer he wanted fastest.
