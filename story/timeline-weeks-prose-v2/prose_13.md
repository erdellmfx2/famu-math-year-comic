# Week Thirteen: Beyond the Campus Map

The insurance company wanted proof that a drill press had existed before water destroyed it.

Malik read the request during his Friday break at North Star. Around him, students ate vending-machine dinners and argued quietly over derivatives. On his laptop, a claims portal listed six items as **NEEDS ADDITIONAL DOCUMENTATION**.

Drill press. Air compressor. Power saw. Custom tool cabinet. Two small appliances. School-record storage.

His father had owned the drill press for eleven years. It had repaired lawn equipment, shaped replacement parts, and helped Marcel earn money from jobs too small for larger shops to accept. It appeared in family photos only as a gray shape in the background.

Malik called home.

"They need a receipt," he said.

"From eleven years ago?" Marcel asked.

"Or a dated photo, bank record, warranty, repair invoice, anything showing model and ownership."

"It was in my shop. The water did not ask for paperwork before ruining it."

Malik closed his eyes.

"I know."

"Do you?"

The question was unfair. It also came from a man looking at the damaged equipment that had once made him independent.

Malik opened a new evidence folder.

## Friday, October 24: Evidence of a Life

During his twenty-minute break, Malik searched years of family messages.

He found a photograph from Micah's tenth birthday. In the background, Marcel stood beside the drill press cutting a replacement bracket before the party. Another image showed the tool cabinet during a garage cleanup. A bank statement contained a payment to a repair shop that had serviced the compressor.

Malik renamed each file with dates and item descriptions. He wrote short notes connecting the images to the inventory.

Ms. Alvarez passed the desk carrying returned calculators. "Eat."

"I have fourteen minutes."

"Which is enough time to eat and not enough time to solve insurance."

He opened the sandwich Nia had made him buy before his shift.

The claims process wanted a clean history for every object. Families did not live that way. They bought used tools in cash, repaired appliances instead of replacing them, inherited furniture, misplaced receipts, and assumed the things surrounding them would not someday need to prove themselves.

Malik submitted the new evidence.

The portal changed from **NEEDS ADDITIONAL DOCUMENTATION** to **UNDER REVIEW**.

It was not approval.

It was movement.

## Saturday, October 25: The Missing Constant

Dr. Brooks introduced antiderivatives by erasing part of a story.

She drew a graph of velocity over time, then asked the class to recover a possible position function.

Students reversed the derivative rules. Malik integrated the polynomial terms. Nia checked by differentiating the result.

"Are we finished?" Dr. Brooks asked.

"Plus C," Amara said.

Dr. Brooks wrote the constant of integration in a box.

The rate of change could reveal how position evolved, but not where the object began. Without an initial value, an entire family of histories fit the same derivative.

"Same rate, different starting point," Nia said.

"Exactly. If I give you one position at one time, you can select a particular history. Without it, do not pretend the rate tells you everything."

Dr. Brooks gave them an initial-value problem: a shuttle's velocity was known, along with its position at noon. Malik found the family of antiderivatives, used the noon position to determine the missing constant, and checked the result by differentiating. Nia drew two candidate position curves that shared the same rate but began miles apart.

"The derivative tells us how the story moves," she said. "The initial value tells us which story we are in."

For the final question, they computed accumulated change across an interval and added it to the starting position. The process felt almost investigative: recover what could not be observed directly from the rate that remained.

Malik thought about the insurance inventory. The current loss did not reveal the family's starting point: which objects were inherited, repaired, or essential to income. Numbers showing damage could not reconstruct every history unless someone supplied the missing constants.

After class, Nia reviewed Malik's Delta application statement one last time.

"You wrote about the storm," she said.

"The lab studies risk."

"You also wrote about your father's equipment."

"Risk is financial too."

Her own statement focused on visualizing evacuation choices so communities could question assumptions instead of merely receiving an official map.

Both had been selected.

The bus left before dawn Sunday.

## Sunday, October 26: Beyond the Gate

At 4:50 a.m., Hart Student Union looked like a gathering of ghosts carrying travel mugs.

Malik arrived with a backpack organized by category. Nia arrived wearing two different socks and blamed the hour.

"They are both blue," she said.

"One is indigo. One is navy."

"At five in the morning, all colors are rumors."

Dr. Price checked names against the travel list. The selected group boarded the bus for Delta National Laboratory, a federal research campus several hours from Bellwether.

Nia texted Julian: **Boarding now. I will call after orientation.**

He replied with a heart and a request for photos if security rules allowed them.

By the time the bus reached Delta's guarded entrance, phones had to be silenced and stored during security orientation. Students received visitor badges, data-handling rules, photography restrictions, and a direct warning that curiosity did not override consent or clearance.

Julian messaged twice during the long briefing.

Nia did not see either message.

Malik did not see his mother's update that the adjuster had delayed a decision.

Inside the laboratory, their ordinary campus concerns did not disappear. They waited beyond the gate while the students entered a world of controlled rooms, wall-sized displays, and questions too large for one spreadsheet.

## Monday, October 27: Water in Motion

Dr. Arturo Vega led the storm-surge demonstration.

On a visualization wall, a model coastline appeared beneath layers of wind, pressure, elevation, and water depth. The simulation advanced. Blue spread inland through channels, across low roads, and into neighborhoods that looked safe on a simple distance map.

Nia moved closer. "Can you separate the layers?"

Dr. Vega toggled rainfall, river flow, and coastal surge. "The image is not decoration. Each layer should answer a question. Too many at once can make uncertainty look like authority."

Malik asked how the model represented household-level loss.

"It does not, directly," Dr. Vega said. "We estimate physical exposure. Economists, public-health experts, emergency managers, and communities help interpret consequences. A water depth is not the same loss for every household."

He displayed two homes under the same projected depth. One had flood-resistant materials and savings. The other housed a family with limited transportation and no easy place to relocate.

"Same hazard," Malik said. "Different vulnerability."

"Exactly."

Nia began sketching a visual that paired route options with uncertainty and access needs. Malik filled a page with questions about repair delays, work interruption, and how models valued equipment used for income.

They approached the simulation from different directions and kept arriving at the same point: a forecast mattered only through the decisions people could make with it.

## Tuesday, October 28: Failure Is Information

Dr. Lena Washington began her presentation with photographs of models that had failed.

She was a Black applied mathematician who led a validation group at Delta. One slide showed a flood map that underestimated water movement through an industrial canal. Another showed a traffic model that performed well in simulation and badly during a real evacuation.

"Nobody invites you to a national laboratory to admire your perfect first draft," she said. "We need you to find where a model stops deserving trust."

Students laughed, then became quiet as she described validation: compare predictions with observations, test extreme cases, examine transferability, document bias, and revise or reject the model when evidence demanded it.

Malik thought of the missing-data chart and the calculus score he had hidden.

During questions, he asked, "How do you keep a failed model from feeling like a verdict on whether you belong in the work?"

Dr. Washington studied him for a moment.

"You separate accountability from identity," she said. "If your method failed, you must name it, learn from it, and protect people from the consequences. But a failed test is information about the work. It is not a complete measurement of the worker."

Nia wrote the answer down word for word.

Malik did not. He wanted to remember it without turning it into another line he could hide behind.

## Wednesday, October 29: Work That Matters

On the ride back to Bellwether, Nia and Malik sat across the aisle from each other, talking over the low engine noise while most of the bus slept.

"I used to think applied math meant getting the classroom answer into the real world," Nia said. "Now it feels like the real world changes what counts as an answer."

Malik looked at the photographs he had been allowed to take: a public lobby display, the group beneath the Delta sign, Nia pointing at a permitted visualization poster.

"My family had forecasts," he said. "They still lost things. I keep wondering whether a better model would have changed anything."

"Maybe not everything."

"That is the problem."

"Or maybe that is the honest reason to do it. Not to promise no loss. To give people more time, clearer choices, or one less surprise."

Malik looked out at the dark highway. "One less surprise is not nothing."

They talked about majors, summer research, and the terrifying possibility that the questions they cared about might become careers. The conversation felt intense without feeling romantic to either of them. It was the closeness of two young people recognizing that their private fears could become public work.

Nia posted three approved photos after the bus returned. One showed her and Malik laughing beneath the Delta visitor sign.

Julian saw that image before he heard her voice.

## Thursday, October 30: What He Was Asking

Julian met Nia outside Bellwether Media House.

"You did not call," he said.

"Security orientation ran long. Then we had sessions, and the hotel Wi-Fi was terrible. I texted when I could."

"You posted."

"After we got back."

"With Malik."

Nia's patience tightened. "There were six students in the group."

"The picture is you and Malik."

"So this is jealousy."

Julian looked away. "Partly."

The honesty should have slowed her. Instead, she was tired and already defending herself.

"Malik is my friend. I am not going to apologize for having a meaningful conversation with someone who understands the work."

"I did not ask you to apologize."

"You are acting like the photo proves something."

"I am acting like a big part of your life happened, and I learned about it through the same post as everybody else. Again."

Nia opened her mouth, then stopped.

Julian continued. "I do not understand the math the way he does. I wanted you to tell me what mattered. I wanted a way into the story."

"You could have asked without making it about Malik."

"I am asking now, badly."

There it was: jealousy, yes, but also exclusion. Nia heard only the first part clearly enough to answer.

"I cannot keep proving our relationship by making my friendships smaller," she said.

Julian's face closed. "And I cannot keep pretending I do not notice where you feel most understood."

They separated without discussing the laboratory at all.

The trip had expanded Nia's world. The argument made her wonder why bringing that world home felt so difficult.
