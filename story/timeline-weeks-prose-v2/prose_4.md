# Week Four: The First Limit

On the first morning of Calculus I, Dr. Camille Brooks drew a graph that looked like it was lying.

The curve traveled smoothly toward an open circle at a height of four. Directly above that empty point, at the same horizontal position, sat a solid dot at seven.

Dr. Brooks placed the chalk on the tray and faced the class.

"What is the answer?"

Malik's hand rose before the final word stopped echoing.

"Seven," he said. "The filled point gives the function value."

"Correct," Dr. Brooks said.

Malik lowered his hand.

"And incomplete."

His hand stopped halfway to the desk.

Around him, the room shifted into attention. Eliza Moss Hall had traded its Bridge-program looseness for the official energy of a first college course. New notebooks lay open. Laptops glowed. Everybody had chosen a seat that might become theirs for the semester.

Nia sat beside Malik, turning a copper pen between her fingers. Two rows ahead, a quiet student named Amara Lewis studied the graph without looking up.

Dr. Brooks circled the empty point. "The function's value is seven. But what value does the graph approach?"

"Four," Nia said.

"Also correct. So which answer tells us what the function is doing?"

Malik looked from the solid dot to the open circle. He disliked questions that allowed two correct answers to stand in the same room without fighting.

Dr. Brooks smiled as if she could see the discomfort crossing his face.

"Welcome to limits," she said.

## Friday, August 22: The Answer Beside the Answer

Dr. Brooks did not begin with notation. She began with approach.

She traced the graph from the left, then from the right. Students described what the outputs did as the input moved closer to the marked value. The function could be defined at seven, undefined entirely, or assigned almost anything at that single point without changing the value the surrounding graph approached.

"A limit asks about behavior nearby," Dr. Brooks said. "Not necessarily what happens at the destination itself."

Nia leaned toward Malik. "Math has trust issues."

"Math has definitions."

"Same thing with better symbols."

Malik copied the formal notation, but his attention remained on the graph. In most of his work, arriving at the answer ended the problem. Here, the course was asking him to study what happened before arrival, to separate a visible point from the pattern surrounding it.

At the end of class, Dr. Brooks assigned a short reflection: explain the difference between a function value and a limit to someone who had never taken calculus.

Malik wrote three precise sentences.

Nia drew a bus approaching a stop, then added a passenger who appeared at a completely different location.

"That is absurd," Malik said.

"That is why people will remember it."

"The passenger represents the function value?"

"Exactly. The bus approaches one place. Somebody drops the passenger somewhere weird. Related location, different story."

Malik stared at the drawing. "Your bus metaphor is mathematically reckless."

"But you understood it."

He hated when she won with evidence.

## Saturday, August 23: Too Ready to Speak

Saturday's review workshop was crowded with students still deciding whether Calculus I belonged on their schedules. Add-drop week had made every seat temporary.

Dr. Brooks displayed three graphs and asked the class to compare their left-hand and right-hand behavior. When silence lasted more than two seconds, Nia filled it.

"On the first graph, both sides approach three, so the limit is three even though the point is missing. On the second, the left side approaches negative one and the right side approaches two, so the two-sided limit does not exist. And on the third--"

"Pause," Dr. Brooks said gently.

Nia stopped.

Amara's hand was halfway raised in the row ahead.

"Amara," Dr. Brooks said, "would you take the third?"

Amara explained that the graph grew without bound from one side. Her voice was soft, but her reasoning was exact. She named the one-sided behavior and carefully avoided calling infinity a function value.

"Well done," Dr. Brooks said.

Nia sank lower in her chair.

Afterward, she caught Amara near the stairs.

"I am sorry," Nia said. "I saw your hand and kept talking anyway."

Amara adjusted the strap of her bag. "You probably did not mean to."

"I didn't. But I still did it."

Amara's expression softened. "Sometimes I take longer to decide how I want to say something. By the time I am ready, the class has moved on."

Nia nodded. "Next time, I can leave some air in the room."

"You do not have to stop answering."

"Good, because that might require medical supervision."

Amara laughed.

When Nia told Keisha about it later, she expected reassurance. Keisha was lacing her running shoes and offered none.

"You apologized?"

"Yes."

"You know what to do differently?"

"Yes."

"Then do that. Guilt is not community service."

Nia threw a pillow at her. Keisha caught it without looking.

## Sunday, August 24: Alternate

The Heron Analytics email arrived at 3:17 Sunday afternoon.

Malik read the subject line twice: **Fall Team Selection Decision**.

DJ sat on his bed reviewing formations on the television. "Open it before your face cracks."

Malik clicked.

The message praised his screening performance, especially his interpretation and written discussion of model limits. Due to roster capacity and his limited experience, he had been selected as an alternate. He could attend open reviews, complete practice assignments, and step into a trial if a position became available.

Alternate.

Not rejected. Not chosen.

The word occupied the most frustrating possible location between two outcomes.

"Well?" DJ asked.

"Alternate."

"Is that good?"

"It is what I expected."

DJ paused the film. "That sentence did not answer me."

"It is a reasonable placement for a first-year."

"Still not an answer."

Malik closed the laptop. "I can attend reviews."

"And you wanted the team."

"I wanted access to the work. I have access."

He repeated the same version to Nia, to his parents, and later to Dr. Bennett when he confirmed the alternate position. Each repetition became smoother.

Only Simone refused to let it pass.

"You can be disappointed and still accept the role," she told him outside Porter Center. "Those facts are not mutually exclusive."

"I am not disappointed."

"Then you are doing an incredible impression of somebody swallowing a stapler."

Malik looked away.

Simone did not press. "Open review is Monday evening. Alternates are allowed to contribute. Contribution is not the same as auditioning every second you are in the room."

He heard the warning. He immediately began planning an audition.

## Monday, August 25: Useful Work

Fall classes officially began Monday, turning McCall-Hart's pathways into fast-moving rivers of students.

DJ returned from practice with two printouts and dropped them on Malik's desk.

"I need a second brain."

"What happened to the first one?"

"It has been awake since five."

The printouts showed two sets of play-call results from practice. One suggested a short route worked best from a certain formation. The other suggested it failed more often than it succeeded.

"Coach wants us to learn tendencies," DJ said. "These tables look like they are arguing."

Malik compared the labels. The first dataset included all practice repetitions. The second included only plays against the starting defense.

"Different domains," he said.

"In human language?"

"The first tells you what happened against everyone. The second tells you what happened against the strongest unit. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions."

DJ sat beside him. "So if I am trying to earn game snaps, which one matters?"

"Both. The full set shows whether the play is generally reliable. The starter set shows where the defense exposes it. We should compare the route depth and coverage too."

They worked until Malik had a small chart separating opponent unit, coverage type, and result. DJ carried it to his position meeting.

That evening, he texted: **Coach said this was useful. Do not become unbearable.**

Malik saved the message.

At analytics review, he did not lead with a grand model. He showed Simone the same comparison and explained what the data could and could not support.

"Useful," she said.

The word should have felt smaller than *selected*. It did not.

## Tuesday, August 26: Define the Date

Nia met Julian at Copper Cup Cafe between classes. He had saved her a seat beside the window and ordered the iced tea she had chosen during their first coffee negotiation.

"You remembered," she said.

"I conduct interviews. Details are the whole job."

"That answer almost ruined the sweetness."

"Almost?"

"You remain under review."

They talked about first classes. Julian's documentary course had assigned a one-minute portrait without dialogue. Nia described limits with enough enthusiasm to make the people at the next table glance over.

"So the graph can be going toward one answer while the actual point is somewhere else," Julian said.

"Yes."

"That sounds emotionally dangerous."

"Calculus is full of warnings."

Julian stirred his coffee. "Speaking of clarity, I want to make sure we are describing Thursday the same way. Heritage Kickoff. You and me. A date."

Nia's smile arrived before she could manage it. "An official date?"

"Unless there is paperwork I missed."

"No paperwork. I might make a rubric."

"I am suddenly less confident."

"Good. Healthy uncertainty."

They agreed to meet outside the union after Nia finished her homework set. She entered it in her planner while Julian watched.

"Personal growth again?" he asked.

"I am becoming a woman who checks her calendar before creating problems for herself."

"I support her."

Nia liked the ease between them. She liked even more that he had called the date what it was.

## Wednesday, August 27: Leave Some Air

The group limit problem began with four students and one piecewise graph.

Malik handled the algebraic simplification. Nia explained the left-hand behavior. When the group reached the right-hand limit, she looked at Amara.

"Do you want this part?"

Amara studied the expression. "Give me a second."

Nia gave her one.

Then another.

The silence felt much longer than it was. Nia resisted the itch to rescue it.

Amara pointed to the denominator. "We cannot substitute yet, but after factoring, the behavior from the right is positive. The magnitude grows because the denominator approaches zero."

Malik leaned closer. "So the one-sided limit is positive infinity, while the left is negative infinity."

"Which means the two-sided limit does not exist," Amara finished.

Their argument was complete.

When the groups presented, Malik surprised Nia by saying, "Amara should explain the final step. She caught the sign change."

Amara did.

Outside class, Nia bumped Malik's arm. "Look at you sharing airtime."

"She had the strongest explanation."

"You could let this be growth."

"I am reporting evidence."

"Growth with a clipboard. Still growth."

## Thursday, August 28: What Malik Does Not Say

Nia emerged from the residence hall wearing copper earrings, an indigo top, and the expression of someone trying not to look like she had thought carefully about looking effortless.

Malik was crossing the courtyard on his way to Porter Center when he saw her.

"Too much?" she asked, turning once.

"For an outdoor event in August? Possibly."

"I meant the outfit."

He looked at her properly. "You look nice."

"Nice?"

"Very nice."

"Better."

Julian approached from Cypress Walk with his saxophone case over one shoulder. He had changed out of rehearsal clothes and carried two event passes.

"Ready?" he asked Nia.

She looked back at Malik. "Good luck at analytics review."

"Have fun."

He meant it. That was the irritating part.

Nia and Julian walked away together, their conversation easy before they reached the path. Malik stood still until DJ called from an upstairs window.

"My brother! That is what feelings look like when you refuse to process them!"

Malik looked up. "Close the window."

At Porter Center, he opened a blank project document and created a work plan for his alternate period: opponent tendency tables, fourth-down conversions, field-position splits, weather variables, player availability, model comparison, and a dashboard no one had requested.

Simone read the list over his shoulder.

"This is six weeks of work," she said.

"I can finish a first version in ten days."

"Why?"

"To contribute."

She studied him. "You are allowed to have a life outside proving you should be in this room."

Malik added a deadline beside the dashboard.

The first week of calculus had taught him that the value at a point and the behavior around it could disagree. Officially, he was an alternate. Nearby, he was working like a starter. Nia was his accountability partner. Nearby, she was walking into a new relationship.

Malik understood the mathematics.

Understanding what to do with it was another problem.
