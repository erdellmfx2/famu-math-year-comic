# Week Thirty-Two: Tests of Convergence

Dr. Delgado wrote four infinite series on the board and forbade anyone from calculating.

"Choose a convergence test," he said. "Defend the choice. Then you may touch the algebra."

Students stared at the series as if they had been asked to identify four strangers from childhood photographs.

One resembled a geometric series but contained an extra factor. One had factorials that suggested the ratio test. One alternated signs. One looked suitable for comparison with a familiar benchmark.

"Can multiple tests work?" Nia asked.

"Yes."

"Then how do we know which one you want?"

"I want the one you can justify. Mathematics is not mind reading."

Malik examined the factorial series. "The ratio test exposes the structure directly."

"Good. Do not use three weaker arguments because you are afraid to commit to one strong one."

The advice landed beyond the board.

## Friday, March 6: Name the Test

Nia chose comparison for a positive-term series.

She bounded it above by a convergent benchmark, then paused. An upper bound by something convergent could establish convergence, but only if the inequalities pointed the correct way. She checked each term rather than trusting the shape.

Across the room, Malik applied the ratio test. The limit fell below one. For once, the conclusion arrived cleanly.

Amara selected the alternating-series test and established conditional convergence. Dr. Delgado asked whether absolute convergence had also been shown.

"No," Amara said. "That would require another test."

"Exactly. A result may be true under one standard and unresolved under another."

After class, Nia and Keisha prepared for the symposium decision. Their project had passed the campus semifinal, but regional reviewers could still reject it. Nia refreshed the portal twice.

"Choose a test," Keisha said.

"For what?"

"Whether the email has arrived. Test one: look at the inbox. Test two through seventeen are still looking at the inbox."

Nia placed her phone face down.

The project would either advance or it would not. Repeated observation could not influence the result.

At 4:38 p.m., the portal changed.

Neither saw it until Saturday morning.

## Saturday, March 7: Two Invitations in One Email

Keisha opened the acceptance while standing in line for oatmeal.

She made a sound so sharp that three people checked the fire alarm.

Nia ran across the dining hall. "What happened?"

Keisha held up the phone.

Their project had been accepted to the Southeastern Undergraduate Mathematics Symposium.

Below that sentence was another.

The team had also been selected for a final-round interview for the national summer fellowship.

Nia read the paragraph until the words stopped moving. Then she grabbed Keisha's hands and jumped. Keisha joined for exactly two jumps before remembering she was carrying oatmeal.

They called Julian, Dr. Price, Alexis, Nia's mother, and Keisha's track teammate who answered half asleep and screamed anyway. Nia typed a message to Malik, erased an overly emotional version, and sent the simple news.

He replied immediately: **You earned the room. Congratulations to all three of you.**

All three.

Nia noticed the wording.

The interview instructions emphasized collaboration, public relevance, and evidence of revision. Fellowship judges would examine not only the final model but how the team responded when assumptions failed.

"We have abundant failure evidence," Julian said when they met.

"Finally, a competitive advantage," Keisha replied.

Nia laughed, but fear moved beneath the joy. She had admitted how badly she wanted this. Now the desired outcome was close enough to imagine losing in detail.

## Sunday, March 8: No Single Hero in the Frame

Julian rebuilt the route animation.

The first version opened on Nia speaking beside a glowing map. Her narration drove every transition. Keisha's code appeared as a small technical label. Community interview clips functioned like supporting evidence in Nia's explanation.

Julian watched the sequence once and deleted the opening.

"What are you doing?" Nia asked.

"Fixing the point of view."

The revised version began with three contradictory warning experiences, used with permission. Keisha then showed how those differences became scenario inputs. Nia explained how route recommendations changed. Julian's visual sequence connected each update to its source and uncertainty.

No one person appeared as the project.

"You removed my strongest introduction," Nia said.

"Your introduction made everybody else's work arrive as proof that you were insightful."

Nia winced. "Accurate and rude."

"Accurate and billable."

Keisha reviewed the animation and requested clearer labels for the software's decision states. Nia shortened her narration. Julian added credits by role rather than placing three names beneath the vague phrase *project team*.

The result was easier to understand because the audience could see where each claim originated.

Nia still served as lead presenter. Leadership no longer required the camera to treat everyone else as scenery.

## Monday, March 9: The Pep-Bus Deadline

McCall-Hart's women's basketball team reached the conference final.

The student pep group organized an overnight bus. Keisha wanted to go. She also owed the research team a final code review before the fellowship-interview materials were locked.

Old Nia would have suggested Keisha skip the trip, then praised her sacrifice as commitment.

Current Nia asked, "What do you need from us to finish before departure?"

They divided the review. Nia checked whether every visual matched the correct scenario output. Julian verified that consent restrictions followed each interview clip. Keisha ran automated tests, examined the route-history logs, and froze the presentation version at 3:12 p.m.

At 3:20, she left for the pep bus wearing an indigo sweatshirt and carrying a statistics worksheet.

"You are taking homework to a championship game?" Julian asked.

"The bus has seats."

"This university does not understand leisure."

Keisha completed the worksheet before the bus crossed the county line. That evening, she sent one message to the team: **Code review clean. I am now unavailable unless the model catches fire. If it catches fire, document it.**

Nia did not contact her.

Shared ownership meant trusting the work after the owner stepped away.

## Tuesday, March 10: A Future After the Buzzer

The conference final stayed close until the final minute.

Keisha's voice disappeared somewhere in the third quarter. She texted score updates to the group, each one containing fewer vowels as tension increased.

McCall-Hart tied the game with twenty seconds left. Their opponent answered with two free throws. The Herons' final shot rolled around the rim and out.

This time, the loss did not end the season.

During the trophy ceremony, the conference announced McCall-Hart had earned a postseason invitation. The players were devastated by the final and proud of the opportunity, sometimes within the same expression.

On the bus home, Keisha joined the research team's call.

"Do not ask me to describe the last shot," she said.

"We will not," Nia replied. "How is the team?"

"Sad. Also already discussing film tomorrow."

The future did not erase the immediate result. It gave the result somewhere to go.

Nia looked at the symposium acceptance taped above her desk. If the fellowship interview went badly, other work could still follow. That did not mean she needed to reduce her hope in advance.

She could prepare for the next round and still admit the current one mattered.

## Wednesday, March 11: The Opportunity Gets a Voice

Simone's London interview lasted forty-seven minutes.

Malik waited in the Porter Center lobby with two coffees growing cold. He had helped her practice case questions, but she entered the interview alone.

When she emerged, her face revealed nothing for three steps. Then she smiled.

"It went well."

Malik stood and hugged her. "Tell me everything."

She described the panel, the market-risk case, the question about adapting to a new city, and the program director's detailed explanation of summer placements. The opportunity had offices, mentors, transit routes, and a welcome week. London was no longer a word in an application. It had acquired a voice.

Malik congratulated her sincerely.

Then he said, "Part of me hoped the interview would make it less real."

Simone went still.

"Not that you would fail," he added. "I hoped you would dislike the program, or they would describe something you did not want. I wanted the problem to disappear without either of us choosing."

"Thank you for telling me," she said. "I do not like hearing it."

"I know."

She accepted one of the cold coffees. They sat in the lobby while students moved around them.

His honesty did not repair the conflict. It prevented false enthusiasm from becoming another one.

## Thursday, March 12: Not Yet Is Still an Answer

Malik and Simone met again before spring break.

They could break up now, protecting themselves from an offer that might never come. They could promise long distance now, protecting themselves from admitting they might choose differently later. Both decisions would convert uncertainty into action.

Neither felt honest.

"I do not want to end something meaningful because I am afraid of a possible email," Simone said.

"I do not want staying together this month to become an automatic promise about summer."

"Then it does not."

They agreed to continue the relationship without pretending it was uncomplicated. Simone would not minimize London to reassure him. Malik would not make every difficult feeling a forecast of their ending. If an offer arrived, they would decide from the life in front of them, not a hypothetical version.

Malik took her hand. "I still want this."

"So do I."

The words were true and not sufficient to solve everything.

Across campus, Nia, Keisha, and Julian finalized plans to remain for the first two days of spring break. The symposium presentation needed revisions, interviews needed editing, and the route animation still moved too quickly for viewers to read its uncertainty labels.

Malik saw their shared calendar notification.

He told himself it was project work.

That was true too.

Spring break opened with several honest answers and no final conclusions.
